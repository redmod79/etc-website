    Verses¶

    Question¶

    What does Scripture explicitly say about the nature and composition of human beings (biblical anthropology)? Focus on nephesh (soul), ruach (spirit), neshamah (breath), the creation of man, whether humans ARE souls or HAVE souls, and what happens at death.

    Nephesh chayyah applied to sea creatures before humans¶

    And God said, Let the waters bring forth abundantly the moving creature that hath life, and fowl [that] may fly above the earth in the open firmament of heaven. And God created great whales, and every living creature that moveth, which the waters brought forth abundantly, after their kind, and every winged fowl after his kind: and God saw that [it was] good.

    Context: Fifth day of creation. Animals receive the same designation (nephesh chayyah) that will later be applied to the man in Gen 2:7.

    Notes: The Hebrew 'nephesh chayyah' (living creature/soul) is first applied to animals, not humans. This establishes that nephesh is not an exclusively human property.

    Nephesh chayyah applied to land animals¶

    And God said, Let the earth bring forth the living creature after his kind, cattle, and creeping thing, and beast of the earth after his kind: and it was so. And God made the beast of the earth after his kind, and cattle after their kind, and every thing that creepeth upon the earth after his kind: and God saw that [it was] good.

    Context: Sixth day of creation, before man is created. Land animals are also nephesh chayyah.

    Notes: Same terminology (nephesh chayyah) used for land animals as will be used for man in Gen 2:7.

    Creation of man in the image of God — tselem and demuth¶

    And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth, and over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth. So God created man in his [own] image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them.

    Context: Sixth day of creation. God uses 1cp cohortative (na'aseh) — 'let us make.' Two terms: tselem (image) and demuth (likeness). Dominion mandate immediately follows.

    Notes: na'aseh (Qal.Impf.1p of asah) — 1cp cohortative. tselem (Noun.ms.Abs.+1up) in 'our image'; demuth (Noun.fs.Abs.+1up) 'after our likeness.' The image of God distinguishes humans from animals who are also nephesh chayyah. See grammar reference: cohortative-1cp-divine-speech.md.

    FOUNDATIONAL creation text — dust + neshamah = nephesh chayyah¶

    And the LORD God formed man [of] the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.

    Context: The detailed creation account. Three-part formula: (1) yatsar — God FORMED man from dust (aphar) of the ground (adamah), (2) naphach — God BREATHED (blew) into his nostrils the neshamah of chayyim, (3) wayehi — man BECAME a nephesh chayyah.

    Notes: wayehi (Verb.Qal.Wayq.3ms of hayah) — transformation verb 'became,' not 'received.' Man did not receive a soul; the entire entity IS the nephesh chayyah. The breath is neshamah (not ruach) — the specific, rarest term. nephesh (Noun.fs.Abs) + chayyah (Adj.fs.Abs) — same compound applied to animals in 1:20-24. See word studies: H5315-nephesh, H5397-nshamah, H2416-chay, H1961-hayah.

    Mot tamut — death penalty for disobedience¶

    But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

    Context: God's command to Adam in the garden, before the Fall. The mot tamut construction (infinitive absolute + imperfect) is the same used for judicial death penalties throughout the Pentateuch.

    Notes: mot (Verb.Qal.InfAbs of muwth) + tamut (Verb.Qal.Impf.2ms of muwth) — emphatic 'dying you shall die' / 'you shall surely die.' See grammar reference: infinitive-absolute-intensification.md. Same construction in Exo 21:12 for capital punishment.

    Tree of life in the garden — conditional immortality¶

    And out of the ground made the LORD God to grow every tree that is pleasant to the sight, and good for food; the tree of life also in the midst of the garden, and the tree of knowledge of good and evil.

    Context: The tree of life was accessible before the Fall, implying that continued life was conditional on access to this tree.

    Notes: The presence of the tree of life suggests that immortality was not inherent but dependent on the tree. This is confirmed in Gen 3:22-24 where access is barred.

    Animals formed from the same ground as man¶

    And out of the ground the LORD God formed every beast of the field, and every fowl of the air; and brought [them] unto Adam to see what he would call them: and whatsoever Adam called every living creature, that [was] the name thereof.

    Context: Same verb yatsar (to form) and same material (adamah/ground) used for animals as for man in Gen 2:7.

    Notes: Animals share the same material origin as humans. The distinguishing factor is not the substance but the image of God (Gen 1:26-27) and the direct divine breathing of neshamah (Gen 2:7).

    Dust returns to dust — reversal of creation¶

    In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread, till thou return unto the ground; for out of it wast thou taken: for dust thou [art], and unto dust shalt thou return.

    Context: God's judgment on Adam after the Fall. Death is described as a reversal of the creation process — returning to the ground/dust from which man was taken.

    Notes: The reversal language directly mirrors Gen 2:7. Man came from dust (aphar) and returns to dust. No mention of a surviving component.

    Tree of life barred — most direct statement humans are not inherently immortal¶

    And the LORD God said, Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil: and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever: Therefore the LORD God sent him forth from the garden of Eden, to till the ground from whence he was taken. So he drove out the man; and he placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life.

    Context: After the Fall. God explicitly bars access to the tree of life to PREVENT man from living forever — which would be unnecessary if man had an inherently immortal soul.

    Notes: 'Lest he... eat, and live for ever' — this is the most direct statement in Scripture that humans are not inherently immortal. The mechanism for eternal life (the tree) had to be removed to ensure death would result. Cherubim and flaming sword guard the way.

    Rachel's nephesh departing at death¶

    And it came to pass, as her soul was in departing, (for she died) that she called his name Benoni: but his father called him Benjamin.

    Context: Rachel's death during childbirth. Her nephesh 'goes out' (yatsa) at the moment of death.

    Notes: Nephesh departing = death occurring. The text does not describe where the nephesh goes or what state it enters. Compare with 1 Kgs 17:21-22 where Elijah prays for the nephesh to return.

    Nephesh meth — dead soul/body (nephesh used for corpse)¶

    Neither shall he go in to any dead body, nor defile himself for his father, or for his mother;

    Context: Priestly regulations. The Hebrew is nephesh meth — literally 'a dead nephesh/soul.' Nephesh here refers to a corpse.

    Notes: nephesh meth (construct chain) — 'dead soul.' This usage demolishes any claim that nephesh inherently means an immortal entity. A nephesh can be dead. See also Num 6:6.

    Nephesh meth — dead body/soul (Nazirite regulations)¶

    All the days that he separateth [himself] unto the LORD he shall come at no dead body.

    Context: Nazirite vow regulations. Same term nephesh meth as Lev 21:11.

    Notes: Confirms nephesh meth usage for a corpse/dead body. Nephesh can die and refer to dead entities.

    The soul (nephesh) that sins shall die¶

    Behold, all souls are mine; as the soul of the father, so also the soul of the son is mine: the soul that sinneth, it shall die.

    Context: God declares His ownership of all souls and the principle of individual accountability. The nephesh that sins — it (hi') shall die (tamut).

    Notes: hannephesh hachote't hi' tamut — 'the soul (nephesh, fs) the sinning-one (Qal.Ptcp.fs) she (PersPron.3fs) shall-die (Qal.Impf.3fs).' Death is predicated of the nephesh itself. If nephesh were inherently immortal, this statement would be self-contradictory.

    The soul (nephesh) that sins, it shall die — repeated¶

    The soul that sinneth, it shall die. The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son: the righteousness of the righteous shall be upon him, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon him.

    Context: Restatement of the principle from v.4 with expansion on individual accountability.

    Notes: Identical formula repeated for emphasis. Individual accountability and the mortality of the nephesh.

    Elijah's prayer for the nephesh to return — nephesh can depart and return¶

    And he stretched himself upon the child three times, and cried unto the LORD, and said, O LORD my God, I pray thee, let this child's soul come into him again. And the LORD heard the voice of Elijah; and the soul of the child came into him again, and he revived.

    Context: Elijah raises the widow's son from death. The nephesh 'comes back' (shub) into the child.

    Notes: The nephesh departed at death (as with Rachel in Gen 35:18) and returned when life was restored. This is consistent with nephesh as the living being — when the animating force departs, the nephesh ceases; when it returns, the nephesh revives.

    Ruach chayyim — breath/spirit of life in all creatures¶

    And, behold, I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth, to destroy all flesh, wherein [is] the breath of life, from under heaven; [and] every thing that [is] in the earth shall die.

    Context: God announces the flood. All flesh with ruach chayyim (spirit/breath of life) will die — humans and animals share this ruach.

    Notes: Ruach chayyim is shared by all flesh — not exclusively human. This parallels Ecc 3:19 where man and beast have 'one breath.'

    Neshamah-ruach-chayyim triple compound — all three terms together¶

    All in whose nostrils [was] the breath of life, of all that [was] in the dry [land], died.

    Context: The flood kills everything on dry land. The Hebrew contains the remarkable triple compound: nishmat-ruach-chayyim (breath of the spirit of life).

    Notes: nishmat (Noun.fs.Cst of neshamah) ruach (Noun.s.Abs) chayyim (Noun.mp.Abs) — all three anthropological terms in a single construct chain. This is the only verse that combines neshamah, ruach, and chayyim together. Applied to ALL creatures that died in the flood, not just humans.

    Ruach of God made me, neshamah of the Almighty gives life¶

    The Spirit of God hath made me, and the breath of the Almighty hath given me life.

    Context: Elihu's speech. He affirms that both ruach and neshamah are divine gifts that give life.

    Notes: Ruach and neshamah in synonymous parallelism. Both attributed to God as source. Human life is entirely dependent on the divine gift of breath/spirit.

    If God gathers ruach and neshamah, all flesh perishes¶

    If he set his heart upon man, [if] he gather unto himself his spirit and his breath; All flesh shall perish together, and man shall turn again unto dust.

    Context: Elihu continues. If God were to recall His ruach and neshamah from all living things, all flesh would die and return to dust.

    Notes: rucho (Noun.s.Abs.+3ms — his spirit/wind) and nishmato (Noun.fs.Abs.+3ms — his breath). If God gathers these back, 'all flesh shall perish together, and man shall turn again unto dust' — echoing Gen 3:19 and Ecc 12:7. Life is sustained only by continued divine bestowal.

    God takes away ruach — they die; sends forth ruach — they are created¶

    Thou hidest thy face, they are troubled: thou takest away their breath, they die, and return to their dust. Thou sendest forth thy spirit, they are created: and thou renewest the face of the earth.

    Context: A creation psalm celebrating God's sustaining providence. The pattern: God's ruach given = life; God's ruach withdrawn = death and return to dust.

    Notes: bidmut elohim — 'in the likeness of God.' The image persists through generations. Called their name 'Adam' (human) — both male and female.

    In the image of God made he man — image reaffirmed after the flood¶

    Whoso sheddeth man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed: for in the image of God made he man.

    Context: Noahic covenant. The image of God in man is the basis for the prohibition against murder — even after the Fall.

    Notes: betselem elohim asah et-ha'adam — 'in the image of God made he man.' The image of God persists after the Fall and after the Flood. It is the basis for human dignity and the capital prohibition.

    What is man? Made a little lower than the angels — human place in creation¶

    What is man, that thou art mindful of him? and the son of man, that thou visitest him? For thou hast made him a little lower than the angels, and hast crowned him with glory and honour. Thou madest him to have dominion over the works of thy hands; thou hast put all [things] under his feet:

    Context: David's reflection on human dignity despite human smallness. Man is 'a little lower than the angels' (or 'than God' — me'at me'elohim) with dominion over creation.

    Notes: me'at me'elohim — 'a little lower than elohim' (God or angels). This connects back to Gen 1:26-28 — the dominion mandate. Man is crowned with glory (kabod) and honor (hadar).

    Man is the image and glory of God — image language in NT¶

    For a man indeed ought not to cover [his] head, forasmuch as he is the image and glory of God: but the woman is the glory of the man.

    Context: Paul's discussion of head coverings. Man is called 'the image (eikon) and glory (doxa) of God.'

    Notes: eikon (G1504) kai doxa (G1391) theou — man IS the image of God (present tense). The image persists after the Fall, in the NT era.

    Men made after the similitude (homoiosis) of God¶

    Therewith bless we God, even the Father; and therewith curse we men, which are made after the similitude of God.

    Context: James on the tongue. People are made 'after the similitude (homoiosis) of God' — the reason cursing people is contradictory to blessing God.

    Notes: kath' homoiosin theou gegonotas — 'after the likeness of God having been made' (perfect passive participle). The image of God remains the basis for human dignity.

    Renewed in knowledge after the image of the creator¶

    And have put on the new [man], which is renewed in knowledge after the image of him that created him:

    Context: Paul on the new life in Christ. The 'new man' is being renewed according to the image (eikon) of the creator.

    Notes: kat' eikona tou ktisantos auton — 'according to the image of the one who created him.' The image of God is being restored in believers through renewal.

    God made man upright — original state of creation¶

    Lo, this only have I found, that God hath made man upright; but they have sought out many inventions.

    Context: The Preacher's finding about human nature. God made man yashar (upright/straight), but humanity went astray.

    Notes: asah ha'elohim et-ha'adam yashar — God made man upright. The original creation was good; the corruption came from human choices.

    Guard your heart — leb as the executive center¶

    Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it [are] the issues of life.

    Context: Wisdom instruction. The heart (leb) is the executive center from which the issues of life flow.

    Notes: mikol-mishmar netsor libbekha — 'above all guarding, guard your heart.' ki-mimmenu tots'ot chayyim — 'for from it are the outgoings of life.' The heart is the central anthropological term in Hebrew wisdom — the directing center of the whole person.

    Flesh and spirit contrast — moral/qualitative, not ontological¶

    Now the Egyptians [are] men, and not God; and their horses flesh, and not spirit. When the LORD shall stretch out his hand, both he that helpeth shall fall, and he that is holpen shall fall down, and they all shall fail together.

    Context: Isaiah's warning against trusting Egypt. The flesh/spirit contrast is about weakness vs. power, not two parts of a human being.

    Notes: adam welo' el — 'man and not God.' basar welo' ruach — 'flesh and not spirit.' This is the foundational OT flesh-spirit contrast, and it is clearly moral/qualitative (human weakness vs. divine power), NOT ontological (body vs. soul).

    Works of the flesh — include non-bodily sins¶

    Now the works of the flesh are manifest, which are [these]; Adultery, fornication, uncleanness, lasciviousness, Idolatry, witchcraft, hatred, variance, emulations, wrath, strife, seditions, heresies, Envyings, murders, drunkenness, revellings, and such like: of the which I tell you before, as I have also told [you] in time past, that they which do such things shall not inherit the kingdom of God.

    Context: Paul's flesh-spirit contrast in the context of Christian living. The 'works of the flesh' include hatred, jealousy, envy, seditions — clearly not bodily sins.

    Notes: ta erga tes sarkos — 'the works of the flesh.' The list includes idolatry, witchcraft, hatred, strife, heresies, envyings — these are not physical/bodily sins. This confirms that sarx in Paul's ethical usage means the sinful orientation, not the physical body.

    Carnally minded vs. spiritually minded — moral orientation, not body parts¶

    For they that are after the flesh do mind the things of the flesh; but they that are after the Spirit the things of the Spirit. For to be carnally minded [is] death; but to be spiritually minded [is] life and peace. Because the carnal mind [is] enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be. So then they that are in the flesh cannot please God.

    Context: Paul's discussion of life in the Spirit vs. life in the flesh. 'Carnally minded' (to phronema tes sarkos) is a mindset, not the physical body.

    Notes: to phronema tes sarkos — 'the mind-set of the flesh.' This is an orientation of the whole person toward self rather than God. The flesh-spirit contrast in Paul is moral/volitional, not ontological.

    Souls under the altar cry out — apocalyptic genre, personification¶

    And when he had opened the fifth seal, I saw under the altar the souls of them that were slain for the word of God, and for the testimony which they held: And they cried with a loud voice, saying, How long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge and avenge our blood on them that dwell on the earth? And white robes were given unto every one of them; and it was said unto them, that they should rest yet for a little season, until their fellowservants also and their brethren, that should be killed as they [were], should be fulfilled.

    Context: Fifth seal in John's apocalyptic vision. Souls (psychai) of martyrs are seen under the altar crying for justice. Apocalyptic genre.

    Notes: This is apocalyptic literature with extensive symbolism. Parallel to Gen 4:10 where Abel's blood 'cries from the ground' — personification of shed blood/life demanding justice. The souls are specifically 'under the altar' — the place where sacrificial blood was poured (Lev 4:7). This is the blood of the martyrs personified as crying for vindication.

    Every living soul (psyche) in the sea died — psyche applied to sea creatures¶

    And the second angel poured out his vial upon the sea; and it became as the blood of a dead [man]: and every living soul died in the sea.

    Context: The second bowl judgment. Psyche (soul) is applied to sea creatures — the same term used for human souls.

    Notes: pasa psyche zosa apethanen — 'every living soul died.' Psyche is applied to sea creatures, mirroring the OT usage of nephesh chayyah for animals (Gen 1:20-21). If psyche here means 'immortal soul,' then sea creatures have immortal souls too.

    Death and hades cast into the lake of fire — the second death¶

    And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death.

    Context: After the final judgment. Death (thanatos) and hades (G86) themselves are destroyed in the lake of fire. This IS the second death.

    Notes: thanatos kai hades — death and hades personified and destroyed. The second death (ho thanatos ho deuteros) uses the same word thanatos — death. The lake of fire is identified AS the second death, not as something distinct from death.

    Soul and spirit in poetic parallelism — synonymous, not distinct components¶

    And Mary said, My soul doth magnify the Lord, And my spirit hath rejoiced in God my Saviour.

    Context: Mary's Magnificat. Psyche and pneuma in synonymous parallelism.

    Notes: he psyche mou / to pneuma mou — 'my soul / my spirit.' These are in synonymous Hebrew-style parallelism (A says X, B says the same in different words). This argues against reading them as two ontologically distinct components.

    Soul and spirit in OT poetic parallelism¶

    With my soul have I desired thee in the night; yea, with my spirit within me will I seek thee early: for when thy judgments [are] in the earth, the inhabitants of the world will learn righteousness.

    Context: Isaiah's song. Nephesh and ruach in synonymous parallelism — desiring/seeking God.

    Notes: benaphshi — 'with my soul (nephesh)' / ruach beqirbi — 'with my spirit (ruach) within me.' Soul and spirit function as synonymous terms for the whole inner person longing for God.

    OT faithful died NOT having received the promises¶

    And these all, having obtained a good report through faith, received not the promise: God having provided some better thing for us, that they without us should not be made perfect.

    Context: The conclusion of the faith chapter. All the OT heroes of faith died without receiving what was promised — they are not yet 'made perfect.'

    Notes: ouk ekomisanto ten epangelian — 'did not receive the promise.' hina me choris hemon teleiothosin — 'that apart from us they should not be made perfect.' The OT faithful are waiting — they have NOT yet received their reward. This contradicts the idea of immediate heavenly reward at death.

    Heart, soul, might — the Shema, not three ontological parts¶

    And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might.

    Context: The Shema — the foundational command of Israel. Three terms (leb, nephesh, me'od) describe the totality of devotion, not three separate parts of a person.

    Notes: leb (heart) + nephesh (soul/being) + me'od (might/muchness). No one reads this as three ontological components. It means 'with your whole self.' This is the background for 1 Thess 5:23.

    Through one man sin entered, and death through sin — origin of death¶

    Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned:

    Context: Paul's Adam-Christ typology. Death entered through sin — it was not part of the original creation.

    Notes: di' henos anthropou he hamartia eis ton kosmon eiselthen, kai dia tes hamartias ho thanatos — 'through one man sin entered the world, and through sin death.' Death is a consequence of sin, not an inherent feature of human existence.

    Wages of sin is death, gift of God is eternal life — death-life binary¶

    For the wages of sin [is] death; but the gift of God [is] eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.

    Context: Paul's summary of the sin-death and grace-life contrast.

    Notes: ta opsonia tes hamartias thanatos — 'the wages of sin is death.' to charisma tou theou zoe aionios — 'the gift of God is eternal life.' Death and eternal life are presented as an exhaustive binary. Death is the consequence; eternal life is the gift. No third option (eternal conscious torment) appears in this formulation.

    Mortal man compared to houses of clay — human insignificance and mortality¶

    Shall mortal man be more just than God? shall a man be more pure than his maker? Behold, he put no trust in his servants; and his angels he charged with folly: How much less [in] them that dwell in houses of clay, whose foundation [is] in the dust, [which] are crushed before the moth?

    Context: Eliphaz's first speech. Humans are described as dwelling in 'houses of clay' with their 'foundation in the dust' — fragile, mortal beings.

    Notes: enosh (mortal man) — distinct from adam (man generically). 'Houses of clay' (batey-chomer) — the body as a clay house with foundation in dust (aphar). Crushed before a moth — extreme fragility. Affirms human mortality and material composition.

    Into thy hand I commit my ruach — spirit entrusted to God¶

    Into thine hand I commit my spirit: thou hast redeemed me, O LORD God of truth.

    Context: David's prayer of trust. He commits his ruach (spirit) into God's hand. Quoted by Jesus on the cross (Luke 23:46).

    Notes: beyad'kha afqid ruchi — 'into your hand I deposit my spirit.' The same words Jesus spoke on the cross (Luke 23:46, pneuma). Committing the spirit to God at death is consistent with Ecc 12:7 — the spirit returns to God who gave it.

    Ruach departs, returns to earth, thoughts perish — death sequence¶

    His breath goeth forth, he returneth to his earth; in that very day his thoughts perish.

    Context: Warning against trusting in mortal humans. When the ruach departs, the person returns to the ground and thoughts cease that very day.

    Notes: tetse' rucho (his spirit goes out) yashub le'admato (he returns to his ground) bayom hahu abdu eshtonotav (in that very day his thoughts perish). Three events at death: (1) spirit departs, (2) body returns to ground, (3) thoughts perish. Consciousness ceases at death — 'in that very day.'

    Thy dead shall live — resurrection hope in OT¶

    Thy dead [men] shall live, [together with] my dead body shall they arise. Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust: for thy dew [is as] the dew of herbs, and the earth shall cast out the dead.

    Context: Isaiah's apocalypse (chapters 24-27). A promise of resurrection: the dead shall live, those dwelling in dust shall awake.

    Notes: yichyu metekha — 'your dead shall live.' haqitsu weranenu shokne aphar — 'awake and sing, dwellers of dust.' The 'dust' language connects to Gen 2:7 and 3:19. Resurrection is from the dust, not from a conscious intermediate state.

    Today you will be with me in paradise — debated intermediate-state text¶

    And Jesus said unto him, Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise.

    Context: Jesus on the cross, speaking to the penitent thief. Commonly cited for conscious intermediate state.

    Notes: semeron met' emou ese en to paradeiso — 'today with me you will be in paradise.' Key issue: Jesus was NOT in paradise that day — John 20:17 has Jesus saying on Sunday morning 'I am not yet ascended to my Father.' The comma placement in the KJV ('I say unto thee, To day') is an editorial choice. Greek has no punctuation. The statement may be 'I say unto thee today, thou shalt be with me in paradise' — emphasizing the certainty of the promise, not the timing of its fulfillment.

    Lazarus sleeps — Jesus calls death sleep¶

    These things said he: and after that he saith unto them, Our friend Lazarus sleepeth; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep. Then said his disciples, Lord, if he sleep, he shall do well. Howbeit Jesus spake of his death: but they thought that he had spoken of taking of rest in sleep. Then said Jesus unto them plainly, Lazarus is dead.

    Context: Jesus speaking about Lazarus's death. He calls death 'sleep' (koimao), then clarifies 'plainly' that Lazarus is dead.

    Notes: Lazaros ho philos hemon kekoimetai — 'Lazarus our friend has fallen asleep.' Jesus uses koimao (to sleep) for death, then clarifies with apethanen (he died). This is significant because Jesus knew exactly what death was and chose to call it sleep.

    God gives to all life, breath (pnoe), and all things — divine sustaining¶

    Neither is worshipped with men's hands, as though he needed any thing, seeing he giveth to all life, and breath, and all things;

    Context: Paul's Areopagus speech in Athens. God is the source and sustainer of all life and breath (pnoe).

    Notes: didous pasi zoen kai pnoen kai ta panta — 'giving to all life and breath and all things.' Pnoe (G4157) — breath, the NT equivalent of neshamah via LXX. God is the ongoing source of life and breath for all creatures. Life is not self-sustaining but continually dependent on God.

    By man came death, by man came resurrection — Adam-Christ typology¶

    For since by man [came] death, by man [came] also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive.

    Context: Paul's Adam-Christ typology in the resurrection chapter. Death came through Adam; resurrection comes through Christ.

    Notes: di' anthropou thanatos / di' anthropou anastasis nekron — 'through man death / through man resurrection of the dead.' en to Adam pantes apothneskousin / en to Christo pantes zoopoiethesontai — 'in Adam all die / in Christ all shall be made alive.' Death is universal through Adam; life is through Christ. The answer to death is resurrection, not an immortal soul.

    Made a little lower than the angels — NT citation of Psa 8¶

    Thou madest him a little lower than the angels; thou crownedst him with glory and honour, and didst set him over the works of thy hands: Thou hast put all things in subjection under his feet. For in that he put all in subjection under him, he left nothing [that is] not put under him. But now we see not yet all things put under him.

    Context: The author of Hebrews quoting Psalm 8:4-6. Applied to both humanity's original dignity and Christ's incarnation.

    Notes: elatosas auton brachy ti par' angelous — 'you made him a little lower than the angels.' The LXX translates elohim as angelous (angels). The passage establishes humanity's place: lower than angels but crowned with glory, given dominion. 'But now we see not yet all things put under him' — the present reality is not yet the eschatological fulfillment.

    LORD forms the spirit (ruach) of man within him — spirit as divine gift¶

    The burden of the word of the LORD for Israel, saith the LORD, which stretcheth forth the heavens, and layeth the foundation of the earth, and formeth the spirit of man within him.

    Context: Zechariah's oracle. God is described as the one who forms (yotser) the ruach of man within him — the spirit is a divine creation.

    Notes: weyotser ruach-adam beqirbo — 'and forming the spirit of man within him.' The verb yotser (Qal.Ptcp of yatsar — the same verb as Gen 2:7 for 'formed') presents the spirit as something God actively creates within each person. The spirit is not self-generated but divinely bestowed.

    Spirit (neshamah) of man is the lamp of the LORD — neshamah as divine connection¶

    The spirit of man [is] the candle of the LORD, searching all the inward parts of the belly.

    Context: A wisdom proverb. The neshamah of man serves as God's lamp — a means by which God searches the inner person.

    Notes: ner YHWH nishmat adam — 'the lamp of the LORD is the neshamah of man.' chofes kol-chadrey-baten — 'searching all the chambers of the belly/inner parts.' The neshamah here functions as God's instrument of searching within the person — connecting the divine breath of Gen 2:7 to ongoing divine access to human consciousness.

    God gives neshamah to the people and ruach to those who walk on earth¶

    Thus saith God the LORD, he that created the heavens, and stretched them out; he that spread forth the earth, and that which cometh out of it; he that giveth breath unto the people upon it, and spirit to them that walk therein:

    Context: God introduces His servant. Described as creator of heavens and earth, and giver of neshamah and ruach to all people.

    Notes: noten neshamah la'am aleha weruach laholkhim bah — 'giving breath (neshamah) to the people upon it and spirit (ruach) to those walking in it.' Both neshamah and ruach in one verse, both presented as divine gifts to humanity. Parallels Job 33:4 and Gen 2:7.

    First Adam living soul (psyche), last Adam quickening spirit (pneuma)¶

    And so it is written, The first man Adam was made a living soul; the last Adam [was made] a quickening spirit.

    Context: Paul quotes Gen 2:7 (LXX) and draws the Adam-Christ contrast. The first Adam became a living psyche; the last Adam became a life-giving pneuma.

    Notes: egeneto ho protos anthropos Adam eis psychen zosan — 'the first man Adam became a living soul' (quoting Gen 2:7 LXX). ho eschatos Adam eis pneuma zoopoioun — 'the last Adam a life-giving spirit.' The verb egeneto (became, from ginomai G1096) matches wayehi in Gen 2:7. Adam became a living psyche — he did not receive one. Christ, as the last Adam, is the source of life (zoopoioun, from zoopoieo G2227).

    There is a spirit (ruach) in man, the neshamah of the Almighty gives understanding¶

    But [there is] a spirit in man: and the inspiration of the Almighty giveth them understanding.

    Context: Elihu's speech. He attributes understanding not to age but to the ruach and neshamah from God.

    Notes: aken ruach-hi' be'enosh — 'but it is a spirit in man.' wenishmat shaddai tebinem — 'and the breath/inspiration of the Almighty gives them understanding.' Both ruach and neshamah appear, with neshamah specifically as the source of understanding/wisdom. This is the illumination function of neshamah (see H5397 word study, semantic category 4).

    Right to the tree of life — eschatological access restored¶

    Blessed [are] they that do his commandments, that they may have right to the tree of life, and may enter in through the gates into the city.

    Context: Near the end of Revelation. Access to the tree of life is restored for the redeemed — bookending Gen 3:22-24 where access was barred.

    Notes: hina estai he exousia auton epi to xylon tes zoes — 'that their authority/right may be upon the tree of life.' The tree of life reappears (cf. Gen 2:9, 3:22-24). What was barred after the Fall is restored in the new creation. Immortality through the tree of life — conditional on obedience and relationship, not inherent.
