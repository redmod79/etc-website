    CONCLUSION — What Is Man? Biblical Anthropology¶

    Question¶

    What does Scripture explicitly say about the nature and composition of human beings (biblical anthropology)? Focus on nephesh (soul), ruach (spirit), neshamah (breath), the creation of man, whether humans ARE souls or HAVE souls, and what happens at death.

    Summary Answer¶

    Genesis 2:7 provides the foundational formula for human composition: God formed man from dust, breathed the neshamah of life into his nostrils, and man BECAME a living nephesh. The verb wayehi (Qal.Wayq.3ms of hayah, H1961) is a transformation verb — man did not receive a soul; the composite entity of dust animated by divine breath IS the living soul. The same compound term nephesh chayyah (H5315 + H2416) is applied to sea creatures (Gen 1:20-21) and land animals (Gen 1:24-25) before it is applied to humans. What distinguishes humans from animals is not soul or spirit — which animals share — but the image of God (tselem, H6754, and demuth, H1823; Gen 1:26-27).

    Death reverses this creation formula. Dust returns to earth (Gen 3:19; Ecc 12:7), the spirit returns to God who gave it (Ecc 12:7), and thoughts perish that very day (Psa 146:4). Multiple authors across Psalms, Ecclesiastes, and Job describe the dead as unconscious — knowing nothing (Ecc 9:5), having no work or wisdom in sheol (Ecc 9:10), unable to praise God (Psa 115:17), silent (Psa 6:5). The biblical hope for the dead is resurrection (Dan 12:2; 1 Thess 4:16; 1 Cor 15:42-54), not disembodied survival. Immortality belongs to God alone (1 Tim 6:16) and is a future gift that mortals "put on" at the resurrection (1 Cor 15:53-54), not a present human possession.

    Key Verses¶

    The Creation Formula (Genesis 2:7) "And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul."

    The Soul That Sins Dies (Ezekiel 18:4) "Behold, all souls are mine; as the soul of the father, so also the soul of the son is mine: the soul that sinneth, it shall die."

    Man and Beast Share One Breath (Ecclesiastes 3:19) "For that which befalleth the sons of men befalleth beasts; even one thing befalleth them: as the one dieth, so dieth the other; yea, they have all one breath; so that a man hath no preeminence above a beast: for all is vanity."

    The Dead Know Nothing (Ecclesiastes 9:5-6) "For the living know that they shall die: but the dead know not any thing, neither have they any more a reward; for the memory of them is forgotten. Also their love, and their hatred, and their envy, is now perished."

    Reversal of Creation at Death (Ecclesiastes 12:7) "Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it."

    Thoughts Perish at Death (Psalm 146:4) "His breath goeth forth, he returneth to his earth; in that very day his thoughts perish."

    God Alone Has Immortality (1 Timothy 6:16) "Who only hath immortality, dwelling in the light which no man can approach unto; whom no man hath seen, nor can see: to whom be honour and power everlasting. Amen."

    Mortals Must Put On Immortality (1 Corinthians 15:53-54) "For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality. So when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality, then shall be brought to pass the saying that is written, Death is swallowed up in victory."

    Tree of Life Barred (Genesis 3:22-24) "And the LORD God said, Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil: and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever: Therefore the LORD God sent him forth from the garden of Eden."

    God Can Destroy Soul and Body (Matthew 10:28) "And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell."

    Analysis¶

    The Creation Formula: What Humans Are Made Of¶

    The starting point for biblical anthropology is Genesis 2:7. The text describes a three-step process: (1) God formed (yatsar, H3335) man from dust (aphar, H6083) of the ground (adamah, H127); (2) God breathed (naphach, H5301) into his nostrils the neshamah (H5397) of chayyim (H2416); (3) man became (wayehi, Qal.Wayq.3ms of hayah, H1961) a nephesh chayyah (H5315 + H2416). The Hebrew parsing is critical. The verb wayehi is a transformation verb meaning "became." Man did not receive a soul as an additional component; the combined entity of dust animated by divine breath IS the living soul. The word study for hayah (H1961) documents 4,280 occurrences, with the "becoming/coming into being" sense well-attested.

    The word used for what God breathed into man is neshamah (H5397), not ruach (H7307). The word study for neshamah documents only 25 OT occurrences — making it the rarest of the three key anthropological terms. The LXX translates neshamah as pnoe (G4157, physical breath) rather than pneuma (spirit), preserving its concrete breath-of-life nuance. This matters because neshamah emphasizes the physical, God-given nature of the life-breath rather than an immaterial spirit-substance.

    Paul directly quotes Genesis 2:7 in 1 Corinthians 15:45 (via the LXX): "The first man Adam was made (egeneto, from ginomai, G1096) a living soul (psychen zosan, G5590)." The verb egeneto preserves the "became" from the Hebrew wayehi. Even in the NT, the holistic reading is maintained — Adam became a living psyche.

    Animals Share the Same Terminology¶

    The compound nephesh chayyah appears for sea creatures (Gen 1:20-21) and land animals (Gen 1:24-25) before it appears for humans (Gen 2:7). Animals are also formed from the ground (Gen 2:19) using the same verb yatsar. The ruach chayyim (spirit/breath of life) belongs to all flesh — humans and animals alike (Gen 6:17). Genesis 7:22 contains the remarkable triple compound nishmat-ruach-chayyim (breath of spirit of life) applied to ALL creatures that died in the flood, not just humans. Ecclesiastes 3:19 states directly: man and beast have "one breath" (ruach echad) and the same fate — both come from dust and return to dust.

    This shared vocabulary means that nephesh, ruach, and neshamah cannot be what distinguishes humans from animals. Whatever makes humans unique must be found elsewhere.

    The Image of God: What Makes Humans Unique¶

    Genesis 1:26-27 supplies the distinguishing feature: man is created in the tselem (H6754, image) and demuth (H1823, likeness) of God. The word study for tselem documents 18 occurrences across four semantic categories: humanity in God's image, cult-images, crafted figures, and fleeting appearance. The image is connected to the dominion mandate (Gen 1:28; Psa 8:5-6) and persists after the Fall (Gen 9:6 — the basis for the prohibition against murder), through the OT, and into the NT era. Paul says man IS the image (eikon, G1504) and glory of God (1 Cor 11:7, present tense). James says men are made after the homoiosis of God (Jas 3:9). Colossians 3:10 speaks of believers being renewed after the image of the creator.

    Psalm 8:4-6 places humanity "a little lower than the angels" (me'at me'elohim), crowned with glory and honor, with dominion over creation. Hebrews 2:7-8 quotes this passage, applying it to both humanity's original dignity and Christ's incarnation.

    The Semantic Range of Nephesh and Psyche¶

    The word study for nephesh (H5315) documents 788 occurrences across 31 books with 214 unique KJV translations. This extraordinary breadth includes: the whole person (Gen 2:7), life at risk (Job 2:4), desire/appetite (Deu 14:26), reflexive pronoun "self" (Amos 6:8), and corpses (Lev 21:11; Num 6:6, where nephesh meth literally means "dead soul"). The nephesh can die (Ezek 18:4, 20 — hannephesh hachote't hi' tamut, "the sinning nephesh, she shall die"), depart at death (Gen 35:18), and be destroyed by God (Matt 10:28 via psyche). This range prevents equating nephesh with the Greek philosophical concept of an "immortal soul."

    Psyche (G5590) is the NT equivalent, with 104 occurrences. The LXX maps nephesh to psyche 599 times (87%). Psyche is applied to sea creatures (Rev 16:3 — pasa psyche zosa apethanen, "every living soul died"), confirming continuity with the OT usage. In Matt 10:28, God can destroy (apollymi, G622) both psyche and soma in gehenna — the psyche is destructible.

    Ruach, Pneuma, and the Animating Principle¶

    Ruach (H7307) has 434 OT occurrences across three major categories: spirit (~50%), wind (~15%), breath (~3%). In anthropological contexts, ruach is the life-force that God gives and withdraws. When God takes away ruach, creatures die and return to dust (Psa 104:29-30). If God were to gather his ruach and neshamah, all flesh would perish (Job 34:14-15). At death, the ruach returns to God who gave it (Ecc 12:7). When ruach departs, thoughts perish that very day (Psa 146:4).

    A critical observation: ruach is NEVER used for corpses. Unlike nephesh, which can designate dead bodies (nephesh meth), ruach is always associated with the animating principle. This distinction suggests that ruach is the life-force while nephesh is the whole being that results from the combination of body and life-force.

    Pneuma (G4151) is the NT equivalent, with 385 occurrences. The body without pneuma is dead (Jas 2:26). This is consistent with the OT pattern: departure of the spirit = death. The LXX maps ruach to pneuma 235 times (~90%).

    Neshamah: The Rarest and Most Specific Term¶

    The word study for neshamah (H5397) documents 25 occurrences across five semantic categories: breath of life at creation (Gen 2:7), divine blast in judgment (Job 4:9), total annihilation formula (Deu 20:16), human inner life/illumination (Pro 20:27, Job 32:8), and every living being (Psa 150:6). Job dominates with 7 of 24 unique verses.

    Key passages for neshamah: Genesis 2:7 (the specific word for what God breathed into man), Genesis 7:22 (the triple compound neshamah-ruach-chayyim applied to all creatures), Job 32:8 (neshamah of the Almighty gives understanding), Job 33:4 (neshamah of the Almighty gives life), Proverbs 20:27 (neshamah of man is the lamp of the LORD, by which God searches the inner person), and Isaiah 42:5 (God gives neshamah to the people and ruach to those who walk on earth).

    The neshamah-ruach relationship is clarified in passages that use both together. Job 34:14-15 parallels ruach and neshamah — if God gathers both, all flesh perishes. Isaiah 42:5 presents both as divine gifts. Genesis 7:22 links all three (neshamah, ruach, chayyim) in a single construct chain. The two terms overlap considerably in meaning, with neshamah emphasizing the physical breath and divine origin of the life-force.

    The Heart (Leb/Kardia) as the Biblical Center of the Person¶

    The word study for leb (H3820) documents 654 occurrences across 33 books — making it the most frequent anthropological term in Hebrew. What modern Western anthropology assigns to "soul" or "mind," Hebrew assigns to "heart." The heart encompasses thought (1 Kgs 3:9 — Solomon asks for an understanding heart), will (Gen 8:21), emotion (Gen 6:6 — it grieved God at his heart), moral condition (Gen 6:5 — imagination of the heart was evil), and devotion (Deu 6:5 — love the LORD with all your heart).

    Proverbs 4:23 identifies the heart as the executive center: "Guard your heart with all diligence, for out of it are the issues of life." Jeremiah 17:9-10 identifies it as the site of moral condition that only God can fully know. Ezekiel 36:26 promises renewal through "a new heart" and "a new spirit" — heart and spirit in parallelism.

    Kardia (G2588) continues this in the NT with 160 occurrences. The heart is where faith dwells (Rom 10:10), where God writes his law (Heb 8:10), and what God searches (Heb 4:12). The "inner man" language (Rom 7:22; Eph 3:16) corresponds to the OT "heart" rather than to a separable soul.

    The Bipartite/Tripartite Question: 1 Thessalonians 5:23 and Hebrews 4:12¶

    1 Thessalonians 5:23 lists pneuma (G4151), psyche (G5590), and soma (G4983) connected by kai...kai...kai. The Greek parsing shows: holokeron (G3648, "whole/complete") hymon to pneuma kai he psyche kai to soma. The emphasis is on WHOLENESS — holoteles (G3651, "completely") and holokeron ("entire/complete"). Paul's prayer is for the WHOLE person to be preserved blameless, not for three separate parts to be individually sanctified.

    The parallel with the Shema (Deu 6:5 — heart, soul, might) is instructive. No one reads Deuteronomy's three terms as three ontological components. Mark 12:30 uses four terms (heart, soul, mind, strength). The number varies because the point is totality of devotion, not precise enumeration of parts.

    Hebrews 4:12 describes the word of God "piercing even to the dividing asunder of soul (psyche) and spirit (pneuma), and of the joints (harmon, G719) and marrow (myelon, G3452)." The parallel structure is soul/spirit :: joints/marrow. Joints and marrow are not two separate body parts that can be physically separated — they are intimately connected tissues. The point is God's penetrating discernment into the deepest recesses of human existence, not a lesson in human anatomy.

    Isaiah 26:9 and Luke 1:46-47 use soul and spirit in synonymous parallelism: "With my soul (nephesh) have I desired thee... with my spirit (ruach) within me will I seek thee" (Isa 26:9). "My soul (psyche) doth magnify the Lord, and my spirit (pneuma) hath rejoiced in God my Saviour" (Luke 1:46-47). These passages treat soul and spirit as interchangeable terms for the whole inner person, not as two distinct substances.

    What Happens at Death: The Reversal of Creation¶

    Death reverses Genesis 2:7. Ecclesiastes 12:7 states the reversal with linguistic precision: dust returns (yashub) to the earth "as it was" (keshehayyah); the spirit (ruach) returns (tashub) to God "who gave it" (asher netanah). The Hebrew parsing shows both verbs are forms of shub (to return) — dust returns to its source, breath returns to its source.

    The ruach returning to God is not a conscious entity going to heaven. Three lines of evidence establish this. First, Psalm 146:4: when the ruach departs, "in that very day his thoughts perish" (abdu eshtonotav). Consciousness ceases at the moment the spirit leaves. Second, Ecclesiastes 9:5: "the dead know not any thing." Third, the same ruach returns to God whether the person is righteous or wicked (Ecc 12:7 is universal), and whether the creature is human or animal (Ecc 3:19 — one ruach for all). The spirit returning to God is the life-force returning to its Giver, not a personality traveling to a destination.

    The OT consistently describes the dead as unconscious. No remembrance of God in death (Psa 6:5). The dead praise not the LORD (Psa 115:17). No work, device, knowledge, or wisdom in sheol (Ecc 9:10). The dead's love, hatred, and envy have perished (Ecc 9:6). The grave is "the land of forgetfulness" (Psa 88:12). Dead men's sons come to honor and the dead man knows it not (Job 14:21).

    Death is called "sleep" by at least seven biblical authors: Moses (Deu 31:16), Job (14:12), Jeremiah (51:39), Daniel (12:2), Jesus (John 11:11-14 — "Our friend Lazarus sleepeth... Lazarus is dead"), Stephen via Luke (Acts 7:60), and Paul (1 Cor 15:6, 18, 51; 1 Thess 4:14). Jesus, who knew exactly what death was, chose to call it sleep.

    Who Has Immortality?¶

    The immortality vocabulary settles the question of inherent human immortality. Athanasia (G110) occurs only 3 times in the NT. In 1 Timothy 6:16, God is described as ho monos echon athanasian — "the only one having immortality." The monos ("alone/only") is an unambiguous exclusion. If God ALONE has immortality, no created being possesses it inherently. In 1 Corinthians 15:53-54, mortals "put on" (endysasthai, G1746, aorist middle infinitive) immortality at the resurrection. The clothing metaphor indicates transformation — acquiring something not previously possessed.

    Aphtharsia (G861) occurs 8 times and is presented as something to seek (Rom 2:7 — seeking immortality would be nonsensical if humans already had it), something brought to light through the gospel (2 Tim 1:10), and something to put on at the resurrection (1 Cor 15:42, 50, 53, 54). Thnetos (G2349, "mortal") is applied to human bodies 6 times — confirming mortality as the present human condition. Aphthartos (G862, "incorruptible") is applied to God (Rom 1:23; 1 Tim 1:17) and contrasted with phthartos ("corruptible") applied to man (Rom 1:23).

    Genesis 3:22-24 provides the narrative confirmation: God barred access to the tree of life "lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever." If man already had an immortal soul, this action would be pointless. The tree of life was the mechanism for immortality, and barring access ensures death. Revelation 22:14 restores access to the tree of life eschatologically — the conditional immortality framework spans from Genesis to Revelation.

    Eternal life is consistently presented as: a gift (Rom 6:23 — "the gift of God is eternal life"), a promise (Tit 1:2; 1 John 2:25), something conditional on Christ (1 John 5:11-12 — "He that hath the Son hath life; and he that hath not the Son of God hath not life"), and something to seek (Rom 2:7).

    The Flesh-Spirit Contrast: Moral, Not Ontological¶

    The foundational OT flesh-spirit contrast appears in Isaiah 31:3: "The Egyptians are men, and not God; and their horses flesh, and not spirit." This is about human weakness versus divine power — a qualitative/moral distinction, not two parts of the human being.

    Paul's usage confirms this. The "works of the flesh" in Galatians 5:19-21 include "hatred, variance, emulations, wrath, strife, seditions, heresies, envyings" — clearly not physical/bodily sins. "To be carnally minded" (to phronema tes sarkos) in Romans 8:5-8 is a mindset, an orientation of the whole person toward self rather than God. Being "in the flesh" (Rom 8:8-9) means being unregenerate, not being in a physical body.

    The Resurrection as the Biblical Hope¶

    The biblical answer to death is resurrection, not disembodied survival. Paul's comfort for grieving believers in 1 Thessalonians 4:13-16 is specifically the resurrection: "the dead in Christ shall rise first." He does not say "they are already with Christ in heaven." Peter states that David — a man after God's own heart — "is not ascended into the heavens" even centuries after his death (Acts 2:34). David is still in his tomb (Acts 2:29). The author of Hebrews states that all the OT heroes of faith "received not the promise" and "without us should not be made perfect" (Heb 11:39-40). They are waiting.

    Daniel 12:2 describes the dead as "sleepers of the ground of dust" who will "awake" — resurrection from the dust. Isaiah 26:19 uses the same language: "Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust." The dust language connects directly to Genesis 2:7 and 3:19. Ezekiel 37:1-14 presents resurrection as re-creation: physical bodies formed (bones, sinews, flesh, skin) + ruach breathed in = living beings. The vision explicitly recapitulates the creation formula.

    1 Corinthians 15:42-54 presents resurrection as transformation: sown in corruption, raised in incorruption; sown in dishonour, raised in glory; sown in weakness, raised in power; sown a natural body, raised a spiritual body. The present mortal condition must be transformed, not escaped. Death and hades are themselves destroyed (Rev 20:14; 1 Cor 15:26) — they are temporary, not eternal.

    Difficult Passages: Genre, Grammar, and Context¶

    Several passages are cited for a conscious intermediate state. Each requires careful analysis.

    2 Corinthians 5:1-8 — Paul says he would rather be "absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord" (v.8). However, v.4 is determinative: Paul explicitly states he does NOT want to be "unclothed" (ekdysasthai — disembodied) but "clothed upon" (ependysasthai — the resurrection body), "that mortality might be swallowed up of life." Paul's horizon is the resurrection, not disembodiment. From the dead person's perspective — with no consciousness in between (Ecc 9:5; Psa 146:4) — the next conscious moment after death IS being with Christ at the resurrection.

    Philippians 1:23 — Paul desires "to depart, and to be with Christ." The same letter has Paul hoping for the resurrection (3:11) and expecting Christ to "change our vile body" at his return (3:20-21). Paul treats departing and being with Christ as a single expectation. The interval between is no more experienced than the passage of time during sleep.

    Luke 23:43 — Jesus tells the thief, "Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise." Greek has no punctuation. The comma placement is editorial. Jesus was NOT in paradise that day: on Sunday morning (John 20:17) he says "I am not yet ascended to my Father." The statement reads consistently as "I say unto thee today, thou shalt be with me in paradise" — the emphasis falls on the certainty of the promise, not the timing.

    Revelation 6:9-11 — Souls of martyrs under the altar cry "How long?" This is apocalyptic literature. The parallel with Genesis 4:10 (Abel's blood "cries from the ground") establishes the pattern: shed blood/life personified as demanding justice. The souls are "under the altar" — the place where sacrificial blood was poured (Lev 4:7). The same group is told to "rest" and later participates in the "first resurrection" (Rev 20:4-5), confirming they were not yet alive.

    Matthew 10:28 — Man can kill the body "but are not able to kill the soul." This does NOT teach soul immortality. The verse's conclusion is that God can DESTROY (apollymi, G622) both psyche and soma in gehenna. The psyche is destructible by God. The distinction is about the scope of divine versus human power, not about an immortal component.

    Word Studies¶

    Nephesh (H5315): 788 occurrences, 214 translations. Man BECAME a nephesh (Gen 2:7). Nephesh can refer to corpses (nephesh meth), can die (Ezek 18:4), and is shared with animals. The breadth prevents any equation with "immortal soul."

    Ruach (H7307): 434 occurrences. The animating principle shared by all creatures. Returns to God at death (Ecc 12:7). Never used for corpses. Departure = death; thoughts perish at departure.

    Neshamah (H5397): 25 occurrences. The specific term for what God breathed into man (Gen 2:7). Rarest of the three key terms. LXX maps to pnoe (physical breath), not pneuma (spirit).

    Athanasia (G110): 3 occurrences. God alone has it (1 Tim 6:16). Mortals put it on at resurrection (1 Cor 15:53-54). No present human possession.

    Leb (H3820): 654 occurrences. The most frequent OT anthropological term. The heart as the executive center of thought, will, emotion, and moral condition.

    Difficult Passages¶

    2 Corinthians 5:1-8: Paul's hope is the resurrection body (v.4 — "clothed upon"), not disembodiment. "Absent from body, present with Lord" is resurrection language read from the perspective of one who experiences no conscious interval.

    Philippians 1:23: Same letter has Paul hoping for resurrection (3:11, 20-21). Departing and being with Christ are a single expectation with no conscious gap between.

    Luke 23:43: Jesus was not in paradise that day (John 20:17). Greek has no commas. The emphasis is on the certainty of the promise.

    Revelation 6:9-11: Apocalyptic genre. Personification of shed blood demanding justice (parallel: Gen 4:10). Same group participates in the "first resurrection" (Rev 20:4-5).

    1 Thessalonians 5:23: Three terms describe totality (compare Deu 6:5 — three terms; Mark 12:30 — four terms). The emphasis is on wholeness (holoteles, holokeron), not enumeration of ontological parts.

    Matthew 10:28: God can DESTROY both soul and body. The psyche is destructible. This teaches God's power over the whole person, not soul immortality.

    I-B Resolution: I4 — Conscious intermediate state from 2 Cor 5:8 / Phil 1:23¶

    Step 1 — Tension: - FOR (conscious intermediate state): E61 (Paul willing to be absent from body and present with the Lord, 2 Cor 5:8), E62 (Paul desires to depart and be with Christ, Phil 1:23) - AGAINST (unconscious death until resurrection): E12 (thoughts perish at death, Psa 146:4), E13 (no remembrance in death, Psa 6:5), E14 (dead praise not the LORD, Psa 115:17), E15 (the dead know nothing, Ecc 9:5), E16 (no knowledge/wisdom in sheol, Ecc 9:10), E40 (David not ascended into heaven, Acts 2:34), E41 (OT faithful not yet received the promise, Heb 11:39-40), E39 (dead in Christ rise at Christ's coming, 1 Thess 4:16)

    Step 2 — Clarity Assessment:

    Item Level Rationale
    E12 (Psa 146:4) Plain Directly states thoughts perish the day spirit departs. Death vocabulary in a psalm about mortal humans.
    E13 (Psa 6:5) Plain Directly states no remembrance in death.
    E14 (Psa 115:17) Plain Directly states the dead do not praise.
    E15 (Ecc 9:5) Plain Directly states the dead know nothing.
    E16 (Ecc 9:10) Plain Directly states no work/knowledge/wisdom in sheol.
    E40 (Acts 2:34) Plain Peter directly denies David has ascended to heaven.
    E41 (Heb 11:39-40) Contextually Clear States the OT faithful "received not the promise"; requires understanding "made perfect" = receiving the reward.
    E39 (1 Thess 4:16) Plain Paul directly states the dead in Christ rise at the coming.
    E61 (2 Cor 5:4,8) Contextually Clear Paul expresses preference but also states he does NOT want disembodiment; requires understanding his resurrection horizon.
    E62 (Phil 1:23) Ambiguous Paul expresses desire to depart and be with Christ but does not specify mechanism or timing; same letter hopes for resurrection (3:11, 20-21).

    Step 3 — Weight: AGAINST the intermediate state: 6 Plain statements + 2 Contextually Clear, from at least 5 different authors (David, Heman, the Preacher, Peter, Paul), spanning OT and NT. FOR the intermediate state: 1 Contextually Clear + 1 Ambiguous, both from a single author (Paul).

    Step 4 — SIS Application: The Plain statements from multiple independent authors determine the reading of the Ambiguous one.     • Soul and spirit are used in synonymous parallelism — Isaiah 26:9; Luke 1:46-47

    What CANNOT Be Said (not explicitly stated or necessarily implied by Scripture)¶

    • Scripture does not state that humans have an "immortal soul" — the phrase does not appear
    • Scripture does not define death as "separation of soul from body" — no verse uses this definition
    • Scripture does not define death as "separation from God" — no verse defines it this way
    • Scripture does not state that the spirit (ruach) returning to God at death (Ecc 12:7) is a conscious entity going to heaven — it says "the spirit returns to God who gave it" without attributing consciousness or personality to it
    • Scripture does not state that nephesh or psyche is an immaterial substance that survives death independently — nephesh is used for corpses and animals; psyche is applied to sea creatures
    • Scripture does not state that man has three ontologically distinct parts (body, soul, spirit) — the listing in 1 Thessalonians 5:23 emphasizes wholeness
    • Scripture does not state that 2 Corinthians 5:8 or Philippians 1:23 describe a conscious intermediate state — Paul's stated preference is the resurrection body, not disembodiment
    • Scripture does not state that the dead are currently receiving their reward — Hebrews 11:39-40 explicitly denies this

    Conclusion¶

    The biblical data on human composition and death follows a consistent pattern from Genesis through Revelation. The creation formula of Genesis 2:7 defines humanity as dust animated by divine breath — the resulting entity IS the living soul, not a possessor of one. The same terminology applies to animals; the distinguishing feature is the image of God.

    At death, the formula reverses: dust returns to earth, breath returns to God, consciousness ceases. Multiple authors across both testaments describe the dead as unconscious and call death "sleep." The biblical hope is resurrection — transformation from mortality to immortality, from corruption to incorruption.

    Immortality belongs exclusively to God as a present possession. For humans, it is a future gift at the resurrection, conditional on Christ. The phrase "immortal soul" does not appear in Scripture.

    At the highest evidence tiers (E and N), every positional item points in the conditionalist direction. The ECT case on biblical anthropology depends entirely on inferences — concepts the text does not state (I-C and I-D) or ambiguous passages resolved against the weight of plain statements (I-B resolved Strong toward conditionalist). The evidence hierarchy governs: E > N > I-A > I-B > I-C > I-D.
