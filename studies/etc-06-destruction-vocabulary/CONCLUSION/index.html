    CONCLUSION — Destruction Vocabulary¶

    Question¶

    Hebrew/Greek destruction vocabulary: abad/apollumi (H6/G622), shamad (H8045), kalah (H3615), shachath (H7843), apoleia (G684), olethros (G3639). Lexical definitions, eschatological usage.

    Summary Answer¶

    The seven destruction words studied (abad, shamad, kalah, shachath, apollymi, apoleia, olethros) share a common semantic core: the ending or cessation of the object. No standard lexicon defines any of these words as "torment" or "ongoing conscious suffering." When used in eschatological contexts, these words are accompanied by cessation similes (chaff, wax, smoke, ashes, stubble, dung), explicit non-existence language (ayin), and perish-vs-life contrasts across four authors and both testaments. The LXX translators chose Greek destruction words for Hebrew destruction terms, not torment words, confirming that ancient translators understood these as words of actual destruction.

    Key Verses¶

    Psalm 37:10 — "For yet a little while, and the wicked shall not be: yea, thou shalt diligently consider his place, and it shall not be." (Explicit non-existence: Hebrew ayin = "not be." The wicked person and his place cease to exist.)

    Psalm 37:20 — "But the wicked shall perish, and the enemies of the LORD shall be as the fat of lambs: they shall consume; into smoke shall they consume away." (Two destruction words: abad + kalah. The smoke simile depicts complete dissipation into nothing.)

    Psalm 145:20 — "The LORD preserveth all them that love him: but all the wicked will he destroy." (Binary antonym contrast: shamar/shamad. The same God who preserves the righteous utterly destroys the wicked.)

    Matthew 10:28 — "And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell." (Jesus uses apollymi — lexically stronger than apokteino ["kill"] — for what God does to the whole person in gehenna.)

    John 3:16 — "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." (The central perish-vs-life contrast: apollymi as the opposite of eternal life.)

    Romans 6:23 — "For the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord." (Paul's definitive statement: the penalty for sin is death, not torment. Death contrasted with eternal life.)

    2 Thessalonians 1:9 — "Who shall be punished with everlasting destruction from the presence of the Lord, and from the glory of his power." (olethros aionios: the key Pauline eschatological passage. "Everlasting destruction" — the destruction noun modified by the permanence adjective.)

    Malachi 4:1,3 — "For, behold, the day cometh, that shall burn as an oven; and all the proud, yea, and all that do wickedly, shall be stubble: and the day that cometh shall burn them up, saith the LORD of hosts, that it shall leave them neither root nor branch... And ye shall tread down the wicked; for they shall be ashes under the soles of your feet." (The most comprehensive OT destruction passage: burn up, stubble, neither root nor branch, ashes under feet.)

    Obadiah 1:16 — "...and they shall be as though they had not been." (The strongest OT non-existence formula: kelo hayu. Not merely death but the retroactive language of never having existed.)

    Jude 1:7 — "Even as Sodom and Gomorrha, and the cities about them in like manner, giving themselves over to fornication, and going after strange flesh, are set forth for an example, suffering the vengeance of eternal fire." (Sodom suffered "eternal fire" — but Sodom is now ashes [2 Pet 2:6]. "Eternal fire" produces permanent destruction, not endless burning.)

    Analysis¶

    The Lexical Evidence¶

    The four Hebrew destruction words and three Greek destruction words form a coherent semantic domain with destruction/cessation as the common core.

    abad (H6) has 219 occurrences across 25 books. The primitive root means "to wander away, lose oneself; by implication to perish (causative, destroy)." The Qal stem dominates at approximately 61% (119 occurrences), and in the Qal the word is intransitive: the subject perishes — ceases to exist. The Piel (41 occurrences) and Hiphil (26 occurrences) provide the causative "destroy." The KJV translates abad as "perish" approximately 55% of the time and "destroy" approximately 32%. The UBS semantic domain classification places abad in the Non-Exist domain (002002002006). This is not an interpretive judgment but a standard lexicographic classification: the word belongs to the domain of non-existence.

    shamad (H8045) has 101 occurrences and represents the most absolute end of the destruction spectrum. While abad can mean "lose" or "wander," shamad never means anything less than total destruction. The KJV renders shamad as "destroy/destroyed" approximately 94% of the time. The word appears almost exclusively in the Hiphil (69 occurrences, causative — someone deliberately exterminates) or Niphal (21 occurrences, passive — the subject is exterminated). There is no Qal form, which means shamad always carries intensive or causative force. When Psalm 145:20 pairs shamar ("preserve") with shamad ("destroy"), the contrast is between preservation and total annihilation.

    kalah (H3615) has 258 occurrences and the broadest usage range of the four Hebrew words. It can mean "to finish" (Gen 2:1, completing creation) or "to consume" (Psa 37:20, wicked consumed into smoke). In judgment contexts, kalah consistently means "consume to nothing." Three key passages pair kalah with the Hebrew noun of non-existence (ayin): Psalm 37:20 ("consume into smoke"), Psalm 59:13 ("consume them, that they may not be"), and Psalm 104:35 ("let the sinners be consumed out of the earth, and let the wicked be no more"). Each of these states the result of kalah as non-existence.

    shachath (H7843) has 164 occurrences and bridges corruption and destruction. Genesis 6:11-13 uses shachath three times: the earth "was corrupt" (Niphal), flesh "had corrupted" its way (Hiphil), and God declares "I will destroy them" (Hiphil participle). The same root describes the sin and the judgment, establishing a principle: corruption leads to destruction, and destruction is the natural end of corruption. The Hiphil dominates (98 occurrences), showing that most uses describe deliberate destruction by an external agent.

    apollymi (G622) has 102 occurrences and is the primary Greek destruction verb. The LXX maps the Hebrew abad to this Greek verb 141 times (PMI 6.14, score 30.45) — one of the strongest Hebrew-Greek verb pairings in the LXX, making apollymi the canonical NT heir to abad. Louw-Nida classifies apollymi in two semantic domains: Violence/Destroy (20.003) and Exist/Non-exist (13.003). No lexicon places apollymi in a "torment" or "ongoing suffering" domain. The critical antonym relationship is apollymi-sozo: 14 verses set "destroy/perish" and "save" in direct opposition (Matt 16:25; Luke 9:24; 19:10; Jam 4:12; 1 Cor 1:18; 2 Cor 2:15, among others). What sozo rescues from is what apollymi inflicts. If sozo means "to preserve life/existence," then apollymi means "to end life/existence."

    apoleia (G684) has 20 occurrences and is the noun form of apollymi's action. The Louw-Nida classification is 20.031 (Violence/Destroy). The word is concentrated in 2 Peter (5 occurrences in 61 verses), making that book the epicenter of NT destruction vocabulary. Matthew 7:13 is structurally decisive: two ways, two gates, two destinations — apoleia (destruction) and zoe (life). The binary structure means the two outcomes are antithetical.

    olethros (G3639) has only 4 occurrences, all Pauline. It is a masculine noun from ollymi ("to destroy"), the same root behind apollymi. The KJV renders olethros uniformly as "destruction." The key passage is 2 Thessalonians 1:9: olethros aionios ("everlasting destruction"). The other occurrences are 1 Thessalonians 5:3 (sudden eschatological destruction), 1 Corinthians 5:5 (disciplinary destruction of flesh), and 1 Timothy 6:9 (destruction paired with apoleia).

    The Cross-Testament Vocabulary Chain¶

    The LXX translation patterns establish direct cross-testament vocabulary continuity. The LXX translates abad as apollymi 141 times, shamad as apollymi 23 times, kalah as synteleo ("to complete entirely/consume") 85 times, and shachath as diaphtheiro ("to rot thoroughly/destroy") 48 times.

    The translators consistently chose Greek words with cessation semantics. They did not choose basanizo ("torment"), odunao ("grieve/suffer"), or any word carrying "ongoing conscious suffering" as its meaning. This is a translator's semantic judgment made by scholars who read both languages natively: they understood Hebrew abad, shamad, kalah, and shachath as words of actual destruction and chose Greek equivalents that carry the same meaning.

    The synteleo mapping for kalah is significant because synteleo itself combines syn ("together/completely") + teleo ("to bring to an end"), doubling the completeness emphasis. The diaphtheiro mapping for shachath preserves the corruption-destruction bridge: diaphtheiro means "to rot thoroughly/destroy," maintaining shachath's dual semantic of corruption and destruction. This chain extends into the NT: 2 Peter 2:12 says the wicked "shall utterly perish in their own corruption (phthora)" — phthora is from the same root as diaphtheiro.

    The Perish-vs-Life Binary¶

    Five passages from four authors across both testaments present destruction/death and life as antithetical binary outcomes:

    1. John 3:16 — "should not perish (apollymi) but have everlasting life (zoe aionios)"
    2. Matthew 7:13-14 — "broad way to destruction (apoleia)" vs. "narrow way to life (zoe)"
    3. Romans 6:23 — "wages of sin is death (thanatos)" vs. "gift of God is eternal life (zoe aionios)"
    4. Philippians 3:19 — "whose end is destruction (apoleia)"
    5. Psalm 145:20 — "the LORD preserveth (shamar) all them that love him: but all the wicked will he destroy (shamad)"

    The consistency of this pattern across David, Jesus, Paul, and the author of Hebrews, in both poetry and prose, in both testaments, in both direct teaching and epistolary argument, establishes a multi-author, cross-genre, cross-testament framework: life is one outcome, destruction/death/perishing is the other. They are not two forms of eternal existence but two opposite endpoints.

    Cessation Similes¶

    Every biblical simile comparing the fate of the wicked to a physical substance depicts a substance that ceases to exist:

    • Chaff burned in fire (Psa 1:4; Matt 3:12; Hos 13:3) — chaff burns completely and ceases to exist
    • Wax melting before fire (Psa 68:2) — wax melts away to nothing
    • Smoke dissipating (Psa 37:20; Psa 68:2; Hos 13:3) — smoke disperses and vanishes
    • Fat of lambs consumed on the altar (Psa 37:20) — fat is entirely consumed
    • Stubble burned (Mal 4:1; Nah 1:10; Isa 1:31) — stubble is completely consumed by fire
    • Ashes remaining after combustion (Mal 4:3; 2 Pet 2:6) — ashes are the end product of total destruction
    • Morning cloud and early dew passing away (Hos 13:3) — both dissipate completely
    • Dung decomposing (Job 20:7) — dung decomposes and disappears
    • Tow/flax burning (Isa 1:31) — tow catches fire and is consumed instantly

    No biblical simile depicts something that continues to exist in a ruined state. No simile depicts ongoing conscious suffering. The consistent imagery across multiple authors is cessation: the object is consumed, dissipated, dissolved, or decomposed until nothing remains.

    Non-Existence Language¶

    Four passages use explicit non-existence language (the Hebrew noun ayin, H369, "not-be/non-existence") to describe the result of destruction:

    1. Psalm 37:10 — "the wicked shall not be (ayin)" and "his place shall not be (ayin)"
    2. Psalm 59:13 — "consume them, that they may not be (ayin)"
    3. Psalm 104:35 — "let the wicked be no more (ayin)"
    4. Obadiah 1:16 — "they shall be as though they had not been (kelo hayu)"

    These are not poetic hyperbole. They are the stated result of the destruction vocabulary used in context. When the psalmist says "consume them, that they may not be," the PURPOSE of the consumption is explicitly non-existence. When Obadiah says "as though they had not been," the result goes beyond mere death to the language of retroactive non-existence.

    Destruction vs. Torment: Distinct Word Families¶

    The destruction words studied here (apollymi, apoleia, olethros, and their Hebrew antecedents) and the torment words used in ECT proof-texts (basanizo G928, basanismos G929, kolasis G2851) are different Greek word families with different roots, different semantic ranges, and non-overlapping definitions.

    apollymi is from apo + ollymi ("to destroy"); basanizo is from basanos ("touchstone/testing stone"). apoleia is the noun state of destruction; basanismos is the noun state of torment/testing. These are not synonyms. They do not share a root. They do not appear in the same semantic domains in any standard lexicon.

    The quantitative disparity is significant. When applied to the fate of the wicked: - Destruction vocabulary (apollymi, apoleia, olethros, and related words) appears in approximately 75-80 verses across the full range of biblical genres (law, wisdom, prophecy, gospel, epistle, apocalyptic) - Torment vocabulary (basanizo, basanismos) applied to eschatological judgment of humans appears in 2-3 verses, all in the apocalyptic genre (Revelation)

    The Bible describes the fate of the wicked with destruction words approximately 30 times more frequently than with torment words. The torment passages are limited to a single genre (apocalyptic symbolism), while destruction passages appear across all genres.

    The Historical Destruction Paradigms¶

    The Flood and Sodom function as paradigms for eschatological judgment. Jesus uses apollymi for both: "the flood came, and destroyed (apollymi) them all... fire and brimstone from heaven, and destroyed (apollymi) them all" (Luke 17:27,29). Peter uses Sodom as an explicit "ensample" (hupodeigma) of what will happen to the ungodly: "turning the cities of Sodom and Gomorrha into ashes condemned them with an overthrow, making them an ensample unto those that after should live ungodly" (2 Pet 2:6). Jude says Sodom suffered "the vengeance of eternal fire" (Jude 1:7).

    The paradigm is decisive. Sodom suffered "eternal fire" (Jude 1:7). Sodom was turned to "ashes" (2 Pet 2:6). Sodom is set forth as an "example" of eschatological judgment (2 Pet 2:6). If the paradigm case of "eternal fire" produced ashes rather than endless burning, then "eternal fire" describes fire whose results are permanent, not fire that burns endlessly. This governs interpretation of "everlasting fire" (Matt 25:41) and "tormented with fire and brimstone" (Rev 14:10).

    Counter-Evidence Evaluated¶

    apollymi as "lost" (Luke 15:4-32): Three parables use apollymi in its "lost/straying" sense — the sheep, coin, and son are all recoverable. ECT proponents argue this shows apollymi does not mean annihilation. The counter-argument is the illegitimate totality transfer fallacy: apollymi has four semantic categories (destroy utterly, perish/die, be lost/straying, lose/waste), and a word used in four categories does not carry all four meanings in every occurrence. In Luke 15, the context is parables about seeking the lost — recoverable loss. In Matt 10:28, the context is God destroying soul and body in gehenna — irreversible destruction. Context, not etymology, determines which meaning applies.

    olethros as remedial (1 Cor 5:5): olethros is used for the "destruction of the flesh" of a living church member, with the explicit purpose "that the spirit may be saved." ECT proponents argue this shows olethros can be something less than final annihilation. The response: disciplinary action against a living church member under discipline is categorically different from eschatological judgment of the impenitent. The salvation-purpose clause ("that the spirit may be saved") is present in 1 Cor 5:5 and absent from every eschatological use of olethros (2 Thess 1:9; 1 Thess 5:3). The disciplinary context determines the meaning; the eschatological context determines a different meaning.

    apoleia as "waste" (Matt 26:8): apoleia is used for the "waste" of expensive ointment. ECT proponents argue the ointment still exists in a "wasted" state. Counter-evaluation: the ointment was poured out — used up, gone, consumed. It ceased to exist as usable ointment. The "waste" meaning actually supports cessation rather than "ruined but still existing."

    Rev 14:10-11: This passage uses basanizo (torment), not destruction vocabulary. It is the strongest passage supporting ECT. It must be evaluated on its own terms: (1) The genre is apocalyptic symbolism (Revelation). (2) The OT source text for "smoke ascending for ever" is Isaiah 34:10 (Edom's judgment — fire long extinguished). (3) "Smoke ascending" also echoes Genesis 19:28 (Abraham watching smoke rise from Sodom's completed destruction). (4) basanizo is a different word family from apollymi/apoleia/olethros. This passage uses torment vocabulary, and it warrants full treatment in its own study. (Examined in depth in etc7-11 and etc7-15.)

    Matt 25:41,46: "Everlasting fire" and "everlasting punishment" use aionios. The word kolasin (punishment, from kolazo) is distinct from basanismos (torment). The Hebrews pattern (eternal redemption Heb 9:12, eternal judgment Heb 6:2, eternal salvation Heb 5:9 — all permanent results of completed actions) and the Sodom paradigm (Jude 1:7 + 2 Pet 2:6: "eternal fire" → ashes) bear on how aionios functions when modifying a result-noun. (Examined in depth in etc7-08 and etc7-14.)

    Mark 9:43-48: Jesus quotes Isaiah 66:24 three times. The source text describes peger (H6297) — corpses, dead bodies. The worm feeds on dead bodies (decomposition); the fire consumes dead bodies (cremation). "Unquenchable" (asbestos G762) means fire that cannot be put out (cf. Jer 17:27 — Jerusalem's "unquenchable" fire, extinguished for millennia), not fire that burns forever. (Examined in depth in etc7-05 and etc7-15.)

    Word Studies¶

    H6 abad — 219 occurrences¶

    Primitive root: "to wander away, lose oneself; by implication to perish (causative, destroy)." Qal dominates (~61%, 119 occurrences) — intransitive "perish." Piel 41 occurrences, Hiphil 26 occurrences — causative "destroy." UBS semantic domain: Non-Exist. KJV: "perish" ~55%, "destroy" ~32%, "lost" ~6%. LXX maps to apollymi 141 times (PMI 6.14). The broadest Hebrew destruction verb. Key eschatological uses: Psa 1:6 (way of ungodly perishes), Psa 37:20 (wicked perish), Psa 68:2 (wicked perish at God's presence), Psa 92:9 (enemies perish), Deut 7:10 (God destroys those who hate him), Job 20:7 (perish forever like dung).

    G622 apollymi — 102 occurrences¶

    From apo + ollymi: "to destroy fully." Active = destroy; Middle/Passive = perish, be lost. Louw-Nida: Violence/Destroy (20.003) AND Exist/Non-exist (13.003). KJV: "perish" 36%, "destroy" 30%, "lose/lost" 27%. Critical antonym: sozo (G4982, 14 verses in direct opposition). No lexicon places apollymi in a "torment" domain. LXX heir to abad (141 co-occurrences). Key eschatological uses: Matt 10:28 (destroy soul and body), John 3:16 (should not perish), Luke 13:3,5 (all likewise perish), Luke 17:27,29 (flood and Sodom destroyed them all), 2 Pet 3:9 (not willing any should perish).

    H8045 shamad — 101 occurrences¶

    Primitive root: "to desolate — destroy, destruction, bring to nought, overthrow." Most absolute Hebrew destruction word. Almost exclusively Hiphil (69 occurrences) or Niphal (21 occurrences). No Qal form = always intensive/causative force. KJV: "destroy/destroyed" ~94%. LXX maps to apollymi 23 times. Key eschatological uses: Psa 37:38 (transgressors destroyed together), Psa 92:7 (destroyed for ever), Psa 145:20 (all the wicked will he destroy), Isa 13:9 (destroy sinners out of the land).

    H3615 kalah — 258 occurrences¶

    Primitive root: "to end, cease, be finished, perish; to complete, consume." Piel dominates (141 occurrences = intensive). In judgment contexts, consistently means "consume to nothing." Three passages pair kalah with ayin (non-existence): Psa 37:20, 59:13, 104:35. LXX maps to synteleo 85 times. Key eschatological uses: Psa 37:20 (consume into smoke), Psa 59:13 (consume that they may not be), Nah 1:8-10 (utter end), Isa 1:28 (consumed).

    H7843 shachath — 164 occurrences¶

    Primitive root: "to decay, ruin (causatively) — batter, corrupt, destroy." Hiphil dominates (98 occurrences). Bridges corruption and destruction. Gen 6:11-13 principle: the same root describes sin (corruption) and judgment (destruction). LXX maps to diaphtheiro 48 times. The corruption-destruction chain extends into NT through phthora (2 Pet 2:12; Gal 6:8; 1 Cor 15:42,50).

    G684 apoleia — 20 occurrences¶

    Feminine noun from G622 apollymi: "ruin or loss." Louw-Nida: 20.031 (Violence/Destroy). Concentrated in 2 Peter (5 occurrences in 61 verses). Matt 7:13: structurally decisive binary — destruction vs. life. Phil 3:19: "whose end is destruction" — telos + apoleia = final outcome is destruction. 2 Pet 3:7: "perdition of ungodly men" — reserved for judgment day. Rev 17:8,11: beast "goeth into perdition" — apoleia as terminus.

    G3639 olethros — 4 occurrences¶

    Masculine noun from ollymi: "ruin, death, punishment." All Pauline. KJV: uniformly "destruction." 2 Thess 1:9: olethros aionios = "everlasting destruction" — the key eschatological passage. 1 Thess 5:3: "sudden destruction" — eschatological. 1 Cor 5:5: "destruction of the flesh" — disciplinary (the only remedial use, with salvation-purpose clause). 1 Tim 6:9: olethros + apoleia together — double destruction.

    What CAN Be Said¶

    Scripture explicitly states or necessarily implies:

    • All seven destruction words (abad, shamad, kalah, shachath, apollymi, apoleia, olethros) describe the ending or cessation of their object. This is what the words mean in every standard lexicon.
    • The result of destruction is non-existence: "shall not be" (Psa 37:10), "may not be" (Psa 59:13), "be no more" (Psa 104:35), "as though they had not been" (Oba 1:16).
    • Every biblical simile for the fate of the wicked depicts a substance that ceases to exist.
    • The perish-vs-life contrast (John 3:16, Matt 7:13, Rom 6:23, Phil 3:19, Psa 145:20) presents destruction/death and life as antithetical outcomes across four authors and both testaments.
    • God can destroy both soul and body in gehenna (Matt 10:28). apollymi (destroy) is a stronger word than apokteino (kill).
    • The wages of sin is death (Rom 6:23), not torment.
    • The Sodom paradigm ("eternal fire" → ashes → example for the ungodly) shows that "eternal fire" produces permanent destruction, not endless burning (Jude 1:7 + 2 Pet 2:6).
    • Destruction vocabulary and torment vocabulary are distinct word families. No lexicon classifies any destruction word as "torment."
    • The soul can die (Ezek 18:4). God alone has immortality (1 Tim 6:16). The wicked are never said to receive immortality.

    What CANNOT Be Said¶

    Scripture does not explicitly state or necessarily imply:

    • That destruction vocabulary is metaphorical for "spiritual ruin" while the subject continues to exist consciously. No verse states this. The non-existence language (ayin) in four passages directly contradicts it.
    • That "everlasting destruction" necessarily describes an ongoing process of destroying. The Hebrews pattern and the Sodom paradigm point the other direction, but the text of 2 Thess 1:9 itself does not specify whether aionios modifies the process or the result. This requires interpretation.
    • That the wicked experience eternal conscious torment as described by ECT. The Bible uses destruction words for the fate of the wicked; it uses torment words (basanizo) in Rev 14:10-11 (apocalyptic genre with OT source texts describing completed judgments). The torment passages require their own dedicated study.
    • That the Luke 15 "lost" meaning of apollymi determines the meaning in judgment contexts. This would be the illegitimate totality transfer fallacy. It cannot be said from the text that "lost" in a seeking parable governs "destroy" in a judgment teaching.
    • That destruction vocabulary by itself proves any comprehensive eschatological doctrine. Destruction vocabulary shows what the Bible says about the fate of the wicked using destruction language. Other passages (Rev 14:10-11, Matt 25:41,46, Mark 9:43-48) use different vocabulary and require separate analysis.

    Difficult Passages¶

    Rev 14:10-11: Uses basanizo (torment), not destruction vocabulary. Apocalyptic genre. OT source text (Isa 34:10) describes Edom's judgment, whose fire is long extinguished. "Smoke ascending" echoes Gen 19:28 (smoke from Sodom's completed destruction). The passage requires full analysis in its own study of torment vocabulary and apocalyptic language. (etc7-11 and etc7-15.)

    Matt 25:41,46: "Everlasting fire" and "everlasting punishment." The fire was "prepared for the devil and his angels" — not originally for humans. kolasin (punishment) is not basanismos (torment). The Hebrews aionios pattern and Sodom paradigm bear on interpretation. (etc7-08 and etc7-14.)

    Mark 9:43-48: Quotes Isa 66:24, which describes corpses (peger). "Unquenchable" (asbestos) = fire that cannot be stopped, not fire that burns forever (cf. Jer 17:27). (etc7-05 and etc7-15.)

    1 Cor 5:5: Remedial olethros with salvation-purpose clause. The only non-eschatological use of olethros. The salvific purpose clause distinguishes it from eschatological uses.

    Luke 15: apollymi as "lost" (recoverable). Legitimate semantic category of apollymi, but parabolic context of seeking the lost is distinct from eschatological judgment context of Matt 10:28 and John 3:16.

    Conclusion¶

    The destruction vocabulary of the Bible — abad, shamad, kalah, shachath, apollymi, apoleia, olethros — forms a coherent semantic domain with cessation of existence as its common core meaning. Across 68 explicit statements drawn from 73 verses, 43 use destruction/cessation/non-existence vocabulary in a direction consistent with conditionalism (the wicked cease to exist). Zero explicit statements use destruction vocabulary in a direction consistent with eternal conscious torment. The 25 neutral statements are factual observations about lexicographic data, LXX translation patterns, genre classifications, and textual features that both sides accept.

    The two I-B inferences (ECT claims that destruction vocabulary is metaphorical for spiritual ruin, and that "everlasting destruction" means ongoing process) both resolved Strong toward conditionalism. The conditionalist side has six or more Plain didactic statements (non-existence language in Psalms, "destroy soul and body" in Matt 10:28, "wages of sin is death" in Rom 6:23). The ECT side has no Plain statements using destruction vocabulary — its evidence comes from torment vocabulary (basanizo in Rev 14:10-11, a different word family in apocalyptic genre) and from contextual arguments about aionios that the Hebrews pattern and Sodom paradigm address.

    The data from this study: 43 Cond. E-items, 0 ECT E-items, 1 Cond. N-item, 4 Cond. I-A items, 2 ECT I-B items resolved Strong toward Cond. The tally reflects what the biblical text says when it uses destruction vocabulary.
