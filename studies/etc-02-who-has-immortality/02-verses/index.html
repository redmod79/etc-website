    Verses¶

    Question¶

    Who possesses immortality? Is the soul inherently immortal or is immortality a divine gift? Examine athanasia (G110), aphtharsia (G861), thnetos (G2349).

    God alone possesses immortality — the crux text¶

    Which in his times he shall shew, [who is] the blessed and only Potentate, the King of kings, and Lord of lords; Who only hath immortality, dwelling in the light which no man can approach unto; whom no man hath seen, nor can see: to whom [be] honour and power everlasting. Amen.

    Context: Paul's charge to Timothy — doxological climax describing God's exclusive attributes. Follows command to fight the good fight of faith and lay hold on eternal life (v.12).

    Notes: Monos (only, G3441) + echon (having, present active participle) + athanasiān (immortality, accusative). The exclusive attribution is unambiguous: God alone has immortality. No qualifier (self-existent vs. derived) appears in the text. The participle echon describes ongoing possession. Paired with two other exclusive divine attributes: dwelling in unapproachable light, whom no man has seen.

    Sown in corruption, raised in incorruption — the resurrection contrasts¶

    So also [is] the resurrection of the dead. It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption: It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory: it is sown in weakness; it is raised in power: It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body. There is a natural body, and there is a spiritual body.

    Context: Paul's resurrection argument in 1 Corinthians 15. Four contrasts between the present mortal body and the resurrection body: corruption/incorruption, dishonour/glory, weakness/power, natural/spiritual.

    Notes: Phthora (G5356, corruption) paired with aphtharsia (G861, incorruption). The present body is characterized by corruption — decay, mortality, weakness. The resurrection body is characterized by incorruption — the transformation must occur at resurrection, not before.

    Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom — corruption cannot inherit incorruption¶

    Now this I say, brethren, that flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God; neither doth corruption inherit incorruption.

    Context: Transitional verse between the resurrection body argument (vv.35-49) and the mystery of transformation (vv.51-54). States the necessity of transformation.

    Notes: Phthora (corruption) and aphtharsia (incorruption) are placed in absolute opposition. The present corruptible state cannot simply continue into the kingdom — transformation is required. This is a key verse against any claim that immortality is already possessed.

    This mortal must PUT ON immortality — acquisition verb with athanasia¶

    For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal [must] put on immortality. So when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality, then shall be brought to pass the saying that is written, Death is swallowed up in victory.

    Context: Climax of Paul's resurrection argument. Two parallel pairs: corruptible/incorruption (phthartos/aphtharsia) and mortal/immortality (thnetos/athanasia). Both use the verb endyo (put on).

    • δεῖ (G1163) — V-PAI-3S — Pres Act Ind Sg thirdPers
    • γὰρ (G1063) — CONJ — τὸ ὁ G3588 [T-NSN] Nom Sg N
    • φθαρτὸν (G5349) — A-NSN — Nom Sg N
    • τοῦτο (G3778) — D-NSN — Nom Sg N
    • ἐνδύσασθαι (G1746) — V-AMN — Aor Mid Inf
    • ἀφθαρσίαν (G861) — N-ASF — Acc Sg F
    • καὶ (G2532) — CONJ — τὸ ὁ G3588 [T-NSN] Nom Sg N
    • θνητὸν (G2349) — A-NSN — Nom Sg N
    • ἀθανασίαν. (G110) — N-ASF — Acc Sg F

    Notes: Endyo (G1746) is an aorist middle infinitive in v.53 (endysasthai) — 'to clothe oneself with.' In v.54 it shifts to aorist middle subjunctive (endysetai). Thnetos (G2349) is used substantivally — 'this mortal' (touto to thneton), not restricted to 'this mortal body.' Athanasia (G110) appears in accusative as the object acquired. The verb endyo consistently means acquisition of something not previously worn (cf. Rom 13:14, Gal 3:27, Eph 4:24). Death is swallowed up (katepothe, aorist passive of katapino) — quoting Isa 25:8.

    Those who SEEK immortality — aphtharsia as object of aspiration¶

    To them who by patient continuance in well doing seek for glory and honour and immortality, eternal life:

    Context: Paul's description of God's impartial judgment. Those who seek (zeteo, present active participle) glory, honor, and immortality receive eternal life. The seeking implies non-possession.

    • τοῖς (G3588) — T-DPM — Dat Pl M
    • μὲν (G3303) — PRT — καθ' κατά G2596 [PREP]
    • ὑπομονὴν (G5281) — N-ASF — Acc Sg F
    • ἔργου (G2041) — N-GSN — Gen Sg N
    • ἀγαθοῦ (G18) — A-GSN — Gen Sg N
    • δόξαν (G1391) — N-ASF — Acc Sg F
    • καὶ (G2532) — CONJ — τιμὴν τιμή G5092 [N-ASF] Acc Sg F
    • ζητοῦσιν (G2212) — V-PAP-DPM — Pres Act Ptcp Dat Pl M
    • ζωὴν (G2222) — N-ASF — Acc Sg F
    • αἰώνιον· (G166) — A-ASF — Acc Sg F

    Notes: Zetousin (seeking, present active participle of zeteo G2212) + aphtharsian (incorruption/immortality, accusative). If immortality were already possessed, seeking it would be nonsensical. Aphtharsia is listed alongside doxa (glory) and time (honor) as future rewards for patient well-doing. The KJV translates aphtharsia as 'immortality' here.

    Christ brought life and immortality TO LIGHT through the gospel¶

    But is now made manifest by the appearing of our Saviour Jesus Christ, who hath abolished death, and hath brought life and immortality to light through the gospel:

    Context: Paul describing God's saving purpose. Christ's work is twofold: abolishing death (katargeo + thanatos) and illuminating life and immortality (photizo + zoe + aphtharsia).

    • διὰ (G1223) — PREP — τῆς ὁ G3588 [T-GSF] Gen Sg F
    • ἐπιφανείας (G2015) — N-GSF — Gen Sg F

    • Σωτῆρος (G4990) — N-GSM — Gen Sg M
    • ἡμῶν (G1473) — P-1GP — Gen Pl
    • Χριστοῦ (G5547) — N-GSM — Gen Sg M
    • Ἰησοῦ, (G2424) — N-GSM — Gen Sg M
    • καταργήσαντος (G2673) — V-AAP-GSM — Aor Act Ptcp Gen Sg M
    • μὲν (G3303) — PRT — τὸν ὁ G3588 [T-ASM] Acc Sg M
    • θάνατον (G2288) — N-ASM — Acc Sg M
    • φωτίσαντος (G5461) — V-AAP-GSM — Aor Act Ptcp Gen Sg M
    • καὶ (G2532) — CONJ — ἀφθαρσίαν ἀφθαρσία G861 [N-ASF] Acc Sg F

    Notes: Photisantos (aorist active participle of photizo G5461 — 'having brought to light') + zoen (life) + aphtharsian (incorruption/immortality). The verb photizo means to illuminate, reveal, bring to light — Christ revealed immortality through the gospel, making visible what was previously hidden. Katargesantos (aorist active participle of katargeo G2673 — 'having abolished/rendered inoperative') + thanaton (death). Christ's double action: death abolished, life and immortality revealed.

    Mortal body under sin's dominion¶

    Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, that ye should obey it in the lusts thereof.

    Context: Paul's ethical exhortation following the death-to-sin argument (Rom 6:1-11). The mortal body is the present arena of spiritual warfare.

    Notes: Thneto (G2349, dative singular neuter) modifies somati (body). The mortal body is the current human condition — Paul does not speak of an immortal soul alongside the mortal body. Sin reigns in the mortal body because mortality and sin are linked (Rom 5:12).

    God will quicken your MORTAL bodies — future vivification¶

    But if the Spirit of him that raised up Jesus from the dead dwell in you, he that raised up Christ from the dead shall also quicken your mortal bodies by his Spirit that dwelleth in you.

    Context: Paul's Spirit-life argument in Romans 8. The same Spirit that raised Christ will also make alive (zoopoieo, future tense) the believers' mortal bodies.

    • καὶ (G2532) — CONJ — τὰ ὁ G3588 [T-APN] Acc Pl N
    • θνητὰ (G2349) — A-APN — Acc Pl N
    • σώματα (G4983) — N-APN — Acc Pl N

    • αὐτοῦ (G846) — P-GSM — Gen Sg M

    Notes: Zoopoiesei (future active indicative of zoopoieo G2227 — 'will make alive') + ta thneta somata (the mortal bodies, accusative plural). The vivification is future — the mortal bodies are not yet made alive. The indwelling Spirit is the guarantee, not the present fulfillment. Thneta (G2349, accusative plural neuter) modifies somata (bodies).

    Life of Jesus manifested in mortal flesh¶

    For we which live are alway delivered unto death for Jesus' sake, that the life also of Jesus might be made manifest in our mortal flesh.

    Context: Paul's treasure-in-earthen-vessels argument (2 Cor 4:7-12). The apostolic paradox: continual dying so that divine life may be displayed.

    Notes: Thnete (G2349, dative singular feminine) modifies sarki (flesh). Here thnetos agrees with sarx (flesh, feminine), hence the feminine form. The present condition is mortal; divine life is manifested through the mortal vessel.

    Mortal swallowed up by life — clothing metaphor for resurrection¶

The verb abad (perish/be lost) applied to thoughts at the moment of death — consciousness ceases when the breath departs.

    By man came death; by man came resurrection — Adam/Christ typology¶

    For since by man [came] death, by man [came] also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive.

    Context: Paul's Adam/Christ parallel. Death entered through Adam; resurrection comes through Christ. The remedy for death is resurrection, not an already-immortal soul.

    Notes: Di' anthropou thanatos (through man, death) vs. di' anthropou anastasis nekron (through man, resurrection of dead). The parallel is exact: the problem is death, the solution is resurrection. If humans already had immortal souls, resurrection would be unnecessary — the soul would survive death regardless.

    The last enemy destroyed is death — death as enemy, not gateway¶

    The last enemy [that] shall be destroyed [is] death.

    Context: Christ's eschatological victory. Death is the final enemy to be abolished (katargeo).

    Notes: Eschatos echthros katargeitai ho thanatos (last enemy abolished [is] death). Death is called an 'enemy' — not a friend, not a gateway to immortal bliss. It is 'destroyed/abolished' (katargeo G2673, present passive — being rendered inoperative). If death were merely the liberation of an immortal soul from the body, calling it the last 'enemy' would be strange.

    We shall all be changed — transformation at the last trumpet¶

    Behold, I shew you a mystery; We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed, In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed.

    Context: The mystery revealed: some believers will be alive at Christ's return and transformed without dying. The dead are raised incorruptible.

    Notes: Allagesometha (we shall be changed, future passive of allasso G236). The transformation is a discrete event at the last trumpet — not a gradual process or an already-existing state.

    He will swallow up death in victory — OT promise of death's abolition¶

    He will swallow up death in victory; and the Lord GOD will wipe away tears from off all faces; and the rebuke of his people shall he take away from off all the earth: for the LORD hath spoken [it].

    Context: Isaiah's eschatological banquet vision. God will swallow up (bala') death (maveth) for victory (lanetsach). Quoted in 1 Cor 15:54.

    Notes: Billa' hamaveth lanetsach (he has swallowed death forever/to victory). This is the OT background for Paul's 'death swallowed up in victory' (1 Cor 15:54). Death is to be destroyed, not perpetuated.

    Upholding all things by the word of his power — divine sustaining¶

    Who being the brightness of [his] glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power, when he had by himself purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high;

    Context: The prologue to Hebrews. Christ upholds (phero, present active participle) all things (ta panta) by his powerful word.

    Notes: Pheron (bearing/upholding, present active participle of phero G5342) + ta panta (all things) + to rhemati tes dynameos autou (by the word of his power). All things — including human life — are sustained by Christ's active power. If human souls were inherently immortal, they would not need sustaining.

    By him all things consist — Christ holds all together¶

    And he is before all things, and by him all things consist.

    Context: Paul's Christological hymn. Christ is both temporally prior to and sustainer of all created reality.

    Notes: Ta panta en auto synesteken (all things in him hold together, perfect active of synistemi G4921). Without Christ's sustaining power, all things — including human existence — would not hold together.

    Every living soul died in the sea — psyche dies¶

    And the second angel poured out his vial upon the sea; and it became as the blood of a dead [man]: and every living soul died in the sea.

    Context: Second bowl judgment. Every living soul (pasa psyche zosa) in the sea died.

    Notes: Pasa psyche zosa apethanen (every living soul died). Psyche (G5590) is the subject of apethanen (died, aorist active of apothnesko G599). Souls die. This is a direct statement that psyche is mortal.

    Dead body (nephesh met) — nephesh used for corpses¶

    Neither shall he go in to any dead body, nor defile himself for his father, or for his mother;

    Context: Priestly holiness code. The high priest may not approach a dead body — literally 'dead souls' (nephesh met).

    Notes: Nephesh met (H5315 + dead) — literally 'dead soul.' The word nephesh is used for corpses, proving that nephesh is not inherently immortal. A nephesh can be dead.

    Dead body (nephesh met) — Nazirite separation¶

    All the days that he separateth [himself] unto the LORD he shall come at no dead body.

    Context: Nazirite vow regulations. The Nazirite must not approach a dead body (nephesh met).

    Notes: Al-nephesh met (unto/near a dead soul) — same construction as Lev 21:11. Nephesh used for a dead person/corpse. The 'soul' is the dead person, not a surviving conscious entity.

    Animals as nephesh chayyah — same term as for man in Gen 2:7¶

    And God said, Let the waters bring forth abundantly the moving creature that hath life, and fowl [that] may fly above the earth in the open firmament of heaven. And God created great whales, and every living creature that moveth, which the waters brought forth abundantly, after their kind, and every winged fowl after his kind: and God saw that [it was] good.

    Context: Day 5 of creation. Sea creatures and birds called nephesh chayyah — the same term applied to man in Gen 2:7.

    Notes: Nephesh chayyah (living soul/creature) — used for sea animals and birds. The same phrase describes man in Gen 2:7. Man does not receive a qualitatively different type of nephesh from animals in the creation account.

    If no resurrection, the dead in Christ are PERISHED — no hope without resurrection¶

    Now if Christ be preached that he rose from the dead, how say some among you that there is no resurrection of the dead? But if there be no resurrection of the dead, then is Christ not risen: And if Christ be not risen, then [is] our preaching vain, and your faith [is] also vain. Yea, and we are found false witnesses of God; because we have testified of God that he raised up Christ: whom he raised not up, if so be that the dead rise not. For if the dead rise not, then is not Christ raised: And if Christ be not raised, your faith [is] vain; ye are yet in your sins. Then they also which are fallen asleep in Christ are perished. If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men most miserable.

    Context: Paul's reductio ad absurdum argument against denying the resurrection. Without resurrection: faith is vain, sins unforgiven, the dead in Christ have PERISHED.

    Notes: V.18: apollonto (perished, aorist middle indicative of apollymi G622) — those fallen asleep in Christ have perished if there is no resurrection. If they had immortal souls already in God's presence, Paul could not say they have 'perished' — they would be alive in heaven regardless of resurrection. V.19: 'If in this life only we have hope' — the hope is resurrection, not disembodied survival.

    The endyo (put on) metaphor — acquisition across Paul¶

    Rom 13:14: But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to [fulfil] the lusts [thereof]. Gal 3:27: For as many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ. Eph 4:24: And that ye put on the new man, which after God is created in righteousness and true holiness. Eph 6:11: Put on the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to stand against the wiles of the devil. Col 3:10: And have put on the new [man], which is renewed in knowledge after the image of him that created him. Col 3:12: Put on therefore, as the elect of God, holy and beloved, bowels of mercies, kindness, humbleness of mind, meekness, longsuffering.

    Context: Every Pauline use of endyo (G1746) describes acquiring/receiving something not previously possessed — clothing oneself with Christ, the new man, armor, virtues.

    • δεῖ (G1163) — V-PAI-3S — Pres Act Ind Sg thirdPers
    • γὰρ (G1063) — CONJ — τὸ ὁ G3588 [T-NSN] Nom Sg N
    • φθαρτὸν (G5349) — A-NSN — Nom Sg N
    • τοῦτο (G3778) — D-NSN — Nom Sg N
    • ἐνδύσασθαι (G1746) — V-AMN — Aor Mid Inf
    • ἀφθαρσίαν (G861) — N-ASF — Acc Sg F
    • καὶ (G2532) — CONJ — τὸ ὁ G3588 [T-NSN] Nom Sg N
    • θνητὸν (G2349) — A-NSN — Nom Sg N

    Notes: Endyo in Paul always means acquisition: put on Christ (Rom 13:14, Gal 3:27), put on the new man (Eph 4:24, Col 3:10), put on armor (Eph 6:11), put on virtues (Col 3:12). Never does endyo describe unveiling or revealing something already present. In 1 Cor 15:53-54, when the mortal must 'put on' (endyo) immortality, the consistent Pauline usage demands the acquisition reading — immortality is received, not revealed.

    Equal unto the angels; neither can they die any more — immortality as resurrection quality¶

    But they which shall be accounted worthy to obtain that world, and the resurrection from the dead, neither marry, nor are given in marriage: Neither can they die any more: for they are equal unto the angels; and are the children of God, being the children of the resurrection.

    Context: Jesus' response to the Sadducees about resurrection. Those who attain the resurrection can no longer die — immortality is a quality of the resurrected state.

    Notes: Oute apothanein eti dynantai (neither can they die any more). The inability to die (immortality) is a quality of the resurrection state — not of the present human condition. They become 'equal to angels' and 'children of God' through being 'children of the resurrection.' Without resurrection, they CAN die.

    Creation delivered from bondage of corruption — phthora as bondage¶

    Because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God.

    Context: Paul's cosmic eschatology. All creation groans under the bondage of corruption (phthora) and will be delivered at the revelation of God's children.

    Notes: Douleias tes phthoras (bondage of corruption, G5356). Phthora here describes the universal condition of decay and mortality afflicting all creation since the fall. Deliverance from corruption parallels the resurrection transformation from mortal to immortal.

    Christ's body did not see corruption — diaphthora in messianic resurrection proof¶

    Because thou wilt not leave my soul in hell, neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption. He seeing this before spake of the resurrection of Christ, that his soul was not left in hell, neither his flesh did see corruption.

    Context: Peter's Pentecost sermon. David prophesied (Ps 16:10) that the Messiah's body would not undergo decay (diaphthora). Applied to Christ's resurrection.

    Notes: Diaphthoran (G1312, corruption/decay) — Christ's body was preserved from decomposition because he was raised. David's body DID see corruption (Acts 13:36). The resurrection prevented physical decay. Diaphthora is used 6 times, all in Acts 2 and 13, all in the context of messianic resurrection proof.

    David saw corruption; Christ did not — diaphthora continued¶

    And as concerning that he raised him up from the dead, [now] no more to return to corruption, he said on this wise, I will give you the sure mercies of David. Wherefore he saith also in another [psalm], Thou shalt not suffer thine Holy One to see corruption. For David, after he had served his own generation by the will of God, fell on sleep, and was laid unto his fathers, and saw corruption: But he, whom God raised again, saw no corruption.

    Context: Paul's sermon at Pisidian Antioch. Same argument as Peter's in Acts 2. David fell asleep (ekoimēthē) and saw corruption; Christ was raised and did not see corruption.

    Notes: Diaphthoran (G1312) used 4 times in vv.34-37. David ekoimēthē (fell asleep, aorist passive of koimao G2837) — death described as sleep. David's body decayed; Christ's was raised before decay. The contrast proves resurrection is bodily, not merely spiritual. Note the death-as-sleep vocabulary.

    Christ destroyed him who had the power of death — death as enemy defeated¶

    Forasmuch then as the children are partakers of flesh and blood, he also himself likewise took part of the same; that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil; And deliver them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage.

    Context: Christ's incarnation purpose. He shared human nature (flesh and blood) to destroy the devil's power of death and deliver those enslaved by death-fear.

    Notes: Katargesei (render inoperative, aorist active subjunctive of katargeo G2673) + ton to kratos echonta tou thanatou (him having the power of death). Death is a power to be destroyed, not a friend. Christ took on flesh and blood (mortal nature) to defeat death through death. If humans had immortal souls, death-fear bondage would be irrational — they would know they could not truly die.

    The dead in Christ shall rise first — resurrection sequence¶

    But I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope. For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so them also which sleep in Jesus will God bring with him. For this we say unto you by the word of the Lord, that we which are alive [and] remain unto the coming of the Lord shall not prevent them which are asleep. For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first:

    Context: Paul comforts the Thessalonians about dead believers. They are 'asleep' — the resurrection at Christ's coming is the hope, not present conscious existence in heaven.

    Notes: Koimomenon (those sleeping, present passive participle of koimao G2837) — the dead are sleeping. If they were conscious in heaven, Paul's comfort would be 'they are alive with the Lord' — but his comfort is the coming resurrection. The dead in Christ 'shall rise first' (anastesontai proton) — they are currently dead, not alive in heaven.

    Dust returns to earth, spirit returns to God — dissolution, not immortal soul departing¶

    Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it.

    Context: Qoheleth's description of death. The body (dust) returns to earth; the spirit (ruach) returns to God who gave it.

    Notes: Weharuach tashuv el-ha'elohim asher netanah (and the spirit returns to God who gave it). The ruach (spirit/breath) returns to God — this is the life-force, not a conscious immortal soul. God gave the ruach (Gen 2:7); at death it returns. This reverses the creation process: dust + breath = living soul; death reverses it.

    Death and hell cast into the lake of fire — the second death¶

    And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death.

    Context: After the final judgment. Death and hades are destroyed — cast into the lake of fire, which IS the second death.

    Notes: Ho thanatos kai ho hades (death and hades) are personified and destroyed. 'This is the second death' (houtos ho thanatos ho deuteros estin) — the lake of fire is identified as a second death, not as eternal living in torment.

    No more death — death abolished in the new creation¶

    And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away.

    Context: The new heaven and new earth. Death is abolished — it will no longer exist. This is the fulfillment of Isa 25:8 and 1 Cor 15:26.

    Notes: Ho thanatos ouk estai eti (death shall not be any more). Death is abolished only in the new creation — not in the present age. Immortality is a future state, not a present possession.

    Right to the tree of life — conditional access¶

    Blessed [are] they that do his commandments, that they may have right to the tree of life, and may enter in through the gates into the city.

    Context: Epilogue of Revelation. Access to the tree of life is conditional on obedience — echoing Gen 3:22-24.

    Notes: Hina estai he exousia auton epi to xylon tes zoes (that their authority/right may be upon the tree of life). Conditional access: those who do his commandments. The tree of life represents ongoing immortality from an external source, not inherent in human nature.

    I am alive for evermore — Christ holds the keys of death and hades¶

    [I am] he that liveth, and was dead; and, behold, I am alive for evermore, Amen; and have the keys of hell and of death.

    Context: Christ's self-identification in the opening vision. He died and is now alive forever, holding authority (keys) over death and hades.

    Notes: Zon (living, present active participle of zao G2198) — Christ possesses life. Egenomen nekros (I became dead) — Christ truly died. Zon eimi eis tous aionas ton aionon (alive I am unto the ages of the ages) — Christ now possesses everlasting life. Christ holds the keys (authority) of death and hades — death is under his dominion.

    Athenian philosophers mocked resurrection — Greek immortal-soul believers rejected apostolic message¶

    Then certain philosophers of the Epicureans, and of the Stoicks, encountered him. And some said, What will this babbler say? other some, He seemeth to be a setter forth of strange gods: because he preached unto them Jesus, and the resurrection. And they took him, and brought him unto Areopagus, saying, May we know what this new doctrine, whereof thou speakest, [is]? For thou bringest certain strange things to our ears: we would know therefore what these things mean. (For all the Athenians and strangers which were there spent their time in nothing else, but either to tell, or to hear some new thing.) Then Paul stood in the midst of Mars' hill, and said, [Ye] men of Athens, I perceive that in all things ye are too superstitious. For as I passed by, and beheld your devotions, I found an altar with this inscription, TO THE UNKNOWN GOD. Whom therefore ye ignorantly worship, him declare I unto you. God that made the world and all things therein, seeing that he is Lord of heaven and earth, dwelleth not in temples made with hands; Neither is worshipped with men's hands, as though he needed any thing, seeing he giveth to all life, and breath, and all things; And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth, and hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation; That they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not far from every one of us: For in him we live, and move, and have our being; as certain also of your own poets have said, For we are also his offspring. Forasmuch then as we are the offspring of God, we ought not to think that the Godhead is like unto gold, or silver, or stone, graven by art and man's device. And the times of this ignorance God winked at; but now commandeth all men every where to repent: Because he hath appointed a day, in the which he will judge the world in righteousness by [that] man whom he hath ordained; [whereof] he hath given assurance unto all [men], in that he hath raised him from the dead. And when they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some mocked: and others said, We will hear thee again of this [matter].

    Context: Paul's sermon at the Areopagus. Greek philosophers (Epicurean and Stoic) — who already accepted some form of soul immortality — mocked the resurrection. The apostolic message was resurrection-centered, not inherent-immortality-centered.

    Notes: The Athenians mocked specifically when they heard of 'the resurrection of the dead' (v.32). Philosophers who already believed in soul immortality found bodily resurrection absurd — confirming that the Christian message was NOT soul immortality but resurrection of the whole person.

    The dead praise not the LORD — dead are silent¶

    The dead praise not the LORD, neither any that go down into silence.

    Context: Psalm contrasting the living who bless God with the silent dead. The dead are in silence — no conscious activity.

    Notes: Lo' hammetim yehallu-Yah — the dead do not praise Yah. If the dead had conscious immortal souls in God's presence, they would certainly praise him. Their silence indicates unconsciousness.

    Thou shalt surely die — death as penalty for sin¶

    But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

    Context: God's command and warning to Adam. The penalty for eating the forbidden fruit is death — moth tamuth (dying you shall die), emphatic construction.

    Notes: Moth tamuth (dying you shall die) — infinitive absolute + imperfect of muth (H4191), the strongest possible expression of certainty. Death was introduced as the consequence of sin, not as the natural state. If Adam had an immortal soul, the threat of death would be empty.

    No remembrance of God in death — cessation of relationship¶

    For in death [there is] no remembrance of thee: in the grave who shall give thee thanks?

    Context: David's plea for deliverance from death. Death is characterized by absence of memory and thanksgiving — no conscious activity.

    Notes: Ki ein bammaveth zikhrekha (for there is no remembrance of thee in death). If the dead had conscious immortal souls, they would remember and thank God. David's argument for deliverance depends on death being a state of unconsciousness.

    Not leave my soul in sheol — messianic resurrection hope¶

    For thou wilt not leave my soul in hell; neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption. Thou wilt shew me the path of life: in thy presence [is] fulness of joy; at thy right hand [there are] pleasures for evermore.

    Context: Messianic psalm quoted by Peter in Acts 2:27,31. The psalmist's hope is not disembodied survival but resurrection — not being left in sheol, not seeing corruption.

    Notes: Lo'-ta'azob napshi lish'ol (you will not abandon my soul to sheol). The hope is resurrection from sheol, not escape of an immortal soul FROM sheol. Quoted in Acts 2:27,31 as proof of Christ's bodily resurrection — his flesh did not see corruption (diaphthora).

    Man lieth down and riseth not till heavens be no more — resurrection hope¶

    So man lieth down, and riseth not: till the heavens [be] no more, they shall not awake, nor be raised out of their sleep. O that thou wouldest hide me in the grave, that thou wouldest keep me secret, until thy wrath be past, that thou wouldest appoint me a set time, and remember me! If a man die, shall he live [again]? all the days of my appointed time will I wait, till my change come. Thou shalt call, and I will answer thee: thou wilt have a desire to the work of thine hands.

    Context: Job's meditation on death and the hope of future restoration. Death is sleep from which there is no waking until the appointed time. Job asks the fundamental question: 'If a man die, shall he live again?'

    Notes: V.12: lo'-yaqitsu welo'-ye'oru mishenatam (they shall not awake nor be raised from their sleep) — death is sleep, unconsciousness. V.14: im-yamuth geber hayichyeh (if a man die, shall he live?) — the question presupposes that death is real, not just bodily while the soul lives on. V.15: tiqra' wa'anokhi e'enekka (you shall call and I will answer) — the hope is future resurrection at God's call, not present disembodied consciousness.

    In my flesh shall I see God — bodily resurrection hope¶

    For I know [that] my redeemer liveth, and [that] he shall stand at the latter [day] upon the earth: And [though] after my skin [worms] destroy this [body], yet in my flesh shall I see God: Whom I shall see for myself, and mine eyes shall behold, and not another; [though] my reins be consumed within me.

    Context: Job's declaration of faith in a future redeemer. His hope is to see God 'in my flesh' — bodily resurrection, not disembodied immortality.

    Notes: Umibbesari echezeh Eloah (and from my flesh I shall behold God). Job's hope is explicitly embodied — he expects to see God in/from his flesh, with his own eyes. This is resurrection hope, not the hope of a disembodied immortal soul.

    Ransom from the power of the grave — redemption from death¶

    I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death: O death, I will be thy plagues; O grave, I will be thy destruction: repentance shall be hid from mine eyes.

    Context: God's promise of redemption from death and sheol. Quoted/alluded to in 1 Cor 15:55. Death and the grave are enemies to be overcome.

    Notes: Miyad she'ol epha'dem mimaveth eg'alem (from the hand of sheol I will ransom them, from death I will redeem them). Ehi devareyikha maveth ehi qatabkha she'ol (where are your plagues, O death? where is your destruction, O sheol?). Death is personified as an enemy from which God will redeem. Paul adapts this in 1 Cor 15:55.

    If by any means I might attain unto the resurrection — resurrection as goal¶

    That I may know him, and the power of his resurrection, and the fellowship of his sufferings, being made conformable unto his death; If by any means I might attain unto the resurrection of the dead.

    Context: Paul's personal aspiration. The resurrection of the dead is the goal — not presupposed as automatic but something to be attained.

    Notes: Ei pos katanteso eis ten exanastasin ten ek nekron (if somehow I may attain to the out-resurrection from the dead). Paul uses exanastasis (G1815) — only here in the NT — a strengthened form of anastasis. If Paul already had an immortal soul guaranteed to survive death, 'attaining' the resurrection would be unnecessary.

    Change our vile body — body transformation at Christ's coming¶

    Who shall change our vile body, that it may be fashioned like unto his glorious body, according to the working whereby he is able even to subdue all things unto himself.

    Context: Paul's expectation of bodily transformation at Christ's return. The body of humiliation (to soma tes tapeinoseos) is changed to conform to Christ's glorious body.

    Notes: Metaschematizei (will transform) + to soma tes tapeinoseos hemon (the body of our humiliation). The transformation is future, at Christ's coming. The hope is bodily transformation, not soul release from the body.

    Made to be taken and destroyed — destruction language for the wicked¶

    But these, as natural brute beasts, made to be taken and destroyed, speak evil of the things that they understand not; and shall utterly perish in their own corruption;

    Context: Peter's condemnation of false teachers. They shall perish (phtheiro) in their own corruption (phthora) — language of destruction, not eternal conscious torment.

    Notes: En te phthora auton kai phtharesontai (in their corruption they also shall be corrupted/destroyed). Phthora (G5356, corruption) and phtheiro (G5351, to corrupt/destroy) from the same root. The wicked perish in their own corruption — the same corruption/decay vocabulary that is contrasted with aphtharsia (incorruption) in the immortality passages.

    Raise him up at the last day — resurrection as the mechanism of eternal life¶

    And this is the Father's will which hath sent me, that of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing, but should raise it up again at the last day. And this is the will of him that sent me, that every one which seeth the Son, and believeth on him, may have everlasting life: and I will raise him up at the last day.

    Context: Jesus' bread of life discourse. Eternal life is connected to being raised 'at the last day' — four times in this discourse (vv.39,40,44,54).

    Notes: Anasteso auto en te eschate hemera (I will raise it/him up at the last day). The mechanism for receiving eternal life is resurrection at the last day — not immediate possession of an immortal soul. Jesus repeats this phrase four times for emphasis.

    I am the resurrection and the life — life inseparable from Christ and resurrection¶

    Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live: And whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?

    Context: Jesus' declaration to Martha before raising Lazarus. Life is identified with Christ himself AND with resurrection — not as an inherent human quality.

    Notes: Ego eimi he anastasis kai he zoe (I am the resurrection and the life). Jesus does not say 'I am the immortality of the soul' but 'I am the resurrection and the life.' Life and resurrection are inseparable — life comes through resurrection, not through an inherent immortal soul. The believer who dies 'shall live' (zesetai, future) — through resurrection.

    David's flesh rested in hope — messianic resurrection, not disembodied soul survival¶

    Therefore did my heart rejoice, and my tongue was glad; moreover also my flesh shall rest in hope: Because thou wilt not leave my soul in hell, neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption. Thou hast made known to me the ways of life; thou shalt make me full of joy with thy countenance. Men [and] brethren, let me freely speak unto you of the patriarch David, that he is both dead and buried, and his sepulchre is with us unto this day. Therefore being a prophet, and knowing that God had sworn with an oath to him, that of the fruit of his loins, according to the flesh, he would raise up Christ to sit on his throne; He seeing this before spake of the resurrection of Christ, that his soul was not left in hell, neither his flesh did see corruption.

    Context: Peter's Pentecost sermon quoting Psalm 16. David is 'both dead and buried' (v.29) — not alive in heaven. The prophecy is about Christ's resurrection, not David's present conscious survival.

    Notes: Peter explicitly states David is 'dead and buried' and his tomb is with them (v.29). David prophesied about Christ's resurrection, not about his own immortal soul. The soul (psyche) was not left in hades, and the flesh did not see corruption (diaphthora) — both soul and body language refers to resurrection, not to disembodied survival.

    O death where is thy sting — victory over death through Christ¶

    O death, where [is] thy sting? O grave, where [is] thy victory? The sting of death [is] sin; and the strength of sin [is] the law. But thanks [be] to God, which giveth us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ.

    Context: Triumphant conclusion of Paul's resurrection argument. Quoting/adapting Hos 13:14. Victory over death comes through Christ, not through inherent immortality.

    Notes: Pou sou, thanate, to kentron? pou sou, hade, to nikos? (where, O death, is your sting? where, O hades, is your victory?). The victory is God's gift through Christ (v.57). Death is mocked ONLY because of the resurrection — without resurrection, death would have the final word (cf. vv.12-19).

    Promise of the life that now is and of that which is to come¶

    For bodily exercise profiteth little: but godliness is profitable unto all things, having promise of the life that now is, and of that which is to come.

    Context: Paul's instruction to Timothy on the value of godliness. Life — both present and future — is a promise (epangelia), not an inherent possession.

    Notes: Epangelian echousa zoes tes nyn kai tes mellouses (having promise of life, the present and the coming). Life is described as a promise — both present and future. The 'life to come' is not presupposed as already possessed through an immortal soul.

    God will redeem my soul from the power of sheol¶

    None [of them] can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him: (For the redemption of their soul [is] precious, and it ceaseth for ever:) That he should still live for ever, [and] not see corruption. Like sheep they are laid in the grave; death shall feed on them; and the upright shall have dominion over them in the morning; and their beauty shall consume in the grave from their dwelling. But God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave: for he shall receive me. Selah.

    Context: Psalm contrasting human inability to redeem from death with God's power. No human can pay a ransom to avoid death (vv.7-9), but God will redeem the psalmist's soul from sheol's power (v.15).

    Notes: V.7-9: No human can ransom another or pay God enough to live forever. V.14: Death feeds on the wicked — they are consumed. V.15: Ak-Elohim yiph'deh napshi miyad-she'ol (but God will redeem my soul from the hand/power of sheol). The hope is divine redemption from death, not inherent survival of death.

    Thy dead men shall live — OT resurrection promise¶

    Thy dead [men] shall live, [together with] my dead body shall they arise. Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust: for thy dew [is as] the dew of herbs, and the earth shall cast out the dead.

    Context: Isaiah's eschatological vision. The dead will live, arise, awake from the dust — resurrection language, not disembodied survival.

    Notes: Yichyu metheyikha (your dead shall live) + nevelati yequmun (my dead body shall arise) + haqitsu weranenu shokhenei aphar (awake and sing, dwellers in dust). The dead are in the dust (like Gen 3:19) and will arise from it. This is bodily resurrection, not the continuation of immortal souls.

    The wicked shall be cut off; the wicked shall not be — cessation of existence¶

    For evildoers shall be cut off: but those that wait upon the LORD, they shall inherit the earth. For yet a little while, and the wicked [shall] not [be]: yea, thou shalt diligently consider his place, and it [shall] not [be].

    Context: David's contrast between the righteous and the wicked. The wicked will cease to exist — they 'shall not be' and their place will be empty.

    Notes: We'od me'at we'ein rasha' (yet a little while and there is no wicked one). Wehithbonanta al-meqomo we'einennu (and you consider his place and he is not). The wicked cease to exist — they are 'not.' Their very place is empty. This is cessation, not eternal conscious torment.

    Wicked as stubble, ashes under feet — destruction imagery¶

    For, behold, the day cometh, that shall burn as an oven; and all the proud, yea, and all that do wickedly, shall be stubble: and the day that cometh shall burn them up, saith the LORD of hosts, that it shall leave them neither root nor branch. But unto you that fear my name shall the Sun of righteousness arise with healing in his wings; and ye shall go forth, and grow up as calves of the stall. And ye shall tread down the wicked; for they shall be ashes under the soles of your feet in the day that I shall do [this], saith the LORD of hosts.

    Context: Malachi's eschatological prophecy. The wicked are burned up completely — stubble consumed, leaving neither root nor branch. They become ashes underfoot.

    Notes: V.1: wehayah lahem qash (they shall be stubble) + welibat otam (and it shall burn them up) + lo'-ya'azob lahem shoresh we'anaph (it shall leave them neither root nor branch). V.3: ki-yihyu epher tachath kappoth ragleykhem (for they shall be ashes under the soles of your feet). The imagery is total consumption — the wicked are reduced to ashes. This is incompatible with eternal conscious torment but consistent with conditional immortality.

    Eat and not die — life through Christ's provision, not inherent¶

    This is the bread which cometh down from heaven, that a man may eat thereof, and not die. I am the living bread which came down from heaven: if any man eat of this bread, he shall live for ever: and the bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world.

    Context: Jesus' bread of life discourse continued. Life depends on eating the bread from heaven — an external provision, not an inherent quality.

    Notes: Hina tis ex autou phage kai me apothane (that a man may eat of it and not die). Ean tis phage ek toutou tou artou zesei eis ton aiona (if anyone eat of this bread he shall live forever). Life forever depends on partaking of Christ — it is conditional and externally provided, not inherent in human nature.

    Clothing metaphor — clothed upon, not unclothed¶

    For we know that if our earthly house of this tabernacle were dissolved, we have a building of God, an house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens. For in this we groan, earnestly desiring to be clothed upon with our house which is from heaven: If so be that being clothed we shall not be found naked. For we that are in this tabernacle do groan, being burdened: not for that we would be unclothed, but clothed upon, that mortality might be swallowed up of life. Now he that hath wrought us for the selfsame thing is God, who also hath given unto us the earnest of the Spirit.

    Context: Paul discusses the resurrection body. He does not want disembodiment (unclothed) but transformation (clothed upon). V.5 identifies God as the one who has prepared this transformation and given the Spirit as guarantee.

    Notes: Supporting verse added for compliance. Full range includes v.5 which adds the divine initiative and Spirit-guarantee elements.
