    Conclusion: Who Possesses Immortality?¶

    Question¶

    Who possesses immortality? Is the soul inherently immortal or is immortality a divine gift? Examine athanasia (G110), aphtharsia (G861), thnetos (G2349).

    Summary Answer¶

    Scripture attributes immortality exclusively to God as an inherent possession (1 Tim 6:16) and presents it as a gift believers receive at the resurrection (1 Cor 15:53-54). The human soul is never described as immortal in any biblical text. The phrase "immortal soul" does not appear in Scripture. The three core vocabulary terms — athanasia (G110, 3 occurrences), aphtharsia (G861, 8 occurrences), and thnetos (G2349, 6 occurrences) — together paint a consistent picture: God alone has deathlessness, humans are mortal, and the transformation from mortality to immortality occurs at the resurrection through Christ.

    Key Verses¶

    God Alone Has Immortality (1 Timothy 6:16) Who only hath immortality, dwelling in the light which no man can approach unto; whom no man hath seen, nor can see: to whom be honour and power everlasting. Amen.

    This Mortal Must Put On Immortality (1 Corinthians 15:53-54) For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality. So when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality, then shall be brought to pass the saying that is written, Death is swallowed up in victory.

    The Soul That Sins Shall Die (Ezekiel 18:4) Behold, all souls are mine; as the soul of the father, so also the soul of the son is mine: the soul that sinneth, it shall die.

    God Can Destroy Both Soul and Body (Matthew 10:28) And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell.

    Wages of Sin Is Death; Gift of God Is Eternal Life (Romans 6:23) For the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.

    Perish or Have Everlasting Life (John 3:16) For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life.

    He That Hath Not the Son Hath Not Life (1 John 5:11-12) And this is the record, that God hath given to us eternal life, and this life is in his Son. He that hath the Son hath life; and he that hath not the Son of God hath not life.

    Man Became a Living Soul (Genesis 2:7) And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.

    Man Barred from the Tree of Life (Genesis 3:22-24) And the LORD God said, Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil: and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever: Therefore the LORD God sent him forth from the garden of Eden.

    The Dead Know Not Anything (Ecclesiastes 9:5) For the living know that they shall die: but the dead know not any thing, neither have they any more a reward; for the memory of them is forgotten.

    Analysis¶

    The Three Core Terms: What Athanasia, Aphtharsia, and Thnetos Reveal¶

    The NT uses three related but distinct terms for the mortality-immortality concept. Their distribution tells a story.

    Athanasia (G110) — "deathlessness" — appears only 3 times in the entire NT, all in the Pauline epistles: 1 Timothy 6:16 and 1 Corinthians 15:53-54. In 1 Timothy 6:16, God alone (monos) has athanasia. The Greek parser confirms the construction: monos (G3441, Nom Sg M, adjective) + echon (G2192, Present Active Participle, Nom Sg M — "having") + athanasiān (G110, Acc Sg F — "immortality"). The present active participle echon indicates ongoing, continuous possession. Monos functions as an exclusive attributive: "the only one having immortality." In the other two occurrences (1 Cor 15:53-54), athanasia is the accusative direct object of the verb endyo (G1746, "to put on, clothe oneself with") — something the mortal must acquire at the resurrection.

    The word athanasia is absent from the Gospels, the General Epistles, and Revelation. It is never applied to the human soul. It is never predicated of any creature as a present possession. It occurs exclusively in two contexts: God's exclusive attribute (1 Tim 6:16) and the resurrection transformation (1 Cor 15:53-54).

    Aphtharsia (G861) — "incorruptibility, un-decay" — appears 8 times across 5 books, all in the Pauline corpus. Four occurrences describe the resurrection transformation (1 Cor 15:42, 50, 53, 54): the body sown in corruption (phthora) is raised in incorruption (aphtharsia). Two describe immortality as an object of aspiration or revelation: Romans 2:7 (those who seek [zeteo, Present Active Participle] aphtharsia) and 2 Timothy 1:10 (Christ brought [photizo, Aorist Active Participle] life and aphtharsia to light). Two are ethical metaphors: Ephesians 6:24 (love in sincerity/incorruption) and Titus 2:7 (doctrine showing uncorruptness). The KJV translates aphtharsia as "incorruption" (4x), "immortality" (2x), and "sincerity" (2x).

    In Romans 2:7, the grammar is decisive. The parser shows zetousin (G2212, Present Active Participle, Dat Pl M — "seeking") with aphtharsian (G861, Acc Sg F) as its object. People seek what they do not possess. If immortality were inherent, seeking it would be a category error.

    Thnetos (G2349) — "mortal, liable to die" — appears 6 times in 3 books, exclusively Pauline. It describes the present human condition: the mortal body under sin's dominion (Rom 6:12), the mortal body awaiting quickening (Rom 8:11, where zoopoiesei [G2227] is Future Active Indicative — the vivification is future), life manifested in mortal flesh (2 Cor 4:11), mortality swallowed up by life (2 Cor 5:4), and the mortal putting on immortality (1 Cor 15:53-54).

    In 1 Corinthians 15:53, thneton is used substantivally — "this mortal" (touto to thneton) — not restricted to "this mortal body." Paul does not name an immortal counterpart to the mortal human. The LXX maps thnetos to adam (H120, "man/human being"), confirming that human mortality is the core concept.

    The Aphthartos Adjective: God's Nature vs. Believers' Future¶

    The adjective aphthartos (G862, "incorruptible") appears 7 times in 4 books. Two describe God: Romans 1:23 (the incorruptible God vs. corruptible man — a Creator/creature contrast) and 1 Timothy 1:17 (God described as aphthartos alongside monos, "only" — paralleling 1 Tim 6:16). One describes the resurrection body: 1 Corinthians 15:52 (the dead shall be raised incorruptible — aphthartoi [Nom Pl M] as a predicate describing what the dead become). Four describe future rewards or spiritual qualities: an incorruptible crown (1 Cor 9:25), an incorruptible inheritance reserved in heaven (1 Pet 1:4), incorruptible seed — the word of God (1 Pet 1:23), and the incorruptible ornament of a meek spirit (1 Pet 3:4).

    The pattern is consistent: God has incorruptibility as an inherent attribute. Believers receive incorruptible things in the future or through spiritual transformation. The human soul as such is never called aphthartos.

    The Corruption Word Family: What Humans Presently Are¶

    The phtheiro word family forms the negative counterpart. Phthora (G5356, "corruption/decay") appears 9 times: creation groans under bondage to corruption (Rom 8:21), the body is sown in corruption (1 Cor 15:42), corruption cannot inherit incorruption (1 Cor 15:50), sowing to the flesh reaps corruption (Gal 6:8), corruption in the world through lust (2 Pet 1:4), the wicked perish in their own corruption (2 Pet 2:12). Phthartos (G5349, "corruptible") appears 6 times: corruptible man vs. incorruptible God (Rom 1:23), corruptible crown vs. incorruptible (1 Cor 9:25), this corruptible must put on incorruption (1 Cor 15:53-54), not redeemed with corruptible things (1 Pet 1:18), corruptible seed vs. incorruptible (1 Pet 1:23).

    The picture is clear: humans are presently corruptible and mortal (phthartos, thnetos). They must become incorruptible and immortal (aphthartos, athanatos). The transformation happens at the resurrection. No text suggests that part of the human being (the soul) is already on the immortal/incorruptible side of the divide.

    The Monos Qualifier: Does "Only" Admit Qualification?¶

    In 1 Timothy 6:16, the word monos (G3441, "only/alone") establishes that God alone has immortality. The ECT position requires interpreting this as "God alone has self-existent immortality" — adding a derived/underived distinction the text does not contain.

    The word study for monos (47 NT occurrences) shows that in doxological contexts, monos marks unqualified divine exclusivity: "the only true God" (John 17:3), "the only wise God" (1 Tim 1:17, Jude 25), "thou only art holy" (Rev 15:4). In each case, monos admits no qualification. No one argues that John 17:3 means "the only self-existently true God" as opposed to "derivedly true" creatures. No one argues that Jude 25 means "the only self-existently wise God" while allowing for "derivedly wise" beings. Monos means "only" — it excludes all others from the predicate.

    The translation relationship study (TR-bad-monos.md) confirms that the Hebrew equivalent bad functions the same way: unqualified exclusivity. Adding "self-existent vs. derived" to 1 Timothy 6:16 requires importing a concept not found in the text or in any parallel use of monos.

    The Endyo Metaphor: Acquisition, Not Unveiling¶

    The verb endyo (G1746) appears approximately 31 times in the NT. In 1 Corinthians 15:53-54, the mortal must "put on" (endyo) immortality. The parser confirms: endysasthai (Aorist Middle Infinitive — "to clothe oneself with") in v.53 and endysetai (Aorist Middle Subjunctive) in v.54.

    Every Pauline use of endyo describes acquiring something not previously worn: put on Christ (Rom 13:14, Gal 3:27), put on the new man (Eph 4:24, Col 3:10), put on the whole armor of God (Eph 6:11), put on compassion, kindness, humility (Col 3:12). In no case does endyo mean "reveal" or "uncover" something already present. The clothing metaphor consistently describes acquisition.

    The related verb ependyomai (G1902, "to clothe upon") in 2 Corinthians 5:2, 4 describes super-addition — putting on the resurrection body over the mortal body. Paul explicitly contrasts this with ekdyo (G1562, "to unclothe") — he does not want death/disembodiment but transformation. This confirms that Paul's anthropology is holistic: the hope is not soul-release from the body but the whole person transformed from mortal to immortal.

    The Soul: Never Called Immortal, Can Die¶

    Nephesh (H5315) appears 788 times in the OT. Psyche (G5590) appears 105 times in the NT. Neither word is ever modified by "immortal" in any biblical text. The phrase "immortal soul" does not appear in Scripture.

    What Scripture does say about the soul: - The soul can die: "The soul that sinneth, it shall die" (Ezek 18:4, 20). The Hebrew parsing confirms: hannephesh (the soul) is the grammatical subject of tamut (shall die, Qal Imperfect 3fs of muth). - The soul can be destroyed: "Fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in gehenna" (Matt 10:28). The Greek parsing confirms: apolesai (Aorist Active Infinitive of apollymi — "to destroy") with kai psychen kai soma (both soul and body) as direct objects. - The soul can be a corpse: Leviticus 21:11 and Numbers 6:6 use nephesh met ("dead soul") for a dead body. - Animals are also called nephesh chayyah (Gen 1:20-21, 24) — the same term applied to man in Genesis 2:7.

    The creation formula of Genesis 2:7 is foundational. The Hebrew parsing shows: wayhi (Qal Wayyiqtol of hayah — "and he became") + ha'adam (the man) + lenephesh chayyah (into a living soul). Man became a nephesh chayyah — the whole person IS the living soul. Man did not receive a soul as a separate component; man became a soul. The same phrase describes animals (Gen 1:20-21), establishing that nephesh chayyah is a description of animate living beings, not a unique immortal human component.

    The Tree of Life: Immortality Required an External Source¶

    Genesis 3:22-24 records God's action after the fall: "lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live forever." The Hebrew parsing of v.22 confirms a purpose clause of prevention: pen (lest) + yishlach (he send forth) + wachay leolam (and live forever). God deliberately prevented Adam from accessing the tree of life. The logic is straightforward: without the tree, man will not live forever. If man already had an immortal soul, barring access to the tree would be pointless — he would live forever regardless.

    The tree of life reappears at the end of Scripture. Revelation 2:7 promises access to the tree of life to those who overcome — conditional access. Revelation 22:14 places the tree of life in the new creation with conditional access for those who do his commandments. From Genesis to Revelation, the tree of life represents an external divine source of continued existence — immortality is not inherent in human nature but must be received from outside.

    Eternal Life as Gift: Convergent Testimony from Multiple Authors¶

    The pattern across independent biblical authors is remarkably consistent:

    Paul: The wages of sin is death, but the gift (charisma) of God is eternal life (Rom 6:23). Humans seek (zeteo) immortality (Rom 2:7). Eternal life is promised (Tit 1:2) and hoped for (1 Tim 4:8). The mortal must put on immortality at the resurrection (1 Cor 15:53-54).

    John (recording Jesus): Whoever believes should not perish but have everlasting life (John 3:16). Christ gives (didomi) eternal life (John 10:28). The Father gave the Son authority to give eternal life (John 17:2). Life is defined as knowing God and Christ (John 17:3). Christ will raise believers at the last day — repeated four times in John 6 (vv.39, 40, 44, 54).

    John (in his epistle): God gave (edoken) eternal life; this life is in his Son. Without the Son, a person does not have life (1 John 5:11-12). Eternal life is a promise (1 John 2:25).

    No passage in the NT describes eternal life as an inherent human possession apart from God. The consistent testimony is: life comes from God, is located in Christ, is given as a gift, is promised as a future inheritance, is hoped for, is sought, and is received at the resurrection.

    Death as Sleep and Unconsciousness¶

    Multiple OT passages describe the state of the dead as unconsciousness: the dead know nothing (Ecc 9:5), there is no work or knowledge in sheol (Ecc 9:10), thoughts perish at the moment of death (Ps 146:4), the dead do not praise God (Ps 115:17), and there is no remembrance of God in death (Ps 6:5).

    The NT describes death as "sleep" (koimao, G2837): the dead in Christ are "asleep" (1 Thess 4:13-15), David "fell asleep" (Acts 13:36), Stephen "fell asleep" (Acts 7:60), and Paul speaks of those who have "fallen asleep" in Christ (1 Cor 15:6, 18, 51). If the dead had conscious immortal souls in God's presence, calling their state "sleep" would be misleading.

    The force of 1 Corinthians 15:18 is particularly significant. Paul says that if there is no resurrection, "then they also which are fallen asleep in Christ are perished" (apolonto, Aorist Middle Indicative of apollymi — "they have perished"). The parser confirms the parsing. If the dead in Christ had immortal souls already in heaven, Paul could not say they have "perished" without resurrection. His argument only works if death is real cessation, and resurrection is the only hope.

    God as Sustainer of All Life¶

    Job 34:14-15 states: if God were to gather his spirit (ruach) and breath (neshamah) to himself, all flesh would expire (gawa) together and man would return to dust. Psalm 104:29-30 presents the same cycle: God takes away breath, they die and return to dust; God sends forth his spirit, they are created. Acts 17:25-28: God gives all life and breath; in him we live and move and have our being. Hebrews 1:3: Christ upholds all things by the word of his power. Colossians 1:17: by Christ all things hold together.

    These texts teach that no created life is self-sustaining. All life — including human life — depends on God's active, ongoing sustaining. If human souls were inherently immortal, they would continue existing by their own nature regardless of God's sustaining power. But Scripture teaches the opposite: withdraw the breath, and all flesh perishes.

    The Greek Response to Resurrection Preaching¶

    Acts 17:18-32 records that Athenian philosophers — Epicureans and Stoics who already accepted some form of soul immortality — mocked Paul when he preached the resurrection. If the apostolic message had been soul immortality, the Greeks would have recognized it as familiar. Instead, they found bodily resurrection absurd. This confirms that the early Christian message was resurrection-centered, not inherent-immortality-centered.

    Necessary Implications¶

    # Necessary Implication Based on Why Unavoidable

    Word Studies¶

    Athanasia (G110): 3 NT occurrences. Deathlessness. Alpha-privative of thanatos. All in Pauline corpus. Never applied to the human soul. Two contexts only: God's exclusive possession (1 Tim 6:16) and resurrection acquisition (1 Cor 15:53-54).

    Aphtharsia (G861): 8 NT occurrences. Incorruptibility. Alpha-privative of phtheiro. Six physical/eschatological uses, two ethical metaphors. Something sought (Rom 2:7), brought to light by Christ (2 Tim 1:10), put on at resurrection (1 Cor 15:53-54). Never predicated of the human soul.

    Thnetos (G2349): 6 NT occurrences. Mortal/liable to die. Describes the present human condition. Used substantivally in 1 Cor 15:53 — "this mortal," not restricted to "this mortal body." LXX maps to adam (H120) — human mortality is the core concept.

    Aphthartos (G862): 7 NT occurrences. Incorruptible adjective. Applied to God (2x), resurrection body (1x), future rewards/spiritual qualities (4x). Never applied to the human soul as a present possession.

    Endyo (G1746): Approximately 31 NT occurrences. "Put on, clothe oneself with." Every Pauline use describes acquisition of something not previously worn. In 1 Cor 15:53-54, the verb demands the reading that immortality is received, not revealed.

    Monos (G3441): 47 NT occurrences. In doxological contexts, marks unqualified divine exclusivity. Adding "self-existent vs. derived" to 1 Tim 6:16 has no textual basis.

    Difficult Passages¶

    1 Peter 3:4 — The incorruptible ornament of a meek spirit. The closest any text comes to applying aphthartos to something inside a human. However, the referent is a moral quality (meekness and quietness), not the soul as an entity. Peter contrasts external adornment with internal character. This is a description of character quality, not an ontological claim about the soul's nature.

    Ecclesiastes 3:21 — "Who knows the spirit of man that goes upward?" A rhetorical question, not a doctrinal assertion. In context (3:19-20), Qoheleth has just stated that humans and animals share the same fate — both die, both return to dust. The question introduces uncertainty about human claims to know what happens after death, not certainty about an immortal soul ascending.

    2 Corinthians 5:6-8 — "Absent from the body, present with the Lord." In context, Paul has just said he does NOT want to be "unclothed" (dead/disembodied, v.4) but "clothed upon" (transformed). His preference is resurrection, not death. The statement in v.8 expresses confidence that separation from the body will ultimately result in presence with the Lord — through resurrection, not through an immortal soul. Paul uses thnetos (mortal, v.4) and the clothing metaphor here, consistent with his teaching in 1 Corinthians 15:53-54.

    Luke 20:38 — "He is not the God of the dead, but of the living; for all live unto him." Jesus argues for the resurrection (v.35-36), not for disembodied survival. His point is that God's covenant with the patriarchs guarantees their future resurrection. They "live unto God" in the sense that God holds them for resurrection. This fits the context (the Sadducees denied resurrection) and is consistent with Acts 2:29, 34 where Peter says David is "both dead and buried" and has not ascended to heaven.

    What CAN Be Said (Scripture explicitly states or necessarily implies)¶

    • God alone (monos) has immortality — athanasia is an exclusive divine attribute (1 Tim 6:16)
    • Humans are presently mortal (thnetos) — all 6 NT uses describe the present human condition
    • Immortality must be "put on" (endyo) at the resurrection — the verb consistently means acquisition (1 Cor 15:53-54)
    • Immortality is sought by believers (Rom 2:7) — seeking implies non-possession
    • Christ brought life and immortality to light through the gospel (2 Tim 1:10)
    • The soul (nephesh) that sins shall die (Ezek 18:4, 20) — the soul is the subject of dying
    • God is able to destroy both soul (psyche) and body in gehenna (Matt 10:28)
    • Man became a living soul (nephesh chayyah) — the same term used for animals (Gen 2:7, Gen 1:20-21)
    • Man was barred from the tree of life to prevent living forever (Gen 3:22-24)
    • Eternal life is a gift (Rom 6:23), a promise (Tit 1:2, 1 John 2:25), given by Christ (John 10:28), and located in the Son — without the Son, no life (1 John 5:11-12)
    • The dead know nothing; their thoughts perish; they do not praise God (Ecc 9:5, Ps 146:4, Ps 115:17)
    • All life depends on God's sustaining breath — withdraw it and all flesh perishes (Job 34:14-15, Ps 104:29)
    • Without resurrection, even the dead in Christ have perished (1 Cor 15:18)
    • The phrase "immortal soul" does not appear anywhere in Scripture

    What CANNOT Be Said (not explicitly stated or necessarily implied by Scripture)¶

    • That the human soul is inherently immortal — no text states this; multiple texts contradict it
    • That "only" (monos) in 1 Tim 6:16 means "only self-existently" — the text contains no self-existent/derived distinction
    • That thnetos (mortal) applies only to the body while the soul is exempt — Paul never names an immortal counterpart to the mortal body
    • That nephesh/psyche dying or being destroyed refers only to spiritual death — the grammar in Ezek 18:4 and Matt 10:28 uses standard death/destruction vocabulary
    • That "put on" (endyo) immortality means "reveal" immortality already present — endyo consistently means acquisition across all Pauline usage
    • That the dead are conscious — Scripture consistently describes death as unconsciousness and sleep
    • That the tree of life was unnecessary because humans already had immortality — Gen 3:22-24 presupposes that without the tree, man does not live forever
    • That apollymi (perish) means eternal conscious existence — no text defines it this way in an eschatological context

    Conclusion¶

    The biblical vocabulary of immortality is precise and consistent. Three terms — athanasia (deathlessness), aphtharsia (incorruptibility), and thnetos (mortality) — together establish a framework in which God alone possesses immortality as an inherent attribute, humans are presently mortal, and the transformation from mortality to immortality occurs at the resurrection through Christ.

    Twenty explicit statements in this study point in the conditionalist direction — that immortality is not inherent in human nature but is a gift received through Christ at the resurrection. Zero explicit statements point in the ECT direction. Fourteen explicit statements are neutral observations that both sides accept. The two I-B inferences (concerning the meaning of apollymi) are both resolved Strong toward the conditionalist reading, because multiple Plain statements from independent authors consistently use destruction/cessation vocabulary while no Plain statement supports the ongoing-ruin reading.

    The two ECT-direction inferences (I2 and I3) are classified I-C (Compatible External) — they import concepts not found in the text (self-existent vs. derived immortality; soul-exemption from mortality) but do not contradict any specific E-item. The strongest ECT-direction claim (I4 — the human soul is inherently immortal) is classified I-D (Counter-Evidence External) because it requires overriding multiple explicit statements: God alone has immortality (E1), the soul that sins dies (E7), God can destroy the soul (E8).

    The evidence from athanasia, aphtharsia, and thnetos, combined with the soul mortality passages, the gift/promise/acquisition pattern, the death-as-unconsciousness passages, and the tree-of-life narrative, converges on a single conclusion: immortality is not an inherent human possession. It is a divine attribute that God offers to believers as a gift, received at the resurrection through Christ.
