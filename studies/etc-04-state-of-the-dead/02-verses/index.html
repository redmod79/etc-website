    Verses¶

    Question¶

    Condition of the dead -- conscious or unconscious? What happens between death and resurrection? Examine ALL Old Testament death-state passages (the dead know nothing, thoughts perish, no praise, silence, forgetfulness, sleep), ALL New Testament death-as-sleep passages, and ALL alleged conscious intermediate state passages (2 Cor 5:1-9, Phil 1:23, Luke 23:43, Luke 16:19-31, Rev 6:9-11, 1 Sam 28:3-20, Matt 17:1-9, 1 Pet 3:18-20). Examine the Hebrew and Greek 'sleep' vocabulary (shakab H7901, yashen H3462, koimao G2837, katheudo G2518) and 'rest/waiting' vocabulary. Investigate sheol (H7585) and hades (G86) as the abode of the dead.

    Thoughts perish at death — explicit cessation of consciousness¶

    His breath goeth forth, he returneth to his earth; in that very day his thoughts perish.

    Context: A psalm of praise. After warning against trusting in princes (v.3), the psalmist states what happens when a human dies — breath departs, body returns to earth, thoughts perish on that very day.

    Series of rhetorical questions expecting 'no' — death as forgetfulness and darkness¶

    Wilt thou shew wonders to the dead? shall the dead arise [and] praise thee? Selah. Shall thy lovingkindness be declared in the grave? [or] thy faithfulness in destruction? Shall thy wonders be known in the dark? and thy righteousness in the land of forgetfulness?

    Context: Psalm 88, attributed to Heman the Ezrahite, is the darkest psalm in the Psalter. Six rhetorical questions in vv.10-12 all expect 'no' as the answer. The dead cannot experience wonders, praise, lovingkindness, faithfulness, or righteousness.

    Notes: Hebrew parsing of v.11 (MT numbering): halammetim (interrogative + to-the-dead) taaseh (will-you-do) pele (wonder)? im-refaim (if-shades/ghosts) yaqumu (will-arise) yodukha (praise-you)? Selah. Four locations named: qeber (grave), abaddon (destruction/perdition), choshek (darkness), eretz neshiyah (land of forgetfulness). Each is characterized by the absence of God's activity and human response. The term refaim (shades) denotes the dead as powerless, not as conscious spirits.

    Man lies down and rises not till heavens be no more¶

All they shall speak and say unto thee, Art thou also become weak as we? art thou become like unto us? Thy pomp is brought down to the grave, [and] the noise of thy viols: the worm is spread under thee, and the worms cover thee.

    Context: Isaiah's taunt-song (mashal) against the king of Babylon. Sheol is personified as stirring up the dead (refaim) to meet him. This is widely recognized as poetic personification — the same literary device used when trees clap hands (Isa 55:12) and mountains sing (Isa 44:23).

    Notes: Hebrew: sheol mittachat ragezah lekha (sheol beneath is stirred for you). The refaim (shades/dead) are 'stirred up' — but the genre is mashal (v.4, taunt/proverb). V.11: tola (worm) spread under, rimmah (maggots) cover — the reality behind the personification is decomposition. The dead 'speak' in the same way Abel's blood 'cries' (Gen 4:10) — forensic/poetic personification, not literal consciousness.

    They shall sleep a perpetual sleep and not wake¶

    In their heat I will make their feasts, and I will make them drunken, that they may rejoice, and sleep a perpetual sleep, and not wake, saith the LORD.

    Context: God's judgment on Babylon. The Babylonians will sleep a perpetual sleep (shenath olam) and not awake. Death is described as sleep from which there is no awakening — apart from divine intervention.

    Notes: Hebrew: veyashnu shenat-olam (and they shall sleep a sleep of eternity) velo yaqitsu (and not awake). Uses yashen (H3462, to sleep) — the death-sleep word also used in Dan 12:2. The perpetual sleep (shenath olam) describes death as unbroken unconsciousness. The negation 'not awake' reinforces the point — without divine resurrection, the dead remain in this sleep state.

    Thou shalt rest, and stand in thy lot at the end of the days¶

    But go thou thy way till the end [be]: for thou shalt rest, and stand in thy lot at the end of the days.

    Context: The angel's final words to Daniel. Two phases: (1) rest (nuwach) — death as rest/cessation, and (2) stand in thy lot — resurrection at the end of days. Death is rest; resurrection is standing.

    Notes: Hebrew: tanuwach (you shall rest, from nuwach H5117) vetaamod legoralekha leqets hayyamim (and you shall stand in your lot at the end of the days). The sequence is explicit: death = rest, resurrection = standing at the end. Daniel will not be conscious between death and resurrection — he rests. The reward comes 'at the end of the days,' not at death.

    Thou shalt sleep with thy fathers — OT sleep-death formula (shakab)¶

    And the LORD said unto Moses, Behold, thou shalt sleep with thy fathers; and this people will rise up, and go a whoring after the gods of the strangers of the land, whither they go [to be] among them, and will forsake me, and break my covenant which I have made with them.

    Context: God tells Moses he will die. The death formula 'sleep with thy fathers' (shakab im-abotekha) is used — the standard OT euphemism for death. God himself uses this language, calling death 'sleep.'

    Notes: Hebrew: shokeb im-abotekha (lying down with your fathers, from shakab H7901). This is God himself speaking, using the sleep metaphor for death. The same formula appears for David (1 Ki 2:10), Solomon (1 Ki 11:43), and dozens of other kings. If God calls death 'sleep,' the metaphor carries divine authority.

    David slept with his fathers — standard OT death formula¶

    So David slept with his fathers, and was buried in the city of David.

    Context: The record of David's death using the standard regnal death formula. David 'slept' (shakab) with his fathers and was buried. This is the same David whom Peter declares has NOT ascended into the heavens (Acts 2:34).

    Notes: Hebrew: vayyishkab david im-abotav (and David lay down with his fathers). The Qal consecutive imperfect of shakab (H7901). The LXX translates with ekoimathe (from koimao G2837) — the same word used for death-sleep in the NT. Acts 2:29,34 explicitly states David is 'both dead and buried' and has 'NOT ascended into the heavens' — directly contradicting any idea that David is consciously in heaven.

    The soul that sinneth, it shall die — nephesh can die¶

    Behold, all souls are mine; as the soul of the father, so also the soul of the son is mine: the soul that sinneth, it shall die.

    Context: God declares ownership of all souls (nephesh) and states the principle: the sinning soul dies. If the soul were inherently immortal, it could not die. This directly contradicts the concept of an immortal soul.

    Notes: Hebrew: hannephesh hachote't hi tamut (the soul that sins, it shall die). The pronoun hi (it/she) is emphatic — the soul ITSELF dies. Nephesh (H5315) here means the person/being, not an immaterial component. If the soul is immortal, God's statement is false. Combined with Gen 2:7 (man became a nephesh chayyah), the whole person dies at death.

    Jesus: the maid is not dead but sleepeth — Jesus calls death sleep (katheudo)¶

    He said unto them, Give place: for the maid is not dead, but sleepeth. And they laughed him to scorn.

    Context: Jesus arrives at Jairus's house where the daughter has died. He declares she is 'not dead but sleepeth' (katheudei, from katheudo G2518). The crowd laughs because they know she is dead. Jesus uses the sleep metaphor with full knowledge of her death.

    Notes: Greek: ou gar apethanen (for she did not die) to korasion (the girl) alla katheudei (but sleeps, G2518 Pres Act Ind 3s). Jesus redefines death as sleep. This is the same vocabulary pattern as John 11:11-14 (Lazarus sleepeth/is dead). Katheudo (G2518) is used here as a death euphemism by Jesus himself, making it authoritative. The crowd's laughter confirms she was genuinely dead — Jesus is reframing death, not denying it.

    Fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in gehenna¶

    And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell.

    Context: Jesus teaches about whom to fear. Humans can kill the body but not the soul (psyche); God can destroy (apollymi) both soul and body in gehenna. The soul CAN be destroyed — it is not inherently indestructible.

    Notes: Greek: apollesai (G622, Aor Act Inf of apollymi = to destroy utterly) kai psychen kai soma (both soul and body) en geenne (in gehenna, G1067). The verb apollymi means to destroy, ruin, put to death — not to torment forever. If the soul were inherently immortal and indestructible, Jesus's statement that God is 'able to destroy' it would be meaningless. This is Jesus teaching conditional immortality — the soul can perish.

    All in the graves shall hear his voice and come forth — resurrection¶

    Marvel not at this: for the hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves shall hear his voice, And shall come forth; they that have done good, unto the resurrection of life; and they that have done evil, unto the resurrection of damnation.

    Context: Jesus describes the future resurrection. ALL who are in the graves shall hear his voice and come forth — some to life, some to judgment. The dead are IN THE GRAVES, not in heaven or hell, until the hour of resurrection.

    Notes: Greek: pantes hoi en tois mnemeois (all those in the tombs) akousousin (shall hear, Fut Act Ind 3P of akouo G191) tes phones autou (his voice). The dead are located 'in the tombs' — not in heaven, purgatory, or conscious intermediate state. They come forth (ekporeusontai) at the resurrection. Two outcomes: anastasis zoes (resurrection of life) and anastasis kriseos (resurrection of judgment). Both groups are in the graves until they hear Christ's voice.

    Martha: I know he shall rise again in the resurrection at the last day¶

    Jesus saith unto her, Thy brother shall rise again. Martha saith unto him, I know that he shall rise again in the resurrection at the last day. Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live:

    Context: Jesus and Martha's exchange about Lazarus. Martha's theology is clear: resurrection happens 'at the last day' — not at the moment of death. Jesus does not correct her timing; he affirms he IS the resurrection and the life.

    Notes: Greek: anastesetai (G450, Fut Mid Ind 3s of anistemi = he will rise) en te anastasei (in the resurrection) en te eschate hemera (on the last day). Martha's understanding of resurrection timing — at the last day — is identical to Jesus's four-fold statement in John 6:39,40,44,54. Jesus does not correct her eschatology; he personalizes it: 'I AM the resurrection.' The hope is still future resurrection, embodied in the person of Christ.

    David fell on sleep (koimao) and was laid unto his fathers and saw corruption¶

    For David, after he had served his own generation by the will of God, fell on sleep, and was laid unto his fathers, and saw corruption:

    Context: Paul's sermon at Antioch. He distinguishes David from Christ: David fell asleep (ekoimathe, from koimao G2837), was buried, and saw corruption. Christ was raised and did NOT see corruption (v.37).

    Notes: Greek: ekoimathe (G2837, Aor Pass Ind 3s of koimaomai = fell asleep). Paul uses the standard NT death-sleep word for David. Combined with Acts 2:29,34 (David is dead, buried, and has NOT ascended), this establishes David's current state: asleep in death, awaiting resurrection. David's body 'saw corruption' (eiden diaphthoran) — he decomposed. He is not in conscious heavenly bliss.

    Some are fallen asleep (koimao) — Paul uses sleep for dead believers¶

    After that, he was seen of above five hundred brethren at once; of whom the greater part remain unto this present, but some are fallen asleep.

    Context: Paul lists witnesses to the resurrection of Christ. Of the 500+ who saw the risen Christ, some have since died — Paul says they 'are fallen asleep' (ekoimathasan, from koimao G2837).

    Notes: Greek: ekoimathasan (G2837, Aor Pass Ind 3P of koimaomai = fell asleep). Paul naturally uses sleep language for dead believers. This is not a euphemism for polite conversation — it reflects Paul's theology of death as unconscious sleep until resurrection, as the rest of 1 Cor 15 makes clear (vv.18,20,51).

    Christ the firstfruits of them that slept (koimao)¶

    But now is Christ risen from the dead, [and] become the firstfruits of them that slept.

    Context: The turning point of Paul's argument. Christ IS risen — and is the firstfruits (aparche) of those who have died ('slept,' kekoimamenon). The firstfruits metaphor implies a harvest to follow — the general resurrection.

    Notes: Greek: aparche ton kekoimamenon (G2837, Perf Pass Ptcp Gen Pl M of koimaomai = of those having fallen asleep). The perfect participle indicates an ongoing state — those who have fallen asleep and remain asleep. Christ is the FIRST to rise permanently; the sleepers will follow at the harvest (resurrection). The firstfruits/harvest metaphor demands that the dead are currently sleeping (not yet harvested/resurrected).

    Whether we wake or sleep, we should live together with him¶

    Who died for us, that, whether we wake or sleep, we should live together with him.

    Context: Paul's conclusion to the resurrection passage (4:13-5:11). Whether alive ('wake,' gregoromen from gregoreo G1127) or dead ('sleep,' katheudomen from katheudo G2518), believers will live together with Christ — at his coming.

    Notes: Greek: eite gregoromen eite katheudomen (whether we wake or sleep) hama syn auto zesomen (together with him we should live). Katheudo (G2518) is used here for death — the same word Jesus used in Matt 9:24. The living together (zesomen, Aor Act Subj 1P of zao) is FUTURE — connected to the resurrection event of 4:16-17. The assurance is that both living and dead believers share the same resurrection hope.

    Christ brought life and immortality to light through the gospel¶

    But is now made manifest by the appearing of our Saviour Jesus Christ, who hath abolished death, and hath brought life and immortality to light through the gospel:

    Context: Paul describes the effect of Christ's appearing. Christ ABOLISHED death (katargeo) and brought life (zoe) and immortality (aphtharsia G861) to light. Immortality had to be brought to light — it was not already possessed.

    Notes: Greek: katargesantos (G2673, Aor Act Ptcp Gen Sg M of katargeo = having abolished/rendered inoperative) men ton thanaton (death). Photisantos (G5461, Aor Act Ptcp Gen Sg M of photizo = having brought to light) de zoen kai aphtharsian (life and incorruption/immortality G861). If humans already possessed immortal souls, immortality would not need to be 'brought to light' — it would already be evident. Christ's work is precisely to bring immortality as a gift (Rom 2:7, 1 Cor 15:53-54).

    Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord; they REST from their labours¶

    And I heard a voice from heaven saying unto me, Write, Blessed [are] the dead which die in the Lord from henceforth: Yea, saith the Spirit, that they may rest from their labours; and their works do follow them.

    Context: A beatitude for the dead in the Lord. The Spirit declares they REST (anapauomai G373) from their labours. Rest = cessation of activity, consistent with death-as-rest/sleep throughout Scripture.

    Notes: Greek: anapausontai (G373, Fut Mid Ind 3P of anapauomai = they shall rest) ek ton kopon auton (from their labours). Same word used in Rev 6:11 where the souls under the altar are told to 'rest' (anapausontai). The Spirit's characterization of the dead is REST — cessation from labour. Their works 'follow' (akolouthei) them, implying future reward at resurrection, not immediate recompense at death.

    Death and hades delivered up the dead — hades is temporary storage¶

    And the sea gave up the dead which were in it; and death and hell delivered up the dead which were in them: and they were judged every man according to their works. And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death.

    Context: The final judgment. Death and hades DELIVER UP the dead — they held the dead until this point. Then death and hades are themselves cast into the lake of fire and destroyed. Hades is temporary, not eternal.

    Notes: Greek: ho thanatos kai ho hades edokan (G1325, Aor Act Ind 3P of didomi = gave up/delivered) tous nekrous tous en autois (the dead who were in them). The dead were IN death and hades — held there, not conscious elsewhere. After giving up the dead, thanatos (death) and hades (G86) are cast into the lake of fire (limnen tou pyros). This is the second death (ho thanatos ho deuteros). Death itself is destroyed — consistent with 1 Cor 15:26 ('the last enemy that shall be destroyed is death').

    Spirits of just men made perfect — alleged conscious state¶

    To the general assembly and church of the firstborn, which are written in heaven, and to God the Judge of all, and to the spirits of just men made perfect,

    Context: The author of Hebrews lists what believers have come to in the new covenant. 'Spirits of just men made perfect' is often cited as evidence of conscious disembodied existence. However, 'made perfect' (teteleiosmenon, from teleioo G5048) is the same word used in Heb 11:40 — where these same people 'received NOT the promise.'

    Notes: Greek: pneumasi dikaion teteleiosmenon (G5048, Perf Pass Ptcp Gen Pl M of teleioo = having been made perfect/complete). The key is Heb 11:39-40: the OT faithful 'received NOT the promise' and are 'not made perfect' apart from us. The perfection here is covenantal/legal — achieved through Christ's sacrifice (Heb 10:14), not through conscious existence in heaven. 'Spirits' (pneumasi) may refer to the spiritual legacy/record of the just, not disembodied consciousness. The passage lists realities of the heavenly Jerusalem, not a description of an intermediate state.

    In the resurrection they neither marry; God is not God of the dead but of the living¶

    And Jesus answering said unto them, The children of this world marry, and are given in marriage: But they which shall be accounted worthy to obtain that world, and the resurrection from the dead, neither marry, nor are given in marriage: Neither can they die any more: for they are equal unto the angels; and are the children of God, being the children of the resurrection. Now that the dead are raised, even Moses shewed at the bush, when he calleth the Lord the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. For he is not a God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him.

    Context: Jesus answers the Sadducees about the resurrection. His argument is about RESURRECTION (v.35-36), not about a conscious intermediate state. 'All live unto him' (v.38) is proleptic — God counts the dead patriarchs as living because their resurrection is certain (cf. Rom 4:17).

    Notes: Greek v.38: pantes gar auto zosin (for all live unto/for him). The dative auto (unto him) is key — they live 'unto him' (from God's perspective), not 'unto themselves' (in present conscious experience). Cf. Rom 4:17: God 'calleth those things which be not as though they were.' Jesus introduces this as proof 'that the dead ARE RAISED' (v.37, hoti egeirontai hoi nekroi) — resurrection language, not intermediate state language. The Sadducees denied resurrection (not intermediate state), and Jesus's answer addresses resurrection.

    Paradise vision — living person's experience, not about death-state¶

    I knew a man in Christ above fourteen years ago, (whether in the body, I cannot tell; or whether out of the body, I cannot tell: God knoweth;) such an one caught up to the third heaven. And I knew such a man, (whether in the body, or out of the body, I cannot tell: God knoweth;) How that he was caught up into paradise, and heard unspeakable words, which it is not lawful for a man to utter.

    Context: Paul describes a visionary experience — being caught up to the third heaven/paradise while ALIVE. This is a living person's vision, not a death experience. It tells us about paradise's existence but nothing about the death-state.

    Notes: Greek: harpagenta (G726, Aor Pass Ptcp Acc Sg M of harpazo = caught up/snatched away) eis ton paradeison (into paradise, G3857 Acc Sg M). Paul equates the third heaven (v.2) with paradise (v.4). This was a LIVING person's experience — Paul was alive, not dead. It proves paradise exists but says nothing about dead people being conscious there. The phrase 'whether in the body or out of the body' describes the visionary mode, not a death-state.

    Michael contended about the body of Moses — Moses was resurrected¶

    Yet Michael the archangel, when contending with the devil he disputed about the body of Moses, durst not bring against him a railing accusation, but said, The Lord rebuke thee.

    Context: Jude references a tradition about Michael's dispute with the devil over Moses' body. The dispute is about the BODY — why contend for a body unless resurrection was at stake? Moses appeared at the transfiguration (Matt 17:3), but as a resurrected being, not a disembodied spirit.

    Notes: Greek: peri tou Mouseos somatos (G4983, Gen Sg N = about the body of Moses). The dispute was specifically about Moses' body (soma) — physical remains. The devil contested the body's disposition. This implies resurrection: Michael claimed the body for resurrection, the devil disputed it. Moses' appearance at the transfiguration (Matt 17:3) is thus explained as a RESURRECTED Moses, not a disembodied spirit — eliminating the transfiguration as evidence for conscious intermediate state.
