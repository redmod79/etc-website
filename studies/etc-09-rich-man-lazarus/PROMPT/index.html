    Focus Areas¶

    1. The Lukan 'Certain Rich Man' Formula (anthropos tis plousios): This focus area is the first of the purely literary-structural lines of evidence. The scoping agent's prior research (etc6-09) identified this formula as one of nine independent indicators for the parabolic reading. The current study must verify the claim independently through the parser tools, comparing the exact Greek construction at all three sites. If the formula is indeed identical, it establishes a Lukan literary pattern: 'anthropos tis plousios' = parabolic narrative opening. The research agent should also check whether any other Gospel writer uses this formula and whether it ever introduces a historical account rather than a parable. Verses: Luke 12:16, Luke 16:1, Luke 16:19 Strong's: G444, G5100, G4145, G3850

    2. Luke 15:3 Parabole Label and the Five-Narrative Literary Structure: The literary structure of Luke 15-16 is foundational to the genre question. Luke 15:1-2 establishes the audience (Pharisees and scribes murmuring) and the occasion (Jesus receiving sinners). Luke 15:3 then says 'he spoke this parable unto them' (singular 'parabole' for what follows). Three stories follow in chapter 15. Chapter 16 opens with Jesus speaking to his disciples (16:1) but the Pharisees are explicitly present and reacting (16:14). There is no discourse-closing formula between 16:13 and 16:19. The mid-discourse Pharisee reaction at 16:14-15 is particularly significant because it connects the fourth and fifth narratives thematically (money/wealth themes in both the unjust steward and the rich man stories). Verses: Luke 15:1, Luke 15:2, Luke 15:3, Luke 16:1, Luke 16:14, Luke 16:15, Luke 16:19 Strong's: G3850, G5330, G1122, G268

    3. Vocabulary Analysis: basanos/odunao vs. basanizo/basanismos: The word-studies library already contains canonical entries for all four basanos-family words (G928, G929, G930, G931). The research agent should read these studies and verify the distribution data. The critical observation from etc6-09 was that Luke 16 does NOT use the standard eschatological torment vocabulary. The rich man's experience is described with odunao (grief/anguish — the same word used for Mary's sorrow in Luke 2:48 and the Ephesian elders' grief in Acts 20:38) and basanos (originally a touchstone for testing metal purity). Meanwhile, the explicit eschatological passages (Rev 14:10-11; 20:10) consistently use basanizo/basanismos. This vocabulary divergence is a genre indicator. Verses: Luke 16:23, Luke 16:24, Luke 16:25, Luke 16:28, Revelation 14:10, Revelation 14:11, Revelation 20:10 Strong's: G931, G3600, G3601, G928, G929, G930

    4. Abraham's Bosom (kolpos Abraam) — Uniqueness and Significance: The uniqueness of 'Abraham's bosom' as a scriptural concept is a significant genre indicator. Kolpos (G2859) appears in John 1:18 (bosom of the Father), John 13:23 (disciple leaning on Jesus' bosom), Luke 6:38 (into your bosom/lap), and Acts 27:39 (a bay/inlet). The phrase 'Abraham's bosom' as a location for the righteous dead appears ONLY in Luke 16 — not in any other Gospel, not in Acts, not in Paul, not in Revelation. If this were a literal description of where the righteous dead go, we would expect it to appear elsewhere in Scripture's afterlife teaching. Its singular occurrence in a passage with multiple parabolic indicators suggests culturally familiar imagery used as a narrative setting, not doctrinal teaching. Verses: Luke 16:22, Luke 16:23, John 1:18, John 13:23, Luke 6:38, Luke 23:43 Strong's: G2859, G11, G3857

    5. The Passage's Self-Identified Teaching Point (vv. 29, 31): Every parable has a teaching point, and the teaching point is not its setting but its climax. The Prodigal Son's teaching point is about the Father's welcome, not pig-farming economics. Similarly, the Rich Man and Lazarus builds to a climactic exchange in vv. 27-31 that is entirely about heeding Scripture. The afterlife setting is the narrative framework, not the lesson. The ironic foreshadowing in v. 31 ('one rising from the dead') points to both the resurrection of the other Lazarus (John 11) and Jesus' own resurrection, where indeed many did not believe despite the evidence. The research agent should trace the connections between Luke 16:31 and John 11-12. Verses: Luke 16:27, Luke 16:28, Luke 16:29, Luke 16:30, Luke 16:31, John 11:43, John 11:44, John 12:10, John 12:11 Strong's: G3475, G4396, G3992, G450, G191, G3982

    6. Same-Author Evidence: Luke-Acts on the State of the Dead: This focus area addresses perhaps the strongest single argument for the parabolic reading: the same author who narrates the Rich Man and Lazarus also records, in didactic prose, that David — a man after God's own heart — is dead, buried, and has NOT ascended to heaven. If Luke understood the Rich Man and Lazarus as teaching that the righteous dead are conscious in Abraham's bosom, he would be contradicting what he records Peter saying about David in Acts 2. The only way both statements can be true is if Luke 16 is parabolic imagery and Acts 2 is the didactic teaching. Verses: Acts 2:29, Acts 2:34, Acts 13:36, Luke 16:22, Luke 16:23 Strong's: G86, G2288, G305, G3772

    7. Tree 3 Genre Gate Application to Luke 16:19-31: Tree 3 is the central analytical framework for this study. Prior studies consistently classified Luke 16:19-31 as failing Gates 1 and 3, resulting in N-tier (Neutral) classification. The current study must reproduce this independently. Gate 1: the rich man is unnamed (unique among characters Jesus describes in detail), has no genealogy, no historical markers, no cross-reference anywhere in Scripture. Lazarus (if taken as historical) would need to be a different person from the Lazarus of John 11, since the parable's Lazarus dies in poverty while the historical Lazarus of Bethany has sisters wealthy enough to host a dinner (John 12). Gate 3: the evidence from the other focus areas converges on the parabolic classification. The research agent should walk through each gate systematically. Verses: Luke 16:19, Luke 16:22, Luke 16:23, Luke 16:24, Luke 16:26, Luke 16:29, Luke 16:31 Strong's: G3850, G444, G5100, G86, G931, G3600

    8. OT Precedent for Figurative Speech from/about the Dead: The OT mashal tradition provides the direct literary precedent for Luke 16. Isaiah 14:4 explicitly labels what follows as a mashal (H4912), and then describes dead kings rising in sheol to greet the king of Babylon (14:9-10). No interpreter claims Isaiah 14 teaches literal afterlife doctrine — it is universally recognized as figurative mashal speech. Yet the imagery is strikingly similar to Luke 16: dead persons depicted as conscious, speaking, aware of new arrivals. If Isaiah 14 can use this imagery without teaching literal afterlife consciousness, so can Luke 16. The research agent should trace the mashal genre markers and compare with Luke 16's parabolic markers. Verses: Isaiah 14:4, Isaiah 14:9, Isaiah 14:10, Genesis 4:10, Ezekiel 32:21, Ecclesiastes 9:5, Ecclesiastes 9:10, Psalm 146:4, Psalm 6:5, Psalm 115:17 Strong's: H4912, H7585, H7496, G3850, H3045

    9. Physical Impossibilities in the Literal Reading: This focus area addresses the internal coherence of the literal reading. The etc7-01 study established that biblical anthropology is holistic (man IS a living soul, not HAS a separate soul). If the dead are disembodied spirits awaiting resurrection, they do not have tongues, fingers, or physical needs for water. The rich man's request for water makes perfect sense as parabolic detail (expressing desperation) but creates logical problems as literal description. Additionally, etc7-02 established that immortality is conditional and received at resurrection — unredeemed humans do not possess inherent immortality for conscious existence between death and resurrection. Verses: Luke 16:24, Luke 16:25, John 11:11, John 11:14, Luke 8:52, Luke 8:53, 1 Corinthians 15:53, 1 Corinthians 15:54 Strong's: G1147, G1100, G5204, G5395, G5590, G4151

    10. The Naming of Lazarus — Steelman for the Literal Reading: The naming of Lazarus is the steelman argument for the literal reading. Nave's LAZARUS entry lists only the brother of Mary and Martha (John 11) — no separate entry for a historical beggar named Lazarus. The name means 'God helps' (from Eleazar), functioning perfectly as a thematic name in the parable: the poor man whom the rich man ignored is the one whom God helps. The rich man is NOT named, unprecedented for a real person described in such detail. Ezekiel 23:4 names Aholah ('her own tent') and Aholibah ('my tent is in her') in an explicitly allegorical passage. The research agent should evaluate whether the naming overrides the cumulative weight of multiple parabolic indicators. Verses: Luke 16:20, Luke 16:19, John 11:1, John 11:43, Ezekiel 23:4 Strong's: G2976, G444, G5100, G4145

    11. Didactic Death-State Passages vs. Parabolic Imagery — Clarity Hierarchy: The didactic evidence is extensive and multi-author: Ecclesiastes 9:5 ('the dead know not anything'), Psalm 146:4 ('his thoughts perish'), Psalm 6:5 ('in death there is no remembrance'), Psalm 115:17 ('the dead praise not the LORD'), Job 14:21 ('he knoweth it not'), Isaiah 38:18-19 ('the grave cannot praise thee'). These are all in didactic genres. Against this, the literal reading of Luke 16 would introduce conscious post-mortem existence based on a single passage in parabolic genre. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 15:18 ('then they also which are fallen asleep in Christ are perished') works logically ONLY if the dead are NOT already in conscious bliss — if they were in Abraham's bosom, they would not have 'perished' even without resurrection. Verses: Ecclesiastes 9:5, Ecclesiastes 9:10, Psalm 146:4, Psalm 6:5, Psalm 115:17, Job 14:12, Job 14:21, Isaiah 38:18, Isaiah 38:19, 1 Corinthians 15:18 Strong's: H3045, H4191, H7585, G2837, G2348

    12. The Reversal Motif and Connection to Lukan Theology: Luke's Gospel has a distinctive emphasis on wealth-reversal woven from the Magnificat to the final discourse. Mary's song declares God 'hath put down the mighty and exalted them of low degree; filled the hungry with good things and the rich sent empty away' (1:52-53). The Lukan Beatitudes include 'Blessed are ye poor' (6:20) and 'Woe unto you that are rich' (6:24). The rich fool (12:16-21) warns against earthly wealth. The unjust steward (16:1-13) immediately precedes the Rich Man and Lazarus and addresses the Pharisees who 'were lovers of money' (16:14). In this context, the Rich Man and Lazarus is not an isolated afterlife geography passage — it is the culminating narrative in Luke's wealth-reversal theology, directed at the money-loving Pharisees. Verses: Luke 16:25, Luke 1:52, Luke 1:53, Luke 6:20, Luke 6:24, Luke 12:16, Luke 12:20, Luke 12:21, Luke 16:13, Luke 16:14 Strong's: G4145, G4434, G3870, G3600
