    Verses¶

    Question¶

    Luke 16:19-31 -- parable or literal? Genre, first-century Jewish context. Apply Tree 3 genre gate. Literary structure (five parables in Luke 15-16).

    Rich Man and Lazarus: Setting¶

    Luke 16:19 There was a certain rich man, which was clothed in purple and fine linen, and fared sumptuously every day: Luke 16:20 And there was a certain beggar named Lazarus, which was laid at his gate, full of sores, Luke 16:21 And desiring to be fed with the crumbs which fell from the rich man's table: moreover the dogs came and licked his sores.

    Context: Opening formula 'anthropos de tis en plousios' — identical to Luke 12:16 and 16:1. Introduces the fifth consecutive narrative in the Luke 15-16 discourse unit. The naming of Lazarus ('God helps') is unique among Jesus' parables and is the primary steelman argument for the literal reading.

    Notes: The formula 'anthropos tis plousios' appears at Luke 12:16 (rich fool, explicitly labeled parabole), 16:1 (unjust steward), and 16:19 (this passage). Parser confirms identical construction: anthropos [N-NSM] + tis [X-NSM] + plousios [A-NSM].

    Rich Man and Lazarus: Afterlife Setting¶

    Luke 16:22 And it came to pass, that the beggar died, and was carried by the angels into Abraham's bosom: the rich man also died, and was buried; Luke 16:23 And in hell he lift up his eyes, being in torments, and seeth Abraham afar off, and Lazarus in his bosom.

    Context: Introduces the afterlife imagery. 'Abraham's bosom' (kolpos Abraam, G2859) appears nowhere else in Scripture as a destination for the righteous dead. The rich man is 'en to Haide' (locative dative of hades, G86) and 'en basanois' (dative plural of basanos, G931 — 'in torments'). Parser confirms basanois = N-DPF Dat Pl F of basanos G931, NOT basanizo G928.

    Notes: Key vocabulary divergence: Luke 16 uses basanos (G931, touchstone/torment, 3 occurrences total in NT) and odunao (G3600, grief/anguish, 4 occurrences). Standard eschatological passages (Rev 14:10; 20:10) consistently use basanizo (G928, 12 occ.) and basanismos (G929, 6 occ., exclusively in Revelation). This vocabulary divergence is a genre indicator.

    Rich Man's Plea — Physical Impossibilities¶

    Luke 16:24 And he cried and said, Father Abraham, have mercy on me, and send Lazarus, that he may dip the tip of his finger in water, and cool my tongue; for I am tormented in this flame.

    Context: Physical body parts (daktylos G1147 finger, glossa G1100 tongue) attributed to supposedly disembodied souls. Parser confirms odunaomai = V-PPI-1S Pres Pass Ind 1st person singular of odunao G3600 — 'I am being grieved/tormented.' The same verb odunao is used for Mary's parental anxiety (Luke 2:48) and the Ephesian elders' grief at Paul's departure (Acts 20:38). Phlox G5395 (flame) appears — used elsewhere for divine theophany (Rev 1:14; 2:18; Acts 7:30), not eschatological punishment.

    Notes: If the dead are disembodied spirits awaiting resurrection (the literal reading requires), they do not have tongues, fingers, or physical needs for water. The request makes perfect sense as parabolic detail expressing desperation.

    Reversal Motif and Great Chasm¶

    Luke 16:25 But Abraham said, Son, remember that thou in thy lifetime receivedst thy good things, and likewise Lazarus evil things: but now he is comforted, and thou art tormented. Luke 16:26 And beside all this, between us and you there is a great gulf fixed: so that they which would pass from hence to you cannot; neither can they pass to us, that [would come] from thence.

    Context: Parser confirms the vocabulary contrast: parakaleitai (G3870 parakaleo, V-PPI-3S 'is comforted') vs. odunasai (G3600 odunao, V-PPI-2S 'you are grieved'). The reversal motif connects to Lukan theology: Magnificat (1:52-53), Beatitudes/Woes (6:20,24). Chasma mega esteriktai: chasma (G5490, hapax legomenon in NT) + mega (G3173) + esteriktai (G4741 sterizo, V-RPI-3S Perfect Passive Indicative — permanently established). The perfect tense of sterizo indicates a permanent, completed establishment of the barrier.

    Notes: The wealth-reversal in v.25 connects this passage to Luke's distinctive emphasis running from the Magnificat through the Beatitudes. The chasm (chasma) is a hapax legomenon — it appears only here in the entire NT.

    Rich Man's Second Request¶

    Luke 16:27 Then he said, I pray thee therefore, father, that thou wouldest send him to my father's house: Luke 16:28 For I have five brethren; that he may testify unto them, lest they also come into this place of torment.

    Context: The narrative transitions from afterlife imagery to the teaching point. The rich man's request to send Lazarus to his brothers shifts focus from the afterlife setting to the sufficiency of Scripture. 'Place of torment' = topos basanou (G5117 topos + G931 basanos genitive).

    Notes: Five brothers — a specific narrative detail with no historical verification possible. In parables, such details serve the story's purpose, not historical documentation.

    CLIMACTIC: Sufficiency of Moses and the Prophets¶

    Luke 16:29 Abraham saith unto him, They have Moses and the prophets; let them hear them.

    Context: Parser confirms: Echousi (G2192 echo, V-PAI-3P 'they have') Mousea (G3475 Moses, N-ASM) kai tous prophetas (G4396 prophetes, N-APM). Akousatosan (G191 akouo, V-AAM-3P Aorist Active Imperative 3rd person plural — 'let them hear!'). This is the passage's identified teaching point: the sufficiency of Scripture. The imperative mood of akousatosan makes this a command, not a suggestion.

    Notes: Every parable has a teaching point. The Rich Man and Lazarus builds to this climactic exchange: the sufficiency of Moses and the prophets. The afterlife setting is the narrative framework, not the lesson.

    CLIMACTIC CONCLUSION: Resurrection Foreshadowing¶

    Luke 16:30 And he said, Nay, father Abraham: but if one went unto them from the dead, they will repent. Luke 16:31 And he said unto him, If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead.

    Context: Parser confirms v.31 structure: Ei (G1487 conditional) + Mouseos (G3475 Gen Sg) kai ton propheton (G4396 Gen Pl) ouk akouousin (G191 V-PAI-3P present active indicative — 'they do not hear') ... oude ean tis ek nekron (G3498 dead, A-GPM) anaste (G450 anistemi V-2AAS-3S Aorist Active Subjunctive — 'should rise') peisthesontai (G3982 peitho V-FPI-3P Future Passive Indicative — 'they will [not] be persuaded'). Protasis: present indicative (factual condition). Apodosis: future passive of peitho. Ironic foreshadowing: a Lazarus DID rise from the dead (John 11:43-44), and the response was a plot to kill both Jesus and Lazarus (John 12:10-11).

    Notes: This verse is the culminating point of the entire passage. The teaching is about the sufficiency of Scripture and the hardness of unbelief — not about afterlife geography.

    Discourse Frame: Parabole Label¶

    Luke 15:1 Then drew near unto him all the publicans and sinners for to hear him. Luke 15:2 And the Pharisees and scribes murmured, saying, This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them. Luke 15:3 And he spake this parable unto them, saying,

    Context: Parser confirms v.3: eipen (G3004 lego, V-2AAI-3S) + ten parabolen (G3850, N-ASF singular accusative) + tauten (G3778, D-ASF demonstrative 'this'). The singular parabole with demonstrative tauten ('this parable') introduces the entire discourse unit. Three stories follow in chapter 15 under this single label. The audience is established: publicans, sinners (v.1), Pharisees, scribes (v.2).

    Notes: The singular parabole at 15:3 introduces at minimum the three chapter-15 stories. There is no discourse-closing formula between chapters 15 and 16. The Pharisees remain explicitly present (16:14).

    Unjust Steward: Same Formula¶

    Luke 16:1 And he said also unto his disciples, There was a certain rich man, which had a steward; and the same was accused unto him that he had wasted his goods.

    Context: Opens with the same 'anthropos tis plousios' formula as 16:19 and 12:16. No discourse break between chapter 15 and this verse — the discourse continues without interruption.

    Notes: Fourth narrative in the Luke 15-16 discourse sequence. Same opening formula connects it to the parabolic discourse.

    Mid-Discourse Pharisee Reaction¶

    Luke 16:14 And the Pharisees also, who were covetous, heard all these things: and they derided him. Luke 16:15 And he said unto them, Ye are they which justify yourselves before men; but God knoweth your hearts: for that which is highly esteemed among men is abomination in the sight of God.

    Context: The Pharisees (philargyroi — lovers of money) react mid-discourse, connecting the fourth narrative (unjust steward) to the fifth (rich man and Lazarus). This mid-discourse reaction proves the audience is continuous from 15:1-2.

    Notes: The phrase 'who were covetous' (philargyroi, lit. 'money-lovers') directly links the wealth theme of the unjust steward to the rich man and Lazarus story that follows.

    Rich Fool: Same Formula with Parabole Label¶

    Luke 12:16 And he spake a parable unto them, saying, The ground of a certain rich man brought forth plentifully:

    Context: The rich fool parable uses the same 'anthropos tis plousios' formula AND is explicitly labeled parabole (G3850). This establishes the formula as a parabolic marker in Luke's writing.

    Notes: Three occurrences of the identical formula: 12:16 (explicitly parabole), 16:1 (universally accepted as parabolic), 16:19 (disputed). The pattern establishes Lukan convention.

    Same-Author Didactic: David Dead and Buried¶

    Acts 2:29 Men [and] brethren, let me freely speak unto you of the patriarch David, that he is both dead and buried, and his sepulchre is with us unto this day.

    Context: Peter's Pentecost sermon, recorded by the same author (Luke) who narrates Luke 16. Parser confirms: eteleutesen (G5053 teleutao V-AAI-3S 'he died') kai etaphe (G2290 thapto V-2API-3S 'was buried'). Didactic prose about a named historical individual — a higher clarity level than parabolic imagery.

    Notes: Same author (Luke) who narrates Luke 16 also records that David — a man after God's own heart — is dead and buried. This is didactic, not parabolic.

    Same-Author Didactic: David NOT Ascended¶

    Acts 2:34 For David is not ascended into the heavens: but he saith himself, The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou on my right hand,

    Context: Parser confirms emphatic negative: ou (G3756 negative particle) gar (G1063 'for') Daueid (G1138 David N-PRI) anebe (G305 anabaino V-2AAI-3S 'ascended') eis tous ouranous (G3772 ouranos N-APM 'the heavens'). Emphatic: David has NOT ascended into the heavens. If Luke understood Luke 16 as teaching that the righteous dead are conscious in Abraham's bosom, he would be contradicting what he records Peter saying about David.

    Notes: The only way both Acts 2:34 and Luke 16:22 can be true is if Luke 16 is parabolic imagery and Acts 2 is the didactic teaching. Same-author evidence is among the strongest arguments for the parabolic reading.

    Paul on David: Fell Asleep¶

    Acts 13:36 For David, after he had served his own generation by the will of God, fell on sleep, and was laid unto his fathers, and saw corruption:

    Context: Paul, also recorded by Luke, confirms David 'fell on sleep' — the standard biblical idiom for death as unconsciousness.

    Notes: Confirms the Acts 2:29,34 evidence from a different speaker (Paul) in the same book (Acts, by Luke).

    OT Mashal Label: Against King of Babylon¶

    Isaiah 14:4 That thou shalt take up this proverb against the king of Babylon, and say, How hath the oppressor ceased! the golden city ceased!

    Context: Hebrew parser confirms: venasata (nasa H5375, Verb.Qal.Perf.2ms 'you shall lift up') hammashal (mashal H4912, Noun.ms.Abs with article 'the proverb/mashal') hazzeh (zeh, DemPron.ms 'this') al-melek babel ('against the king of Babylon'). This is the classic mashal speech-act formula (see grammar reference). What follows in vv.9-10 is dead kings speaking in sheol — universally recognized as figurative, not literal afterlife teaching.

    Notes: The mashal label is explicit. The imagery of dead persons speaking and being conscious in sheol is the direct OT literary precedent for Luke 16's parabolic afterlife imagery.

    Figurative Speech from Dead in Sheol¶

    Isaiah 14:9 Hell from beneath is moved for thee to meet [thee] at thy coming: it stirreth up the dead for thee, [even] all the chief ones of the earth; it hath raised up from their thrones all the kings of the nations. Isaiah 14:10 All they shall speak and say unto thee, Art thou also become weak as we? art thou become like unto us?

    Context: Dead kings rise from thrones in sheol to greet the king of Babylon. This is explicitly labeled mashal (v.4) and universally recognized as figurative taunt-song. The imagery — dead persons depicted as conscious, speaking, aware of new arrivals — is strikingly similar to Luke 16. If Isaiah 14 can use this imagery without teaching literal afterlife consciousness, so can Luke 16.

    Notes: The rephaim (H7496, 'shades/dead') are consistently portrayed as weak, powerless shadows — not as conscious beings in literal torment.

    Figurative: Dead Speaking¶

    Genesis 4:10 And he said, What hast thou done? the voice of thy brother's blood crieth unto me from the ground.

    Context: Abel's blood 'cries' from the ground. No interpreter takes this as literal speech from the dead. The dead 'speaking' is a recognized OT figurative convention.

    Notes: Precedent for figurative speech attributed to the dead without implying literal consciousness.

    Named Characters in Allegory¶

    Ezekiel 23:4 And the names of them [were] Aholah the elder, and Aholibah her sister: and they were mine, and they bare sons and daughters. Thus [were] their names; Samaria [is] Aholah, and Jerusalem Aholibah.

    Context: Aholah ('her own tent') and Aholibah ('my tent is in her') are named characters in an explicitly allegorical passage. Their names are symbolic, not historical. This is OT precedent for naming characters in figurative/parabolic literature.

    Notes: Demonstrates that the naming of characters does not require a literal historical reading. Symbolic names in figurative discourse are an established OT convention.

    Figurative Speech from Sheol¶

    Ezekiel 32:21 The strong among the mighty shall speak to him out of the midst of hell with them that help him: they are gone down, they lie uncircumcised, slain by the sword.

    Context: Another instance of the dead speaking from sheol in prophetic literature. The mighty dead address the newcomer — the same motif as Isaiah 14:9-10 and similar to Luke 16's afterlife dialogue.

    Notes: Part of Ezekiel's lament over Egypt. Like Isaiah 14, this is figurative/poetic speech attributed to the dead in sheol.

    Didactic Death-State: Dead Know Not Anything¶

    Ecclesiastes 9:5 For the living know that they shall die: but the dead know not any thing, neither have they any more a reward; for the memory of them is forgotten. Ecclesiastes 9:6 Also their love, and their hatred, and their envy, is now perished; neither have they any more a portion for ever in any [thing] that is done under the sun.

    Context: Hebrew parser confirms: ki hachayyim (the living, Adj.mp.Abs with article) yod'im (yada H3045, Verb.Qal.Ptcp.mp.Abs 'knowing') sheyamutu (muth H4191, Verb.Qal.Impf.3mp 'they shall die') vehammetim (the dead, muth Verb.Qal.Ptcp.mp.Abs with article) einam yod'im (ain + yada 'are not knowing') meumah ('anything'). Didactic genre. Multi-author testimony — Solomon. The dead 'know not anything' directly contradicts a literal reading of Luke 16 where the dead are fully conscious, conversing, and aware.

    Notes: This is didactic prose, not parabolic imagery. In the clarity hierarchy, didactic passages govern the interpretation of parabolic imagery.

    Didactic Death-State: No Work in Sheol¶

    Ecclesiastes 9:10 Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do [it] with thy might; for [there is] no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest.

    Context: Sheol (H7585) is explicitly characterized as having no work, no device, no knowledge, no wisdom. This is didactic teaching about the nature of sheol/the grave.

    Notes: The same sheol/hades that Luke 16 depicts as a place of conscious conversation is described in didactic passages as a place of no knowledge.

    Didactic Death-State: Thoughts Perish¶

    Psalm 146:4 His breath goeth forth, he returneth to his earth; in that very day his thoughts perish.

    Context: At death, the person's thoughts (eshtonoth, 'his plans/thoughts') perish. Didactic statement from the Psalmist.

    Notes: If thoughts perish at death, the rich man's extended theological dialogue in Luke 16 cannot be literal.

    Didactic Death-State: No Remembrance in Death¶

    Psalm 6:5 For in death [there is] no remembrance of thee: in the grave who shall give thee thanks?

    Context: David's testimony: no remembrance of God in death, no thanksgiving in sheol. Same author (David) about whom Acts 2:29,34 says he is dead, buried, and NOT ascended.

    Notes: If there is no remembrance of God in death, the rich man's awareness of Abraham, Lazarus, and Scripture in Luke 16 is parabolic imagery.

    Didactic Death-State: Dead Praise Not the LORD¶

    Psalm 115:17 The dead praise not the LORD, neither any that go down into silence.

    Context: The dead go down into 'silence' (dumah). Sheol/death is characterized as a place of silence, not conversation.

    Notes: The rich man and Abraham have an extended dialogue in Luke 16. Didactic passages say the dead go into silence.

    Standard Eschatological Torment Vocabulary¶

    Revelation 14:10 The same shall drink of the wine of the wrath of God, which is poured out without mixture into the cup of his indignation; and he shall be tormented with fire and brimstone in the presence of the holy angels, and in the presence of the Lamb: Revelation 14:11 And the smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever: and they have no rest day nor night, who worship the beast and his image, and whosoever receiveth the mark of his name.

    Context: Standard eschatological torment vocabulary: basanizo G928 (v.10, 'shall be tormented'), basanismos G929 (v.11, 'torment'). Luke 16 uses basanos G931 and odunao G3600 instead. The vocabulary divergence between Luke 16 and Revelation 14 is significant.

    Notes: If Luke 16 were a didactic description of eschatological torment, we would expect basanizo/basanismos — the standard vocabulary. Luke 16 instead uses the rarer basanos (touchstone metaphor) and odunao (grief/sorrow).

    Hades as Temporary, Cast into Lake of Fire¶

    Revelation 20:13 And the sea gave up the dead which were in it; and death and hell delivered up the dead which were in them: and they were judged every man according to their works. Revelation 20:14 And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death.

    Context: Hades is temporary — it delivers up its dead and is itself cast into the lake of fire. This rules out hades as the permanent state of the wicked. Luke 16's 'in hades' setting is therefore not the final judgment.

    Notes: If hades is temporary and emptied at the final judgment, Luke 16's depiction of the rich man in hades cannot be describing the final eschatological state.

    Jesus Calls Death Sleep, Then Corrects¶

    John 11:11 These things said he: and after that he saith unto them, Our friend Lazarus sleepeth; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep. John 11:12 Then said his disciples, Lord, if he sleep, he shall do well. John 11:13 Howbeit Jesus spake of his death: but they thought that he had spoken of taking of rest in sleep. John 11:14 Then said Jesus unto them plainly, Lazarus is dead.

    Context: Jesus uses figurative language ('sleepeth') for death, then states plainly ('Lazarus is dead'). This is the self-interpretation pattern: figurative language followed by literal correction. See grammar reference for self-interpretation-literal-correction.

    Notes: The historical Lazarus of John 11 is from Bethany, has sisters wealthy enough to host dinners (John 12), and was raised by Jesus. The parable's Lazarus dies in poverty with no one to help him. These are different figures.

    Jesus Raises Lazarus — Ironic Connection to Luke 16:31¶

    John 11:43 And when he thus had spoken, he cried with a loud voice, Lazarus, come forth. John 11:44 And he that was dead came forth, bound hand and foot with graveclothes: and his face was bound about with a napkin. Jesus saith unto them, Loose him, and let him go.

    Context: A Lazarus DID rise from the dead — fulfilling the hypothetical of Luke 16:30. The response? Not repentance, but a plot to kill both Jesus and Lazarus (John 12:10-11). Luke 16:31's ironic foreshadowing is realized in John 11-12.

    Notes: This is the culminating irony: 'neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead' (Luke 16:31). A Lazarus did rise. They plotted murder instead of repenting.

    Response to Lazarus Rising: Murder Plot¶

    John 12:10 But the chief priests consulted that they might put Lazarus also to death; John 12:11 Because that by reason of him many of the Jews went away, and believed on Jesus.

    Context: The chief priests plotted to kill Lazarus because his resurrection caused many to believe. This confirms Luke 16:31's prediction that even resurrection would not persuade the hardened.

    Notes: Historical fulfillment of the parable's teaching point.

    Paul's Logic: Without Resurrection, Dead Perished¶

    1 Corinthians 15:18 Then they also which are fallen asleep in Christ are perished.

    Context: Paul's argument works logically ONLY if the dead are NOT already in conscious bliss. If believers were already in Abraham's bosom enjoying conscious fellowship, they would not have 'perished' even without resurrection. Paul's logic requires unconscious death.

    Notes: This is a decisive logical test for the literal reading of Luke 16. If the righteous dead are conscious in Abraham's bosom, Paul's argument in 1 Cor 15:18 collapses.

    Dead in Christ Rise at Last Trump¶

    1 Thessalonians 4:16 For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first:

    Context: The dead in Christ rise at the second coming — not at death. If believers went immediately to Abraham's bosom at death, the resurrection would be a demotion (leaving conscious bliss to be re-embodied).

    Notes: The resurrection as the hope of believers is consistent with unconscious death, not with immediate conscious afterlife.

    Destruction Vocabulary in Judgment¶

    Matthew 10:28 And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell.

    Context: Standard judgment vocabulary: apollymi G622 ('destroy') + psyche G5590 ('soul') + soma G4983 ('body') + geenna G1067 ('Gehenna'). Jesus' standard judgment vocabulary is destruction (apollymi), while Luke 16 uses grief/torment (basanos/odunao). This vocabulary divergence further indicates Luke 16 is not functioning as a didactic judgment passage.

    Notes: Apollymi (G622) is ABSENT from Luke 16. Geenna (G1067) is ABSENT from Luke 16. The passage uses hades (G86) and basanos (G931) — different vocabulary from Jesus' standard judgment teaching.

    God Alone Has Immortality¶

    1 Timothy 6:16 Who only hath immortality, dwelling in the light which no man can approach unto; whom no man hath seen, nor can see: to whom [be] honour and power everlasting. Amen.

    Context: God alone (monos) has immortality (athanasia G110). If humans do not possess inherent immortality, the rich man cannot be consciously existing in hades without it being granted by God.

    Notes: Conditional immortality undermines the prerequisite for conscious intermediate existence in the literal reading of Luke 16.

    Immortality Put On at Resurrection¶

    1 Corinthians 15:53 For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal [must] put on immortality. 1 Corinthians 15:54 So when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality, then shall be brought to pass the saying that is written, Death is swallowed up in victory.

    Context: Immortality (athanasia G110) and incorruption (aphtharsia G861) are received at the resurrection, not at death. This confirms that humans do not presently possess immortality for conscious post-mortem existence.

    Notes: If immortality is 'put on' at resurrection (future), humans do not possess it between death and resurrection.

    Magnificat: Wealth Reversal Theme¶

    Luke 1:52 He hath put down the mighty from [their] seats, and exalted them of low degree. Luke 1:53 He hath filled the hungry with good things; and the rich he hath sent empty away.

    Context: The Magnificat establishes Luke's wealth-reversal theology at the very beginning of his Gospel. The Rich Man and Lazarus parable is the culminating expression of this theme.

    Notes: Lukan theology: mighty put down, lowly exalted; hungry filled, rich sent empty. This is exactly the pattern of the Rich Man and Lazarus.

    Lukan Beatitudes and Woes: Wealth Reversal¶

    Luke 6:20 And he lifted up his eyes on his disciples, and said, Blessed [be ye] poor: for yours is the kingdom of God. Luke 6:24 But woe unto you that are rich! for ye have received your consolation.

    Context: Luke's version of the Beatitudes includes both blessings on the poor and woes on the rich. The Rich Man and Lazarus parable dramatizes exactly this reversal.

    Notes: The Lukan Beatitudes use the same vocabulary as Luke 16:25: 'received good things' (apelabes ta agatha) / 'consolation' (paraklesis).

    Burning Bush Argument: All Live Unto Him¶

    Luke 20:37 Now that the dead are raised, even Moses shewed at the bush, when he calleth the Lord the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. Luke 20:38 For he is not a God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him.

    Context: Jesus' argument about the resurrection uses Abraham. The phrase 'all live unto him' (pantes gar auto zosin) is about eschatological life through resurrection, not about intermediate conscious existence. Jesus proves resurrection, not intermediate state.

    Notes: Jesus uses the Abraham relationship to prove resurrection — future bodily restoration — not to prove current conscious existence in Abraham's bosom.

    Death-State: No Awareness¶

    Job 14:12 So man lieth down, and riseth not: till the heavens [be] no more, they shall not awake, nor be raised out of their sleep. Job 14:21 His sons come to honour, and he knoweth [it] not; and they are brought low, but he perceiveth [it] not of them.

    Context: Job describes the dead as asleep and unaware. The dead do not know what happens to their children (v.21).

    Notes: Yet in Luke 16, the rich man is fully aware of his five brothers' spiritual condition. This is parabolic detail, not didactic teaching about the death-state.

    Didactic: The Grave Cannot Praise God¶

    Isaiah 38:18 For the grave cannot praise thee, death can [not] celebrate thee: they that go down into the pit cannot hope for thy truth. Isaiah 38:19 The living, the living, he shall praise thee, as I [do] this day: the father to the children shall make known thy truth.

    Context: Hezekiah's testimony: the grave/sheol cannot praise God, death cannot celebrate God. Only the living praise God. Same vocabulary field as the other didactic death-state passages.

    Notes: Another didactic passage characterizing sheol/death as a state of no praise, no celebration, no hope — contradicting a literal reading of Luke 16.

    Jesus: Death as Sleep¶

    Luke 8:52 And all wept, and bewailed her: but he said, Weep not; she is not dead, but sleepeth. Luke 8:53 And they laughed him to scorn, knowing that she was dead.

    Context: Jesus calls death 'sleep' in the account of Jairus's daughter — recorded by the same author (Luke) who narrates Luke 16.

    Notes: Same-author evidence: Luke records Jesus calling death 'sleep' in 8:52, yet in 16:19-31 depicts conscious post-mortem activity. The resolution: Luke 8 is narrative/didactic; Luke 16 is parabolic imagery.

    Transition: Cannot Serve Two Masters¶

    Luke 16:13 No servant can serve two masters: for either he will hate the one, and love the other; or else he will hold to the one, and despise the other. Ye cannot serve God and mammon.

    Context: Immediately precedes the Pharisee reaction at 16:14. The wealth/mammon theme connects the unjust steward (16:1-12) to the Pharisees' love of money (16:14) to the rich man (16:19).

    Notes: Thematic continuity: mammon (16:13) → Pharisees as money-lovers (16:14) → rich man (16:19).

    Rich Fool: Parabolic Death¶

    Luke 12:20 But God said unto him, [Thou] fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee: then whose shall those things be, which thou hast provided? Luke 12:21 So [is] he that layeth up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God.

    Context: The rich fool parable (same 'anthropos tis plousios' formula, explicitly labeled parabole at 12:16) has the same moral: earthly wealth without God-ward richness leads to judgment.

    Notes: The rich fool and the rich man of Luke 16 share the same formula, the same wealth theme, and the same judgment trajectory. The rich fool is explicitly a parable.

    Eschatological Torment: Devil, Beast, False Prophet¶

    Revelation 20:10 And the devil that deceived them was cast into the lake of fire and brimstone, where the beast and the false prophet [are], and shall be tormented day and night for ever and ever.

    Context: Standard eschatological torment vocabulary: basanizo G928 ('shall be tormented'). Applied to the devil, beast, and false prophet — not to ordinary human dead. Luke 16 does NOT use basanizo.

    Notes: The vocabulary of Revelation's eschatological judgment (basanizo/basanismos) is consistently different from Luke 16's vocabulary (basanos/odunao).

    Lazarus Introduced — Named Character¶

    And there was a certain beggar named Lazarus, which was laid at his gate, full of sores,

    Context: Introduction of Lazarus — the only named character in any of Jesus' parables. The name means 'God helps' (from Hebrew Eleazar). The rich man, by contrast, is NOT named despite being described in detail. This inversion (the helpless named, the powerful anonymous) is thematic, not historical.

    Notes: Steelman for literal reading: named characters suggest historical persons. Counter: Ezekiel 23:4 names Aholah and Aholibah in allegory. The name is thematic ('God helps'), and Nave's does not list this Lazarus as historical.

    Lazarus's Condition — Dogs and Crumbs¶

    And desiring to be fed with the crumbs which fell from the rich man's table: moreover the dogs came and licked his sores.

    Context: Lazarus's extreme destitution: desiring crumbs, afflicted with sores licked by dogs. The contrast between his condition and the rich man's purple/linen/feasting is the setup for the reversal motif.

    Notes: The dogs licking sores is vivid parabolic detail that heightens the contrast and sets up the reversal in vv.22-25.

    Rich Man in Hades — Torment Vocabulary¶

    And in hell he lift up his eyes, being in torments, and seeth Abraham afar off, and Lazarus in his bosom.

    Context: Parser confirms: en to Haide (G86, N-DSM locative dative) + en basanois (G931, N-DPF dative plural of basanos). The rich man is in hades (not geenna), in basanois (not basanizo). Kolpos (G2859, N-DPM) for Abraham's bosom appears only here and v.22 as afterlife destination.

    Notes: Key vocabulary: basanois = dative plural of basanos G931 (touchstone/torment), NOT basanizo G928 (standard eschatological torment verb). Hades = temporary realm (Rev 20:13-14), NOT geenna (final judgment place).

    Great Chasm — Hapax Legomenon¶

    And beside all this, between us and you there is a great gulf fixed: so that they which would pass from hence to you cannot; neither can they pass to us, that [would come] from thence.

    Context: Chasma (G5490, hapax legomenon) + mega (G3173) + esteriktai (G4741 sterizo, V-RPI-3S Perfect Passive Indicative — permanently established). The perfect tense indicates a completed, permanent establishment. This unique vocabulary appears nowhere else in biblical afterlife teaching.

    Notes: Chasma is a hapax legomenon — it occurs only in this single verse in the entire NT. Unique vocabulary in a passage with multiple parabolic indicators.

    Five Brothers — Place of Torment¶

    For I have five brethren; that he may testify unto them, lest they also come into this place of torment.

    Context: The rich man mentions five brothers — a specific narrative detail with no historical verification possible. 'This place of torment' = topos tou basanou (G5117 + G931 genitive). The narrative builds toward the climactic teaching point about Scripture's sufficiency.

    Notes: In parables, specific numerical details (five brothers, hundred sheep, ten coins) serve the narrative purpose, not historical documentation.

    CLIMACTIC: Neither Persuaded Though One Rose from Dead¶

    And he said unto him, If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead.

    Context: Parser: Ei (G1487 conditional) + ouk akouousin (G191, V-PAI-3P present active indicative — factual condition: 'they do not hear') + oude ean tis ek nekron anaste (G450, V-2AAS-3S aorist active subjunctive — hypothetical: 'should rise') + peisthesontai (G3982, V-FPI-3P future passive indicative — 'they will not be persuaded'). Protasis: present indicative (factual). Apodosis: future passive of peitho. Ironic foreshadowing: Lazarus DID rise (John 11:43-44); response was murder plot (John 12:10-11).

    Notes: The passage's culminating verse. The teaching is about the sufficiency of Scripture and the hardness of unbelief — not about afterlife geography.

    Pharisees Murmur — Discourse Audience¶

    And the Pharisees and scribes murmured, saying, This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them.

    Context: Establishes the Pharisees and scribes as the audience for the discourse that follows. Their murmuring about Jesus receiving sinners is the occasion for the entire parable sequence in Luke 15-16.

    Notes: The Pharisees remain present throughout the discourse (explicitly reacting at 16:14). This audience continuity is evidence that 16:19-31 is part of the same parabolic discourse.

    Parabole Label — Singular Governs Discourse¶

    And he spake this parable unto them, saying,

    Context: Parser confirms: ten parabolen (G3850, N-ASF singular accusative) tauten (G3778, D-ASF demonstrative 'this'). Singular parabole with demonstrative tauten introduces the discourse unit. Three stories follow in chapter 15 under this single label. No discourse-closing formula appears before Luke 16:19.

    Notes: The singular 'this parable' introduces at minimum three stories (lost sheep, lost coin, prodigal son). Whether it extends to cover all five narratives (through 16:31) depends on whether a discourse break exists — and none is present.

    Standard Eschatological Torment Noun — basanismos¶

    And the smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever: and they have no rest day nor night, who worship the beast and his image, and whosoever receiveth the mark of his name.

    Context: Basanismos (G929) — the standard eschatological torment noun. All 6 of its NT occurrences are in Revelation. ABSENT from Luke 16. 'Smoke of their torment' (kapnos tou basanismou) — this is explicit eschatological judgment vocabulary that Luke 16 does not use.

    Notes: Combined with the absence of basanizo (G928) from Luke 16, the absence of basanismos (G929) confirms that Luke 16 uses a fundamentally different vocabulary register from standard eschatological judgment passages.

    Death and Hades Cast into Lake of Fire — Second Death¶

    And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death.

    Context: Hades is temporary — it is itself cast into the lake of fire at the final judgment. Death and hades are destroyed. 'This is the second death' identifies the lake of fire as the second death (thanatos, G2288).

    Notes: If hades is temporary and emptied at the final judgment (v.13) then cast into the lake of fire (v.14), Luke 16's depiction of the rich man 'in hades' cannot describe the final eschatological state.

    Jesus States Plainly: Lazarus Is Dead¶

    Then said Jesus unto them plainly, Lazarus is dead.

    Context: Jesus corrects the figurative language of v.11 ('sleepeth') with the literal statement 'Lazarus is dead.' This is the self-interpretation pattern: figurative language followed by literal correction. The historical Lazarus is from Bethany with wealthy sisters — a different figure from the destitute beggar of Luke 16.

    Notes: Self-interpretation pattern (see grammar reference): Jesus uses figurative 'sleep' then corrects to literal 'dead.' This pattern is relevant to how we read figurative afterlife imagery.

    Magnificat: Rich Sent Empty¶

    He hath filled the hungry with good things; and the rich he hath sent empty away.

    Context: Mary's Magnificat establishes Luke's wealth-reversal theology: hungry filled, rich sent empty. This is the same reversal pattern dramatized in the Rich Man and Lazarus parable (16:25).

    Notes: Lukan theology from the very beginning of the Gospel: wealth-reversal is a central theme, not an isolated idea in Luke 16.

    They Knew She Was Dead — Literal Death, Figurative Sleep¶

    And they laughed him to scorn, knowing that she was dead.

    Context: The mourners knew the girl was literally dead, yet Jesus called her condition 'sleep' (v.52). Same-author evidence (Luke) that death can be described figuratively without implying literal consciousness.

    Notes: Luke records both the figurative description ('sleepeth,' v.52) and the literal reality ('she was dead,' v.53). Luke understands the difference between figurative and literal language about death.

    All Live Unto Him — Resurrection, Not Intermediate State¶

    For he is not a God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him.

    Context: Jesus' argument about the resurrection uses Abraham to prove future bodily restoration, NOT current conscious intermediate existence. 'All live unto him' (pantes gar auto zosin) is eschatological/covenantal — God's relationship with Abraham guarantees resurrection.

    Notes: Jesus proves resurrection from the burning bush — the same Abraham used in Luke 16. The argument is about resurrection hope, not about conscious souls in Abraham's bosom.

    Many Believed Because of Lazarus¶

    Because that by reason of him many of the Jews went away, and believed on Jesus.

    Context: Many Jews believed because of the historical Lazarus's resurrection. Yet the leaders plotted murder (v.10) instead of repenting — fulfilling Luke 16:31's prediction that 'neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead.'

    Notes: Historical fulfillment of the parable's teaching point: one rising from the dead did not persuade the hardened leaders.

    Dead Know Not What Happens to Children¶

    His sons come to honour, and he knoweth [it] not; and they are brought low, but he perceiveth [it] not of them.

    Context: Job states that the dead do not know what happens to their children — they have no awareness of earthly events. Yet in Luke 16, the rich man is fully aware of his five brothers' spiritual condition and wants to warn them.

    Notes: The dead's unawareness of their children (Job 14:21) directly contrasts with the rich man's awareness of his brothers (Luke 16:27-28). The resolution: Luke 16 is parabolic, Job 14 is didactic.

    The Living Shall Praise — Not the Dead¶

    The living, the living, he shall praise thee, as I [do] this day: the father to the children shall make known thy truth.

    Context: Hezekiah's testimony: only the living praise God and make known his truth. The dead cannot (v.18). This is another didactic death-state passage contrasting the living with the silent dead.

    Notes: Paired with v.18 ('the grave cannot praise thee'). The living/dead contrast is consistent across the OT didactic tradition.

    Death Swallowed Up in Victory — At Resurrection¶

    So when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality, then shall be brought to pass the saying that is written, Death is swallowed up in victory.

    Context: Immortality (athanasia G110) and incorruption (aphtharsia G861) are 'put on' at the resurrection — a future event. 'Then' (tote) marks the temporal point: death is defeated at resurrection, not at the moment of individual death.

    Notes: If immortality is future (put on at resurrection), humans do not possess it between death and resurrection. This eliminates the mechanism for conscious intermediate existence.

    Rich Fool Moral: Not Rich Toward God¶

    So [is] he that layeth up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God.

    Context: The moral of the rich fool parable (same 'anthropos tis plousios' formula, explicitly labeled parabole at 12:16). The same moral applies to the rich man of Luke 16: earthly wealth without God-ward richness leads to judgment.

    Notes: The rich fool and the rich man of Luke 16 share the same formula, the same wealth theme, and the same judgment trajectory. The rich fool is explicitly labeled a parable.
