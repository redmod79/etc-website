    Detailed Analysis — Semantic Range of Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, Tartaroo¶

    Question¶

    Semantic range of Sheol (H7585), Hades (G86), Gehenna (G1067), Tartaroo (G5020). Interchangeability? Does any inherently mean eternal torment? LXX translation patterns.

    Verse-by-Verse Analysis¶

    FA1: Semantic Range of Sheol (H7585)¶

    Genesis 37:35 — Jacob expects to go to sheol mourning¶

    Context: Jacob believes Joseph is dead. A righteous patriarch expects to go to sheol to join his deceased son. Direct statement: Jacob, a righteous man, expects sheol as his destination after death. He goes there mourning — not because sheol is punishment, but because his son is there. Original language: Sheol (H7585). KJV translates as "grave." Jacob is righteous by any biblical standard. Cross-references: Genesis 42:38 uses identical language ("bring down my gray hairs with sorrow to the grave/sheol"). Psalm 16:10 shows Christ's soul going to sheol. Both righteous and wicked go to the same place. Relationship to other evidence: Establishes that sheol is NOT a place of punishment. If it were, Jacob's expectation of going there to be with his dead son would be incoherent.

    Genesis 42:38 — Jacob: gray hairs to sheol with sorrow¶

    Context: Jacob refuses to let Benjamin go to Egypt, fearing loss. Direct statement: Same sheol, same person (righteous Jacob), same expectation of going there. Original language: Sheol (H7585). KJV: "grave." Cross-references: Confirms Genesis 37:35 pattern. Relationship to other evidence: Reinforces sheol as a universal destination, not a punitive one.

    Numbers 16:30 — Korah swallowed alive into sheol¶

    Context: Divine judgment on Korah's rebellion. Moses declares that if the earth swallows them, it will prove God's displeasure. Direct statement: The earth opens and swallows Korah and his company into sheol. The result: "they perished from among the congregation" (v.33). Original language: Sheol (H7585), KJV: "pit." Verb "perished" = abad (H6) — destruction vocabulary. Cross-references: Numbers 16:33 confirms with abad. Psalm 55:15 echoes this event. Relationship to other evidence: Even in a judgment context, the outcome is perishing (abad), not ongoing torment. Sheol receives the judged, but the narrative verb is destruction.

    Numbers 16:33 — Korah and company go down into sheol¶

    Context: Continuation of the Korah narrative. The earth closes over them. Direct statement: They went down alive into sheol, the earth closed upon them, and they "perished" (abad) from among the congregation. Original language: Sheol (H7585), KJV: "pit." Abad (H6) = perish, be destroyed. Cross-references: Paired with Numbers 16:30. Relationship to other evidence: The abad-apollymi chain (LXX: abad → apollymi 141x) connects this to NT destruction vocabulary.

    Deuteronomy 32:22 — Fire burns to lowest sheol¶

    Context: Song of Moses — God's anger against unfaithful Israel. Direct statement: A fire kindled in God's anger burns to the lowest sheol and consumes the earth. Original language: Sheol (H7585), KJV: "lowest hell." The verb "consume" is akal (H398) — to eat, devour, consume. Cross-references: Fire + sheol combination. Fire's function is consuming (destruction), not preserving for torment. Relationship to other evidence: Consistent with fire as a consuming agent throughout Scripture. The fire destroys; it does not maintain life in suffering.

    1 Samuel 2:6 — The LORD brings down to sheol and raises up¶

    Context: Hannah's prayer celebrating God's sovereignty over life and death. Direct statement: God kills and makes alive; brings down to sheol and brings up. The parallelism: kill = bring to sheol; make alive = bring up. Original language: Sheol (H7585), KJV: "grave." Cross-references: The kill/sheol and alive/bring-up parallelism frames sheol as the state of death with resurrection hope. Relationship to other evidence: Sheol = death. The reversal of sheol is resurrection ("brings up"), not escape from a torture chamber.

    Job 7:9 — He that goes to sheol shall come up no more¶

    Context: Job laments human mortality from a pre-resurrection perspective. Direct statement: Like a cloud consumed and vanished, one who goes to sheol does not return. Original language: Sheol (H7585), KJV: "grave." Cloud "consumed" = kalah (H3615). Cross-references: Job's limited perspective here. Daniel 12:2 later reveals resurrection. Relationship to other evidence: From Job's vantage point, sheol is permanent death. The imagery — consumed like a cloud — is cessation.

    Job 11:8 — Deeper than sheol¶

    Context: Zophar describes God's wisdom as beyond human comprehension. Direct statement: Heaven-sheol spatial merism. Sheol = the deepest conceivable place. Original language: Sheol (H7585), KJV: "hell." Cross-references: Psalm 139:8; Amos 9:2 — same heaven-sheol merism pattern. Relationship to other evidence: Sheol functions as a spatial extreme (deepest), not a moral category (punishment). It is the opposite of heaven in position, not in moral quality.

    Job 14:13 — Hide me in sheol until wrath passes¶

    Context: Job's lament about human mortality. He wishes God would hide him in sheol temporarily. Direct statement: Job asks to be hidden in sheol until God's wrath passes, with a "set time" appointed. Original language: Sheol (H7585), KJV: "grave." "Until thy wrath be past" + "set time" = temporal duration. Cross-references: This framing is incompatible with sheol as a place of punishment. Job WANTS to go there as a refuge. Relationship to other evidence: Sheol as temporary holding place. A righteous person requests it as protection.

    Job 26:5-6 — Rephaim tremble; sheol naked before God; Abaddon uncovered¶

    Context: Job describes God's power over all creation, including the underworld. Direct statement: The rephaim (H7496, shades/dead ones) tremble under the waters. Sheol is naked before God; Abaddon (H11) has no covering. Original language: Three key terms: rephaim (H7496) = shades/dead; sheol (H7585); Abaddon (H11) = destruction. Cross-references: Sheol-Abaddon pairing (FA11). Proverbs 15:11, 27:20 use the same pair. Relationship to other evidence: Sheol's companion term is Abaddon (destruction), not torment. The inhabitants (rephaim) are characterized by weakness and feebleness, not conscious suffering.

    Psalm 6:5 — No remembrance in death; no praise in sheol¶

    Context: David's prayer in distress — psalm of lament. Direct statement: In death there is no remembrance of God; in sheol no one gives thanks. Original language: Sheol (H7585), KJV: "grave." Didactic psalm — direct teaching about conditions in sheol. Cross-references: Psalm 88:10-12, Psalm 115:17, Ecclesiastes 9:5-10 — consistent characterization. Relationship to other evidence: One of the clearest E-tier statements about sheol's nature. No remembrance and no praise require unconsciousness. This is didactic genre, not metaphor.

    Psalm 9:17 — The wicked turned into sheol¶

    Context: Psalm of praise for God's justice. Direct statement: The wicked shall be turned into sheol, and all nations that forget God. Original language: Sheol (H7585), KJV: "hell." Same word translated "grave" in Psalm 6:5. Cross-references: The wicked go to the same sheol as Jacob (Gen 37:35), David (Ps 86:13), and Christ (Ps 16:10). Relationship to other evidence: The KJV translates sheol as "hell" here because the wicked are the subject, but as "grave" when righteous individuals are the subject. The Hebrew makes no such distinction.

    Psalm 16:10 — God will not leave my soul in sheol¶

Original language: Sheol (H7585), shachath (H7845, corruption/pit), KJV: "hell." Cross-references: Acts 2:27 quotes this with hades (G86) for sheol and diaphthora (G1312) for shachath. This is the single most important passage for establishing sheol-hades equivalence. Relationship to other evidence: Christ went to sheol/hades. If sheol were a place of punishment for the wicked, Christ's presence there creates a theological contradiction.

    Psalm 49:14-15 — Sheep laid in sheol; God redeems from sheol¶

    Context: Wisdom psalm contrasting the foolish rich and the righteous. Direct statement: The foolish are laid in sheol like sheep; death feeds on them (v.14). Cross-references: The feeding/consumption metaphor appears in Isaiah 5:14 (sheol swallowing). Relationship to other evidence: Death "feeding" on the wicked is consumption language. The righteous hope is redemption FROM sheol — resurrection.

    Psalm 55:15 — Let them go down alive into sheol¶

    Context: David's imprecation against enemies. Direct statement: David invokes the Korah precedent — going down alive into sheol. Original language: Sheol (H7585), KJV: "hell." Chayyim (H2416) = alive/quick. Cross-references: Echoes Numbers 16:30,33. Relationship to other evidence: Imprecatory genre. David calls for the same judgment that fell on Korah.     Psalm 86:13 — Delivered from the lowest sheol¶

Original language: Sheol tachtiy (lowest sheol), KJV: "lowest hell." Cross-references: If sheol were exclusively for the wicked, why does righteous David need deliverance from it? Relationship to other evidence: David's deliverance FROM sheol means rescue from death, not escape from a torture chamber. This confirms sheol = death.

    Psalm 88:10-12 — No wonders, lovingkindness, or praise in sheol¶

    Context: The darkest psalm — lament with no resolution. Direct statement: Comprehensive negation: no wonders for the dead, no praise from the dead, no lovingkindness declared in sheol, no faithfulness in Abaddon, no wonders known in the dark, no righteousness in the land of forgetfulness. Original language: Sheol (v.11 "grave") paralleled with Abaddon ("destruction"), dark, land of forgetfulness. Four rhetorical questions, all expecting "no." Cross-references: Psalm 6:5, 115:17, Ecclesiastes 9:5-10. Relationship to other evidence: The most comprehensive negation of any activity in sheol. Parallels sheol with Abaddon (destruction) and characterizes it as darkness and forgetfulness.

    Psalm 115:17 — The dead praise not the LORD¶

    Context: Psalm contrasting the living who praise with the dead who cannot. Direct statement: The dead do not praise the LORD, nor do those who go down into silence. Original language: Sheol characterized as "silence" (dumah). KJV uses "silence" rather than translating the underlying concept of sheol. Cross-references: Psalm 6:5, 88:10-12 — consistent. Relationship to other evidence: Silence, not torment. Consistent across multiple psalms by different authors.

    Psalm 139:8 — If I make my bed in sheol, thou art there¶

Heaven-sheol merism. Cross-references: Job 11:8, Amos 9:2 — same merism pattern. Relationship to other evidence: The point is God's omnipresence, not sheol's nature. Sheol serves as the spatial opposite of heaven.

    Psalm 141:7 — Bones scattered at sheol's mouth¶

Peh (H6310) = mouth. Cross-references: Highly physical imagery — bones, mouth, wood splitting. Relationship to other evidence: No possibility of reading this as a place of conscious torment. The imagery is entirely physical — grave, bones, decay.

Relationship to other evidence: The inhabitants of sheol are rephaim — weak, powerless shades, not conscious tormented beings.

    Song of Solomon 8:6 — Jealousy cruel as sheol¶

    Context: Love poetry — the power of love. Direct statement: Love is strong as death; jealousy is cruel as sheol (the grave). Original language: Death-sheol parallelism. KJV: "grave." Its characteristic is irresistible power (like death), not torment.

Relationship to other evidence: Physical imagery: weapons, swords, bones. This is burial imagery, not a description of conscious torment.

    FA2: Semantic Range of Hades (G86)¶

    Matthew 11:23 — Capernaum brought down to hades¶

    Context: Jesus pronounces woe on unrepentant cities. Direct statement: Capernaum, exalted to heaven, will be brought down to hades. Original language: Hades (G86). Heaven-hades merism. Cross-references: Echoes Isaiah 14:13-15 (heaven-sheol merism for king of Babylon). Luke 10:15 is the Synoptic parallel. Relationship to other evidence: Hades = humiliation/judgment. The subject is a city, not individual humans. The language mirrors Isaiah's use of sheol for the fall of the proud.

    Matthew 16:18 — Gates of hades shall not prevail¶

    Context: Peter's confession at Caesarea Philippi. Direct statement: The gates of hades will not prevail against Christ's church. Original language: Hades (G86). "Gates of hades" = the power of death. Cross-references: The "gates" metaphor connects to ancient Near Eastern imagery of the gates of the underworld. Relationship to other evidence: Hades represents the power of death — death will not overcome the church. This is power-of-death language, not torment language.

    Luke 10:15 — Capernaum thrust down to hades¶

    Context: Synoptic parallel to Matthew 11:23. Direct statement: Same hades usage for the humiliation of an unrepentant city. Original language: Hades (G86). Cross-references: Matthew 11:23. Relationship to other evidence: Confirms the humiliation/judgment semantic range.

    Luke 16:23 — Rich man in hades, in torments¶

    Context: Parable of the Rich Man and Lazarus. Part of a series of parables in Luke 15-16. Direct statement: In hades the rich man lifts his eyes, being in torments (basanois), and sees Abraham with Lazarus. Original language: Hades (G86) + basanos (G931, touchstone/torment). Greek parsing confirms basanois as dative plural of basanos. Cross-references: This is the ONLY passage in all of Scripture associating hades with consciousness or torment. Every other hades/sheol passage in didactic contexts characterizes the realm of the dead by unconsciousness and silence. Relationship to other evidence: Genre is determinative. This is a parable — part of a series (lost sheep, lost coin, prodigal son, unjust steward, rich man and Lazarus). It fails Tree 3 Gate 1 (subject is a parabolic character, not a literal human) and Gate 3 (genre is parabolic, not didactic). One parable cannot override 65+ consistent didactic passages characterizing sheol/hades as a state of unconsciousness.

    Acts 2:27 — Peter quotes Ps 16:10: soul not left in hades¶

    Context: Peter's Pentecost sermon quoting Psalm 16:10 from the LXX. Direct statement: "Thou wilt not leave my soul in hades, neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption." Original language: Psyche (G5590, soul) + hades (G86) + diaphthora (G1312, corruption). Direct quotation replacing sheol (H7585) with hades (G86). Cross-references: Psalm 16:10 (OT source). Acts 2:31 (Peter's interpretation). Relationship to other evidence: The single most important passage for establishing sheol-hades equivalence. Peter, under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, treats hades as the Greek equivalent of sheol.

    Acts 2:31 — Christ's soul not left in hades¶

    Context: Peter's interpretation of Psalm 16:10. Direct statement: David spoke of the resurrection of Christ — his soul was not left in hades, his flesh did not see corruption. Original language: Hades (G86). Cross-references: Confirms Acts 2:27. Relationship to other evidence: Christ went to hades. If hades were a place of punishment for the wicked, Christ's presence there creates the same theological problem as with sheol. Hades is the general abode of all the dead — temporary, preceding resurrection.

    1 Corinthians 15:55 — O death, thy sting; O grave, thy victory¶

    Context: Paul's resurrection chapter climax, quoting Hosea 13:14. Direct statement: "O death, where is thy sting? O grave [hades], where is thy victory?" Original language: Hades (G86), KJV: "grave." Third NT confirmation of sheol-hades equivalence (Paul quotes an OT sheol passage using hades). Cross-references: Hosea 13:14 — sheol in the Hebrew. Relationship to other evidence: Hades is a defeated enemy, triumphed over through resurrection. It is not an eternal state.

    Revelation 1:18 — Keys of hades and death¶

    Context: Christ's self-identification to John. Direct statement: Christ was dead, is alive forevermore, and holds the keys of hades and death. Original language: Hades (G86) + thanatos (G2288). Hades-death pairing mirrors OT sheol-maveth. Cross-references: The hades-thanatos pairing continues the OT sheol-maveth pattern. Relationship to other evidence: Christ holds authority over both death and hades. Keys = power over. Hades is a domain Christ has conquered, not a domain He administers as a torture chamber.

    Revelation 6:8 — Death and Hades follow the pale horse¶

    Context: Fourth seal — apocalyptic vision. Direct statement: Death rides the pale horse; hades follows. Their power is to kill — by sword, hunger, death, and beasts. Original language: Hades (G86) personified alongside thanatos (G2288). Cross-references: Same hades-thanatos pairing as Revelation 1:18. Relationship to other evidence: The power granted is "to kill" — death vocabulary. Hades follows death as the receptacle of the dead.

    Revelation 20:13-14 — Death and hades give up dead; cast into lake of fire¶

    Context: Great White Throne judgment. Direct statement: The sea, death, and hades deliver up the dead. They are judged. Then death and hades are cast into the lake of fire. "This is the second death." Original language: Hades (G86), thanatos (G2288), limne (G3041, lake), pyr (G4442, fire), deuteros (G1208, second). Greek parsing: eblethesan (aorist passive of ballo = were cast). Cross-references: Revelation 21:8 — lake of fire = second death. Relationship to other evidence: Hades is TEMPORARY. It delivers up the dead (empties), then is itself destroyed by being cast into the lake of fire. The lake of fire is identified as "the second death" — death vocabulary, not torment vocabulary. Hades and the lake of fire are distinct: one is cast INTO the other.

    FA3: Semantic Range of Gehenna (G1067)¶

    Matthew 5:22 — In danger of gehenna of fire¶

    Context: Sermon on the Mount — intensifying the law on murder/anger. Direct statement: Escalating warnings: judgment → council → gehenna of fire. Original language: Geennan tou pyros (G1067 + G4442) = gehenna of fire. Greek parsing confirms genitive construction. Cross-references: First NT gehenna reference. Fire is gehenna's defining characteristic. Relationship to other evidence: Jesus introduces gehenna as the ultimate consequence in an escalating series. The term is "gehenna of fire," not "gehenna of torment."

    Matthew 5:29-30 — Whole body cast into gehenna¶

    Context: Sermon on the Mount — lust and radical self-denial. Direct statement: Better for one member to perish (apollymi G622) than for the whole body to be cast into gehenna. Original language: The verb for the alternative is apollymi (G622, to perish/destroy). The body is "cast" (ballo) — disposal language. Cross-references: The same apollymi-gehenna combination as Matt 10:28. Relationship to other evidence: The member "perishes" — destruction vocabulary. The body is "cast" into gehenna like refuse is disposed of. No torment vocabulary appears.

    Matthew 10:28 — Destroy soul and body in gehenna¶

    Context: Jesus sends out the twelve — instructions about whom to fear. Direct statement: Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul. Fear the one who can destroy (apolesai, from apollymi G622) both soul and body in gehenna. Original language: Greek parsing: apolesai = aorist active infinitive of apollymi (G622). Psyche (G5590, soul) + soma (G4983, body) + geenne (G1067, gehenna). Cross-references: Luke 12:5 adds that God kills first, THEN casts into gehenna. Relationship to other evidence: This is the most explicit gehenna verse. The verb is apollymi (destroy), NOT basanizo (torment). God DESTROYS both soul and body in gehenna. If gehenna were a place of eternal conscious torment, "destroy" would be the wrong word. The soul (psyche) is not indestructible.

    Matthew 18:9 — Cast into gehenna fire vs. enter life¶

    Context: Discourse on stumbling blocks. Direct statement: Better to enter life with one eye than to have two eyes and be cast into gehenna fire. Original language: Gehenna (G1067) + pyr (G4442). The contrast: "enter into life" vs. gehenna fire. Cross-references: Mark 9:43,45,47 — same contrast pattern. Relationship to other evidence: The contrast is consistently life vs. gehenna, not bliss vs. torment. If gehenna meant eternal conscious existence in torment, the opposite would not be "life" but some other quality of life.

    Matthew 23:15 — Child of gehenna¶

    Context: Seven woes against scribes and Pharisees. Direct statement: They make a proselyte "twofold more the child of gehenna" than themselves. Original language: "Son of gehenna" (huios geennes) = Semitic idiom of characterization. Cross-references: Like "sons of light" or "children of wrath" — an idiom describing a person's character or destiny. Relationship to other evidence: Metaphorical use. The idiom characterizes a person's nature/destiny, not a literal location.

    Matthew 23:33 — How escape the damnation of gehenna¶

    Context: Final woe — climax of condemnation against scribes and Pharisees. Direct statement: "How can you escape the damnation of gehenna?" Original language: Krisis (G2920) = judgment, not "damnation" in the modern sense. Gehenna (G1067). Cross-references: The question is about escaping judgment (krisis), not about escaping a place of torment. Relationship to other evidence: The concept is judgment and its consequence, not a specific description of what gehenna involves.

    Mark 9:43-48 — Gehenna: worm not die, fire not quenched¶

    Context: Jesus on stumbling blocks — triple repetition of the gehenna warning. Direct statement: Better to enter life maimed than to go to gehenna — where the worm dies not and the fire is not quenched. Triple repetition (vv.44,46,48). Original language: Gehenna (G1067) + asbestos (G762, unquenchable) + skolex (G4663, worm). Jesus quotes Isaiah 66:24. Cross-references: Isaiah 66:24 — the source text. There, the subjects are peger (H6297, corpses/carcasses) — NEVER used for living persons. Relationship to other evidence: The OT source identifies the subjects as dead bodies (peger), not living beings. The worm (tolaath/skolex) is a decomposition agent feeding on corpses. "Unquenchable fire" in OT usage means fire that cannot be extinguished before completing its work (cf. Jeremiah 17:27 — Jerusalem's gates burned with unquenchable fire; they are not still burning). The contrast remains "enter life" vs. gehenna.

    Luke 12:5 — Fear him who after killing has power to cast into gehenna¶

    Context: Jesus on fear of God vs. fear of man. Direct statement: Fear the one who, after killing, has power (exousian) to cast into gehenna. Original language: Apokteinai (G615, to kill) + embalein (from ballo G906, to cast into) + geennan (G1067). Cross-references: Synoptic parallel to Matt 10:28. Luke adds the temporal sequence: God kills FIRST, THEN casts into gehenna. Relationship to other evidence: The sequence matters. God kills first, then casts into gehenna. If gehenna is where conscious beings are tormented, the killing step is redundant. The sequence suggests: death, then disposal in gehenna.

    James 3:6 — Tongue set on fire of gehenna¶

    Context: James on the tongue's destructive power. Direct statement: The tongue is a fire, a world of iniquity, set on fire of gehenna. Original language: Gehenna (G1067). Only non-Jesus gehenna usage in the NT. Cross-references: Purely metaphorical: gehenna's fire represents destructive power. Relationship to other evidence: Metaphorical use — the tongue's destructive power is compared to gehenna's fire. This tells us gehenna's dominant association is fire and destruction.

    FA4: Tartaroo (G5020) — Single Occurrence Analysis¶

    2 Peter 2:4 — Angels cast down to tartaroo¶

    Context: 2 Peter's chain of three OT judgment examples (angels, Flood, Sodom). Direct statement: God did not spare the angels that sinned but cast them down to tartaroo, delivering them into chains of darkness, reserved unto judgment. Original language: Tartarosas (G5020) — aorist active participle. A verb, not a noun. Seirois zophou (chains of darkness). Krisin teroumenous (being reserved unto judgment). Cross-references: Jude 1:6 parallels with "everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day." Relationship to other evidence: Tartaroo is applied exclusively to fallen angels, never to humans. It describes temporary imprisonment ("reserved unto judgment"), not final punishment. The term is a hapax legomenon — used once in all of Scripture.

    Jude 1:6 — Angels in everlasting chains under darkness unto judgment¶

    Context: Jude parallels 2 Peter 2:4 with the same angelic imprisonment. Direct statement: Angels who left their proper habitation are reserved in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day. Original language: Desmois aidiois (G1199 + G126) = everlasting chains. Zophon (G2217) = darkness. "Unto the judgment of the great day" = temporal limit. Cross-references: 2 Peter 2:4 — same event. Relationship to other evidence: The chains are "everlasting" (aidios G126), but the purpose is "unto judgment" — temporal. The permanence describes the security of the imprisonment, not an eternal state. The angels await judgment; their current state is not the final disposition.

    Jude 1:7 — Sodom suffering vengeance of eternal fire¶

    Context: Jude uses Sodom as an example of "eternal fire." Direct statement: Sodom and Gomorrah are set forth as an example, suffering the vengeance of eternal fire. Original language: Pyros aioniou (G4442 + G166) = eternal fire. Deigma (G1164) = example. Hypechousai (G5254, present participle) = undergoing. Cross-references: Isaiah 34:9-10 — same "eternal fire" language for Edom's destruction. Relationship to other evidence: Sodom is "set forth for an example" — the ruins of Sodom demonstrate what "eternal fire" produces. Sodom is not still burning. The fire completed its work; the results are permanent. This establishes the OT/NT convention: "eternal fire" language describes the finality of destruction, not the duration of burning.

    FA5: LXX Translation Patterns — Sheol to Greek¶

The secondary translations are thanatos (death), katabaino (to descend), katago (to lead down), and apoleia (destruction). None of these secondary translations involves torment vocabulary.

    Hosea 13:14 → 1 Corinthians 15:55 — Sheol as hades in triumph¶

Relationship to other evidence: Second LXX-confirmed sheol-hades mapping in the NT. The context is God's triumph over death/hades through resurrection.

    Isaiah 38:18 — Sheol and praise¶

    FA6: Interchangeability — Can These Words Substitute for Each Other?¶

    Interchangeability Test¶

    The study question asks whether these four words are interchangeable. The test is simple: can you substitute one word for another in context without producing absurdity?

    Test 1: Genesis 37:35 — Jacob expects to go to [sheol] mourning to join his dead son. - Substitute gehenna: "I will go down to gehenna mourning." A righteous patriarch expects fiery destruction? Absurd. - Substitute tartaroo: "I will go down to tartaroo." A human expects angelic imprisonment? Absurd. - Result: Sheol is NOT interchangeable with gehenna or tartaroo.

    Test 2: Psalm 6:5 — In death there is no remembrance; in [sheol] who shall give thee thanks? - Substitute gehenna: "In gehenna who shall give thee thanks?" If gehenna is eternal conscious torment, the occupants COULD give thanks (they are conscious). The verse requires unconsciousness. - Result: Sheol is NOT interchangeable with gehenna.

    Test 3: Matthew 10:28 — Fear him who can destroy soul and body in [gehenna]. - Substitute hades: "Destroy soul and body in hades." But hades is temporary (Rev 20:14) — it delivers up the dead and is then destroyed. Gehenna is a final destination. - Substitute sheol: "Destroy soul and body in sheol." But sheol receives both righteous and wicked, and sheol is where God can "bring up" from (1 Sam 2:6). Gehenna has no rescue. - Result: Gehenna is NOT interchangeable with sheol/hades.

    Test 4: 2 Peter 2:4 — God cast angels down to [tartaroo]. - Substitute sheol: "God cast angels down to sheol." But sheol is for human dead. - Substitute gehenna: "God cast angels down to gehenna." But gehenna is not described as a place of imprisonment; it is a place of destruction. - Result: Tartaroo is NOT interchangeable with sheol, hades, or gehenna.

    Test 5: Revelation 20:13-14 — Death and [hades] delivered up the dead and were cast into the lake of fire. - Substitute gehenna: "Death and gehenna delivered up the dead." But gehenna is a final destination, not a temporary holding area. - Result: Hades is NOT interchangeable with gehenna.

    These substitution tests confirm that the four words designate four distinct concepts, not a single unified "hell."

    FA7: Does Any Word Inherently Mean Eternal Conscious Torment?¶

    Sheol (H7585) — Does it mean eternal torment?¶

    The word study shows sheol appearing 68 times. No lexicon defines sheol as a place of eternal conscious torment. Its semantic range: realm of the dead, the grave, the underworld. Characterized by unconsciousness (Ps 6:5; Ecc 9:10), silence (Ps 115:17), darkness (Ps 88:12), and forgetfulness (Ps 88:12). Receives both righteous and wicked. Paired with Abaddon (destruction), not with torment vocabulary. The LXX never translates sheol with a torment-related Greek word. Answer: No.

    Hades (G86) — Does it mean eternal torment?¶

    11 occurrences. Semantic equivalent of sheol (confirmed by LXX mapping and Acts 2:27,31). Temporary — cast into the lake of fire (Rev 20:14). Paired with death, not torment. The only association with consciousness comes from Luke 16:23, a parable (fails Gate 1 and Gate 3). No lexicon defines hades as "a place of eternal conscious torment." Answer: No.

    Gehenna (G1067) — Does it mean eternal torment?¶

    12 occurrences. Jesus' vocabulary in gehenna passages: apollymi (destroy), not basanizo (torment). Matt 10:28: "destroy both soul and body." The contrast is "enter into life" vs. gehenna (life vs. destruction). The OT background (Isaiah 66:24): subjects are corpses (peger), consumed by worm and fire. No gehenna passage applies torment vocabulary to human beings. No lexicon defines gehenna as "a place of eternal conscious torment" — the standard lexical entry is "Valley of Hinnom; place of final punishment." Answer: No.

    Tartaroo (G5020) — Does it mean eternal torment?¶

    1 occurrence. Applied only to angels. Temporary ("reserved unto judgment"). A verb describing the act of casting down, not a state of torment. Answer: No.

    Summary¶

    None of the four words translated "hell" in the KJV inherently means eternal conscious torment. The semantic ranges are: sheol/hades = realm of the dead (temporary, unconscious); gehenna = place of final destruction; tartaroo = angelic imprisonment (temporary).

    FA8: The "Grave" vs. "Hell" Translation Controversy¶

    The KJV translates sheol (H7585) as both "grave" and "hell." The distribution reveals a pattern:

    Translated "grave" when the subject is righteous or neutral: - Genesis 37:35: Jacob → "grave" - Genesis 42:38: Jacob → "grave" - 1 Samuel 2:6: Hannah's prayer → "grave" - Job 14:13: Job → "grave" - Psalm 49:14-15: Wisdom psalm → "grave" - Psalm 141:7: Physical burial → "grave" - Ecclesiastes 9:10: Solomon → "grave" - Song of Solomon 8:6: Love poetry → "grave" - Isaiah 38:18: Hezekiah → "grave"

    Translated "hell" when the subject is wicked or in a judgment context: - Psalm 9:17: Wicked → "hell" - Psalm 55:15: Enemies → "hell" - Proverbs 15:24: Foolish → "hell" - Isaiah 5:14: Jerusalem's wicked → "hell" - Isaiah 14:9,15: King of Babylon → "hell" - Deuteronomy 32:22: Judgment → "lowest hell"

    The underlying Hebrew is the same word — sheol. The KJV translators made a theological decision, not a linguistic one. When righteous people go to sheol, it becomes "the grave." When wicked people go to sheol, it becomes "hell." The Hebrew makes no such distinction.

    FA9: Who Goes to Sheol/Hades?¶

    Righteous individuals who go to or expect sheol: - Jacob (Gen 37:35; 42:38; 44:29) — patriarch, righteous - David (Ps 86:13; 88:3) — "man after God's own heart" - Job (Job 14:13; 17:13) — declared righteous by God (Job 1:8) - Hezekiah (Isa 38:10,18) — righteous king - Christ (Ps 16:10 → Acts 2:27,31) — sinless

    Wicked individuals who go to sheol: - Korah (Num 16:30,33) — rebel - The wicked generally (Ps 9:17; 49:14) - King of Babylon (Isa 14:9,15) - Nations (Ezek 31:16-17; 32:21,27)

    Conclusion: Sheol receives both righteous and wicked. It is the universal destination of the dead, not a place of punishment exclusively for the wicked. This is confirmed by the NT: Christ went to hades (Acts 2:27,31).

    FA10: Gehenna's OT Background — Valley of Hinnom and Child Sacrifice¶

    The Valley of Hinnom (ge-Hinnom → gehenna) follows a trajectory across the OT:

    Stage 1 — Geography: - Joshua 15:8: Boundary marker for Judah. Pure geographic reference. - Joshua 18:16: Boundary marker for Benjamin. Same. - Nehemiah 11:30: Post-exilic settlement reference. Still geographic.

    Stage 2 — Abomination (child sacrifice): - 2 Chronicles 28:3: Ahaz burns children in the Valley of Hinnom. - 2 Chronicles 33:6: Manasseh passes children through fire. - 2 Kings 23:10: Josiah defiles Topheth to end the practice. - Jeremiah 32:35: Molech worship in the Valley of Hinnom — "abomination" God never commanded.

    Stage 3 — Prophetic curse: - Jeremiah 7:31-32: Valley renamed "Valley of Slaughter" — burial ground for corpses. - Jeremiah 19:2-6: Same renaming. Slaughter and burial. - Isaiah 30:33: Topheth ordained of old — fire and brimstone imagery for military defeat. - Isaiah 66:24: Carcasses (peger) consumed by worm and unquenchable fire.

    Key observations: 1. The valley's association is with slaughter, death, and corpse disposal — not with ongoing conscious torment. 2. Jeremiah's prophetic name is "Valley of Slaughter" (haregah), not "Valley of Torment." 3. God's condemnation of the child sacrifice there is emphatic: He "commanded not" this and it never "came into my mind" (Jer 7:31; 32:35). 4. Isaiah 66:24's subjects are "carcasses" (peger) — dead bodies. The worm and fire act on corpses.

    FA11: Sheol-Abaddon Pairing Pattern¶

    The OT pairs sheol with Abaddon (H11, destruction) as companion terms:

    • Job 26:6: "Sheol is naked before him, and Abaddon has no covering."
    • Job 28:22: "Abaddon and death say, We have heard the fame thereof."
    • Job 31:12: "For it is a fire that consumes to Abaddon." (Fire + Abaddon = complete consumption.)
    • Psalm 88:11: "Shall thy lovingkindness be declared in the grave [sheol]? or thy faithfulness in Abaddon [destruction]?"
    • Proverbs 15:11: "Sheol and Abaddon are before the LORD."
    • Proverbs 27:20: "Sheol and Abaddon are never satisfied."
    • Revelation 9:11: "The angel of the bottomless pit, whose name in the Hebrew tongue is Abaddon, but in the Greek tongue hath his name Apollyon." (Abaddon = Apollyon = Destroyer, from apollymi.)

    The sheol-Abaddon pairing associates the realm of the dead with DESTRUCTION. Abaddon is an intensive form of abad (H6, to perish, destroy). Its LXX equivalent is apoleia (G684, destruction), and in Revelation 9:11 it is explicitly equated with Apollyon (Destroyer). The Hebrew authors consistently paired sheol with destruction vocabulary, not torment vocabulary.

    FA12: Hades vs. Lake of Fire — The Distinction¶

    The biblical framework shows a sequence: temporary holding (sheol/hades) → judgment → final outcome (lake of fire = second death).

    • Revelation 1:18: Christ has keys of hades and death — authority over the temporary realm.
    • Revelation 6:8: Death and hades personified together — their power is "to kill."
    • Revelation 19:20: Beast and false prophet cast into lake of fire (symbolic entities).
    • Revelation 20:10: Devil cast into lake of fire where beast and false prophet are. Subjects of torment (basanizo G928): devil, beast, false prophet — supernatural/symbolic entities.
    • Revelation 20:13-14: Death and hades deliver up the dead → judgment → death and hades cast into lake of fire = "the second death."
    • Revelation 20:15: Whoever not found in book of life cast into lake of fire — NO torment vocabulary. The lake of fire = "the second death" (21:8).
    • Revelation 21:8: Lake of fire = "the second death" — death vocabulary applied to human fate.

    The distinction between hades and the lake of fire is explicit: hades delivers up the dead (it empties), is then cast into the lake of fire (it is destroyed). They are sequential, not identical. The lake of fire is identified as "the second death" (thanatos, death vocabulary).

    When the devil, beast, and false prophet encounter the lake of fire, the text uses torment vocabulary (basanizo, Rev 20:10). When humans encounter the lake of fire, the text uses death vocabulary ("second death," Rev 20:14; 21:8). This vocabulary distinction tracks consistently.

    Patterns Identified¶

    Pattern 1: Sheol/Hades = Unconscious, Temporary Realm of All Dead¶

    Eight or more OT authors across multiple genres describe sheol as characterized by unconsciousness, silence, darkness, no work, no knowledge, no wisdom, no praise, and forgetfulness. Both righteous and wicked go there. The LXX translates sheol as hades 58 times. Acts 2:27,31 and 1 Corinthians 15:55 confirm this equivalence in the NT. Hades is temporary — cast into the lake of fire (Rev 20:14).

    Pattern 2: Gehenna = Final Destruction (Not Torment)¶

    In all 12 gehenna occurrences, the vocabulary is destruction (apollymi), not torment (basanizo). The contrast is "enter life" vs. gehenna. The OT background (Isaiah 66:24) describes corpses (peger), not living beings. The prophetic name for the Valley of Hinnom is "Valley of Slaughter," not "Valley of Torment."

    Pattern 3: Four Distinct Concepts, Not One¶

    Sheol/hades = general realm of the dead (temporary). Gehenna = eschatological destruction (final). Tartaroo = angelic imprisonment (temporary). Lake of fire = second death (final, for all enemies of God). These cannot be substituted for one another without producing absurdity.

    Pattern 4: Torment Vocabulary Restricted to Non-Human Subjects¶

    Basanizo appears in Revelation 20:10 — subjects are devil, beast, false prophet. When humans encounter the lake of fire (Rev 20:15; 21:8), the text uses "second death." Basanizo never co-occurs with gehenna in any passage.

    Pattern 5: "Eternal Fire/Smoke" = OT Destruction Formula¶

    Isaiah 34:9-10 (Edom's "smoke ascending forever") is the OT source for Revelation 14:11 and 19:3. Edom is not still burning. Sodom "suffered the vengeance of eternal fire" (Jude 1:7) but is not still burning. The formula describes the finality and completeness of destruction.

    Pattern 6: The Perish/Life Contrast¶

    John 3:16: perish vs. everlasting life. Romans 6:23: death vs. eternal life. Matthew 7:13-14: destruction vs. life. Gehenna passages: gehenna vs. "enter into life." The contrast is consistently cessation/destruction vs. life, not torment vs. bliss.

    Pattern 7: KJV Translation Obscures Distinctions¶

    The KJV translates four distinct Hebrew/Greek words as "hell," creating a unified concept that does not exist in the original languages. It further translates sheol as "grave" for righteous subjects and "hell" for wicked subjects — a theological, not linguistic, decision.

    Word Study Integration¶

    Sheol (H7585) — 68 occurrences¶

    The word study confirms: realm of the dead, paired with death (maveth), Abaddon, pit. LXX: hades 58 times. Receives both righteous and wicked. No lexicon defines sheol as "a place of eternal conscious torment."

    Hades (G86) — 11 occurrences¶

    Semantic equivalent of sheol. Temporary (cast into lake of fire). Only passage associating it with consciousness is Luke 16:23 (parable genre). KJV: "hell" 10 times, "grave" once.

    Gehenna (G1067) — 12 occurrences¶

    From ge-Hinnom. Jesus uses it 11 times; James once. Collocates: fire 6 times, apollymi 3 times. Never collocates with basanizo. The verb in gehenna passages is "destroy," not "torment."

    Tartaroo (G5020) — 1 occurrence¶

    Hapax legomenon verb. Angels only. Temporary ("reserved unto judgment"). No bearing on human fate.

    Apollymi (G622) — 102 occurrences¶

    The verb Jesus uses in gehenna contexts. Active: destroy. Middle/passive: perish. The LXX maps the Hebrew abad (H6) to apollymi 141 times. This is the dominant verb for the fate of the wicked.

    Basanizo (G928) — 12 occurrences¶

    Wide semantic range: sickness, waves, moral distress, childbirth, punishment. NEVER co-occurs with gehenna. In eschatological contexts, applied to devil, beast, false prophet (Rev 20:10), not to human beings in gehenna.

    Kolasis (G2851) — 2 occurrences¶

    Punishment, curtailment. Only in Matt 25:46 (eschatological) and 1 John 4:18 (fear). Etymology: curtail/prune, not torture. The passage does not use gehenna, sheol, hades, or tartaroo.

    Aionios (G166) — 69 occurrences¶

    Adjective from aion. Modifies both positive (life) and negative (punishment, destruction, fire) outcomes. Hebrew source: olam (H5769), which is applied to things that ended (Aaron's priesthood, hills).

    Peger (H6297) — 24 occurrences¶

    Carcass, corpse, dead body. NEVER used for a living person. This is the word in Isaiah 66:24 that Jesus quotes in Mark 9:48. The subjects of worm and fire in Isaiah 66:24 are dead bodies.

    Skolex (G4663) / Tolaath (H8438) — Worm¶

    Decomposition agent. Acts on corpses, not living beings.

    Asbestos (G762) — 4 occurrences¶

    Unquenchable fire = fire completing its work, not fire burning forever. Chaff is totally consumed (Matt 3:12).

    Difficult or Complicating Passages¶

    Luke 16:23 — The Rich Man and Lazarus¶

    This is the only passage associating hades with consciousness and torment. It uses hades (G86) + basanos (G931, torment). The passage is a parable — part of a series in Luke 15-16. It fails Tree 3 Gate 1 (subject is a parabolic character) and Gate 3 (genre is parabolic). One parable cannot override 65+ consistent didactic passages characterizing sheol/hades as unconscious. The word used is basanos (touchstone), not basanismos (ongoing torment). If taken as literal teaching about the intermediate state, it contradicts every didactic passage about sheol's nature.

    Revelation 14:10-11 — Smoke of Torment Ascending Forever¶

    This is the strongest ECT proof text after Revelation 20:10. It uses basanizo (torment) vocabulary. Two qualifications: (1) the genre is apocalyptic vision, and (2) the "smoke ascending forever" language is drawn from Isaiah 34:9-10, where it describes the completed destruction of Edom. The OT source establishes the convention: this language describes the permanence of destruction, not the duration of a process.

    Revelation 20:10 — Tormented Day and Night Forever¶

    This applies basanizo to three subjects: devil, beast, false prophet. The complication: humans are later cast into the same lake of fire (Rev 20:15). Does what applies to the three also apply to humans? The text itself makes a vocabulary distinction: when the three supernatural/symbolic entities enter the lake of fire, the word is basanizo (torment); when humans enter it, the word is thanatos (second death). The text draws the distinction; the question is whether to honor or override it.

    Matthew 25:46 — Everlasting Punishment¶

    The same adjective (aionios) modifies both outcomes (punishment and life). Does "everlasting" mean the same thing for both? Grammatically, the same adjective in the same construction likely carries the same force. The question is what aionios modifies: the process (eternal punishing) or the result (permanent punishment). Kolasis means punishment/curtailment, not torment. The passage does not specify what the punishment consists of.

    Daniel 12:2 — Everlasting Contempt¶

    The wicked awake to "shame and everlasting contempt" (deraon). This word appears only here and in Isaiah 66:24, where the context is corpses viewed with abhorrence. Contempt/abhorrence can be the reaction of the living to the aftermath of destruction, not a description of ongoing conscious experience.

    Preliminary Synthesis¶

    The evidence across all 93 verse entries points consistently in one direction at the explicit and necessary implication tiers:

    1. Sheol and hades designate the same concept — the temporary realm of all the dead, characterized by unconsciousness in every didactic passage. Neither word means eternal torment.

    2. Gehenna designates final destruction, not ongoing torment. Jesus' vocabulary is "destroy" (apollymi), not "torment" (basanizo). The OT background describes corpse disposal, not torture of the living. The prophetic name is "Valley of Slaughter."

    3. Tartaroo applies to angels, not humans. It describes temporary imprisonment, not final punishment.

    4. The four words are not interchangeable. They designate distinct concepts: general realm of the dead (sheol/hades), final destruction (gehenna), angelic imprisonment (tartaroo), and final death (lake of fire).

    5. No word inherently means eternal conscious torment. The semantic ranges established by lexicons, usage patterns, and LXX translations do not include "eternal conscious torment" for any of these four words.

    6. Torment vocabulary in eschatological contexts applies to supernatural/symbolic entities (devil, beast, false prophet in Rev 20:10). When humans encounter the same lake of fire, the text switches to death vocabulary ("second death").

    7. The KJV's translation of all four words as "hell" creates a unified concept that obscures the original distinctions and introduces a theological framework not present in the Hebrew and Greek texts.

    The passages that complicate this picture (Luke 16:23; Rev 14:10-11; Rev 20:10; Matt 25:46) involve genre qualifications (parable, apocalyptic), subject qualifications (symbolic entities, not literal humans), or vocabulary questions (what does aionios modify?). At the E-tier, no passage applies eternal conscious torment vocabulary to human beings in a didactic context.
