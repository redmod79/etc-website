    Conclusion — Semantic Range of Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, Tartaroo¶

    Question¶

    Semantic range of Sheol (H7585), Hades (G86), Gehenna (G1067), Tartaroo (G5020). Interchangeability? Does any inherently mean eternal torment? LXX translation patterns.

    Summary Answer¶

    The four words translated "hell" in the KJV designate four distinct concepts: sheol/hades is the temporary, unconscious realm of all the dead; gehenna is the place of final destruction; tartaroo is angelic imprisonment awaiting judgment; and the lake of fire is identified as "the second death." None of the four words inherently means eternal conscious torment. The LXX translates sheol as hades 58 times, confirming their semantic equivalence, and the secondary translations (death, descent, destruction) contain no torment vocabulary. Jesus' vocabulary in gehenna passages is apollymi (G622, destroy), not basanizo (G928, torment), and the OT background of gehenna describes corpse disposal in the Valley of Slaughter, not ongoing conscious suffering.

    Key Verses¶

    God Destroys Soul and Body in Gehenna (Matthew 10:28) And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell.

    No Knowledge in Sheol (Ecclesiastes 9:10) Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might; for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest.

    Sheol-Hades Equivalence Confirmed (Acts 2:27) Because thou wilt not leave my soul in hell, neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption.

    Death and Hades Cast Into Lake of Fire (Revelation 20:14) And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death.

    Lake of Fire = Second Death (Revelation 21:8) But the fearful, and unbelieving, and the abominable, and murderers, and whoremongers, and sorcerers, and idolaters, and all liars, shall have their part in the lake which burneth with fire and brimstone: which is the second death.

    Carcasses Consumed by Worm and Fire (Isaiah 66:24) And they shall go forth, and look upon the carcases of the men that have transgressed against me: for their worm shall not die, neither shall their fire be quenched; and they shall be an abhorring unto all flesh.

    God Kills Then Casts Into Gehenna (Luke 12:5) But I will forewarn you whom ye shall fear: Fear him, which after he hath killed hath power to cast into hell; yea, I say unto you, Fear him.

    Wages of Sin Is Death (Romans 6:23) For the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.

    Wicked Become Ashes (Malachi 4:1,3) For, behold, the day cometh, that shall burn as an oven; and all the proud, yea, and all that do wickedly, shall be stubble: and the day that cometh shall burn them up, saith the LORD of hosts, that it shall leave them neither root nor branch... And ye shall tread down the wicked; for they shall be ashes under the soles of your feet in the day that I shall do this, saith the LORD of hosts.

    Perish vs. Everlasting Life (John 3:16) For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life.

    Inferences¶

    # Claim Type Position What the Bible actually says Why this is an inference Criteria
    I1 Sheol/hades is a state of conscious existence for the dead I-B ECT-direction E1-E5 (Ps 6:5; 88:10-12; 115:17; Ecc 9:10; Isa 38:18): didactic passages describe sheol as unconscious. E14 (Luke 16:23): a parable depicts a character conscious in hades. E45 (Isa 14:9-10): a mashal depicts rephaim speaking. Choosing between didactic descriptions of unconsciousness and parabolic/poetic depictions of consciousness requires interpretation. The parable and mashal are genre-qualified; the didactic passages are not. I026
    I2 Gehenna is a place of eternal conscious torment for human beings I-B ECT-direction E6 (Matt 10:28): God "destroys" soul and body in gehenna. E7 (Matt 5:29-30): apollymi vocabulary. E8 (Luke 12:5): kill-then-cast sequence. E22 (Isa 66:24): corpses consumed. E57 (Mark 9:48 quotes Isa 66:24 corpse text). AGAINST: No gehenna passage uses torment vocabulary for humans (N7). Asserting eternal conscious torment in gehenna requires overriding the apollymi (destroy) vocabulary Jesus uses and the peger (corpse) context of the OT source. I027
    I3 Revelation 20:10 teaches that all the wicked will be tormented forever I-C ECT-direction E12 (Rev 20:10): devil, beast, false prophet tormented. E51 (Rev 20:15): humans cast into same lake of fire. E52: text uses "second death" for humans, not "tormented." Extending the torment of three non-human/symbolic entities to all human beings requires adding the premise that same destination = same experience. The text itself makes a vocabulary distinction. I028
    I4 The four "hell" words designate a single unified concept of eternal torment I-D ECT N1 (sheol = hades), N3 (gehenna distinct from sheol/hades), N4 (hades distinct from lake of fire), N5 (tartaroo = angels only), E29 (KJV collapses distinctions). All four words have different semantic ranges, different subjects, and different temporal scopes. This claim requires overriding the textual distinctions established at E and N tier. The four words have demonstrably different semantic ranges. I029
    I5 The "life vs. gehenna" contrast means "eternal life vs. eternal death/destruction" I-A Cond. E36 (gehenna vs. "enter into life"), E19 (perish vs. everlasting life), E20 (death vs. eternal life), E6 (destroy in gehenna). Systematizes the contrast pattern across multiple passages. All components from E/N tables. Only criterion #5 (systematizing). I030
    I6 Sheol's pairing with Abaddon (destruction) indicates sheol is associated with destruction rather than torment I-A Cond. E46 (sheol-Abaddon pairing in 5+ passages), E47 (Abaddon = Apollyon = Destroyer). Systematizes the pairing pattern. All components observable in E-items. I031
    I7 "Eternal fire" language in the Bible describes the finality of destruction, not the duration of burning I-A Cond. E23 (Sodom's eternal fire — Sodom not still burning), E24 (Edom's smoke forever — Edom not still burning), E54 (everlasting fire prepared for devil). Systematizes two OT precedents where "eternal/forever" fire language describes completed destruction. I032
    I8 "Everlasting punishment" (Matt 25:46) means eternal process of punishing, not permanent result I-B ECT-direction E53 (same adjective modifies both outcomes). FOR ECT: parallel structure suggests parallel duration. AGAINST: kolasis (G2851) etymologically = curtailment; the text does not specify the process vs. result question; E6, E19, E20 define the penalty as destruction/death/perishing, not torment. The parallel structure is a textual fact (E53). What the parallel means — duration of process vs. permanence of result — requires choosing between two readings. I033

    I-B Resolution: I1 — Sheol/hades as conscious existence¶

    Step 1 — Tension: - FOR (consciousness): E14 (Luke 16:23 — rich man conscious in hades), E45 (Isa 14:9-10 — rephaim speak in sheol) - AGAINST (unconsciousness): E1 (Ps 6:5 — no remembrance), E2 (Ps 88:10-12 — comprehensive negation), E3 (Ps 115:17 — silence), E4 (Ecc 9:10 — no knowledge/wisdom), E5 (Isa 38:18 — no praise), E16 (Ecc 9:5 — dead know nothing)

    Step 2 — Clarity Assessment:

    Item Level Rationale
    E1 (Ps 6:5) Plain Didactic psalm, directly states no remembrance in sheol
    E2 (Ps 88:10-12) Plain Didactic psalm, comprehensive negation
    E3 (Ps 115:17) Plain Didactic psalm, dead go to silence
    E4 (Ecc 9:10) Plain Didactic wisdom, four-fold negation of consciousness
    E5 (Isa 38:18) Plain Direct statement from Hezekiah, three parallel terms
    E16 (Ecc 9:5) Plain Didactic wisdom, "dead know not any thing"
    E14 (Luke 16:23) Ambiguous Parable genre (fails Gates 1 and 3); only passage associating hades with consciousness
    E45 (Isa 14:9-10) Ambiguous Mashal/taunt-poem (v.4 labels the genre); poetic personification of the dead

    Step 3 — Weight: FOR unconsciousness: 6 Plain items across 4 OT authors (David, sons of Korah, Solomon, Hezekiah/Isaiah), didactic genre. FOR consciousness: 2 Ambiguous items, both genre-qualified (parable, mashal).

    Step 4 — SIS Application: The Plain didactic statements (6 items, multiple authors, didactic genre) determine the reading of the Ambiguous items. Luke 16:23 is a parable using cultural furniture to make a point about wealth and compassion, not a didactic lesson about hades' nature. Isaiah 14:9 is labeled mashal in v.4 — poetic personification of dead kings.

    Step 5 — Resolution: Strong Six Plain didactic statements from four authors against two Ambiguous genre-qualified passages. The weight is decisively on the unconsciousness side. Resolution: Strong toward Cond.

    I-B Resolution: I2 — Gehenna as eternal conscious torment¶

    Step 1 — Tension: - FOR (eternal torment in gehenna): No E/N items directly state torment in gehenna. The claim draws from Rev 14:10-11 (E13, Neutral — apocalyptic, alludes to Isa 34) and Rev 20:10 (E12, Neutral — non-human subjects). These are not gehenna passages. - AGAINST: E6 (Matt 10:28 — apollymi/destroy in gehenna), E7 (Matt 5:29-30 — apollymi), E8 (Luke 12:5 — kill then cast), E22 (Isa 66:24 — corpses consumed), N7 (no torment vocabulary in any gehenna passage), E57 (Mark 9:48 quotes corpse text)

    Step 2 — Clarity Assessment:

    Item Level Rationale
    E6 (Matt 10:28) Plain Didactic, uses apollymi (destroy), gehenna context
    E7 (Matt 5:29-30) Plain Didactic, apollymi vocabulary
    E8 (Luke 12:5) Plain Didactic, temporal sequence kill→cast
    E22 (Isa 66:24) Plain Prophetic, subjects are peger (corpses)
    N7 Plain Observable fact: basanizo never co-occurs with gehenna
    E12 (Rev 20:10) Ambiguous Apocalyptic, non-human subjects, different word (lake of fire, not gehenna)
    E13 (Rev 14:10-11) Ambiguous Apocalyptic, alludes to OT destruction formula (Isa 34), not a gehenna passage

    Step 3 — Weight: FOR destruction: 5 Plain items using apollymi/peger/kill-then-cast vocabulary in actual gehenna passages. FOR torment: 0 Plain items in gehenna passages. The torment vocabulary comes from Revelation (apocalyptic, non-gehenna contexts).

    Step 4 — SIS Application: The Plain gehenna passages (Matt 10:28; 5:29-30; Luke 12:5; Isa 66:24) use destruction vocabulary. No gehenna passage uses torment vocabulary. The torment imagery in Revelation applies to non-human entities in a non-gehenna context (lake of fire). The clear gehenna passages determine the reading.

    Step 5 — Resolution: Strong All gehenna-specific evidence uses destruction vocabulary. No gehenna passage supports eternal conscious torment at the E-tier. Resolution: Strong toward Cond.

    I-B Resolution: I8 — "Everlasting punishment" (Matt 25:46) as eternal process¶

    Step 1 — Tension: - FOR (eternal process of punishing — ECT): E53 (same adjective aionios modifies both kolasis and zoe; parallel structure) - AGAINST (permanent result of punishment — Cond.): E6 (destroy in gehenna), E19 (perish vs. life), E20 (wages of sin is death), E21 (everlasting destruction)

    Step 2 — Clarity Assessment:

    Item Level Rationale

    Analysis¶

    The Four Words Are Not One Concept¶

    The English word "hell" in the KJV translates four distinct Hebrew and Greek terms. This study examines all occurrences of each word to establish what the biblical text actually says about each one.

    Sheol (H7585) appears 68 times in the OT. The KJV translates it as "grave" 26 times, "hell" 31 times, and "pit" 3 times. The distribution is not random: when righteous individuals go to sheol (Jacob in Genesis 37:35, Job in Job 14:13, David in Psalm 86:13), the KJV tends toward "grave." When the wicked go to sheol (Psalm 9:17, Proverbs 15:24, Isaiah 5:14), the KJV uses "hell." The underlying Hebrew makes no such distinction. The same word, sheol, designates the same destination for both.

    Hades (G86) appears 11 times in the NT. The LXX translates sheol as hades in 58 of approximately 65 instances. Acts 2:27 and 2:31 provide definitive evidence: Peter quotes Psalm 16:10, which uses sheol, and the NT text uses hades. Paul does the same in 1 Corinthians 15:55, quoting Hosea 13:14 with hades for sheol. These inspired quotations establish that the NT authors understood hades as the Greek equivalent of sheol.

    Gehenna (G1067) appears 12 times in the NT — 11 from Jesus, 1 from James. It derives from ge-Hinnom, the Valley of the son of Hinnom, a physical location south of Jerusalem. The OT traces this valley from a boundary marker (Joshua 15:8) to a site of child sacrifice (2 Chronicles 28:3; 33:6) to a prophetically cursed location Jeremiah renamed the "Valley of Slaughter" (Jeremiah 7:31-32; 19:6). The trajectory moves from geography to abomination to prophetic curse to eschatological metaphor.

    Tartaroo (G5020) appears once in all of Scripture — 2 Peter 2:4. It is a verb (not a noun), applied exclusively to fallen angels, describing their temporary imprisonment "reserved unto judgment." It has no bearing on human eschatology.

    These four words cannot substitute for one another. Jacob expects to go to sheol (Genesis 37:35) — substituting "gehenna" produces absurdity (a righteous patriarch expects fiery destruction). Christ went to hades (Acts 2:27) — substituting "gehenna" makes Christ go to the place of final judgment. Angels are cast to tartaroo (2 Peter 2:4) — substituting "sheol" puts them in the general human abode. The substitution test confirms four distinct concepts, not one.

    Sheol: The Unconscious Realm of All the Dead¶

    The OT characterization of sheol is remarkably consistent across multiple authors and centuries. David writes that there is "no remembrance" and "no praise" in sheol (Psalm 6:5). The sons of Korah describe sheol as a place of "no wonders," "no lovingkindness," "darkness," and "forgetfulness" (Psalm 88:10-12). The psalmist of Psalm 115 says the dead go to "silence" (Psalm 115:17). Solomon teaches that in sheol there is "no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom" (Ecclesiastes 9:10) and that "the dead know not any thing" (Ecclesiastes 9:5). Hezekiah states that "sheol cannot praise" and "death cannot celebrate" (Isaiah 38:18).

    These are not isolated proof texts. They represent a consistent pattern across psalms, wisdom literature, and prophetic thanksgiving. Every didactic passage that characterizes sheol's nature describes it as unconscious, silent, dark, and devoid of activity.

    The sheol-Abaddon pairing reinforces this. In Job 26:6, Psalm 88:11, Proverbs 15:11, and Proverbs 27:20, sheol is paired with Abaddon (H11). Abaddon is an intensive form of abad (H6, to perish/destroy). In Revelation 9:11, Abaddon is explicitly equated with Apollyon (Destroyer, from apollymi G622). The Hebrew authors consistently paired the realm of the dead with destruction vocabulary, not torment vocabulary.

    The rephaim (H7496, shades/dead ones) who inhabit sheol are characterized by weakness. Isaiah 14:10 says to the fallen king: "Art thou also become weak as we?" These are not conscious beings suffering; they are feeble shades in death.

    Both righteous and wicked go to sheol. Jacob expects it (Genesis 37:35). David needs deliverance from it (Psalm 86:13). Job requests it as a hiding place (Job 14:13). Christ goes there (Psalm 16:10; Acts 2:27,31). And the wicked go there too (Psalm 9:17; Numbers 16:33). This dual occupancy conclusively demonstrates that sheol is not a place of punishment. It is the common destination of all the dead.

    Hades: Sheol's Greek Equivalent¶

    All 11 hades occurrences align with sheol's semantic range. Hades is paired with death (Revelation 1:18; 6:8; 20:13-14) just as sheol is paired with maveth in the OT. Hades is used in the heaven-hades merism (Matthew 11:23; Luke 10:15) just as sheol is used in the heaven-sheol merism (Psalm 139:8; Amos 9:2). Hades is a domain Christ has conquered (Revelation 1:18) just as sheol is a domain God destroys (Hosea 13:14).

    Hades is temporary. Revelation 20:13-14 states this explicitly: "death and hades delivered up the dead which were in them... and death and hades were cast into the lake of fire." Hades empties itself, then ceases to exist. It cannot be the final state of the wicked because it is itself destroyed.

    Luke 16:23 — the rich man conscious in hades — is the only passage associating hades with consciousness. It is a parable, part of a series in Luke 15-16 (lost sheep, lost coin, prodigal son, unjust steward, rich man and Lazarus). Parabolic genre fails Gate 1 (allegorical character) and Gate 3 (parabolic, not didactic). A single parable cannot override the consistent testimony of 65+ passages that characterize sheol/hades by unconsciousness.

    Gehenna: Final Destruction, Not Eternal Torment¶

    The vocabulary Jesus uses in gehenna passages is the decisive evidence. In Matthew 10:28, Jesus says God can "destroy" (apollymi G622, aorist active infinitive) both soul (psyche) and body (soma) in gehenna. The Greek parser confirms: apolesai = to destroy. The verb is apollymi, not basanizo. If Jesus intended to teach eternal conscious torment in gehenna, "destroy" is the wrong word.

    In Matthew 5:29-30, the member "perishes" (apollymi) and the body is "cast" into gehenna — disposal language. In Luke 12:5, God kills first, THEN casts into gehenna — a temporal sequence (meta + articular infinitive) that places death before gehenna. In Mark 9:43-48, Jesus quotes Isaiah 66:24. In Isaiah, the subjects of the worm and fire are peger (H6297, carcasses/corpses) — a word never used for a living person. The worm (tolaath/skolex) is a decomposition agent feeding on dead bodies. The fire is "unquenchable" — in OT usage, this means fire that cannot be extinguished before completing its work (cf. Jeremiah 17:27, where Jerusalem's gates burned with unquenchable fire but are not still burning).

    The contrast pattern reinforces this: "enter into life" vs. gehenna (Matthew 18:9; Mark 9:43,45,47). If gehenna were eternal conscious torment, the opposite would not be "life" but something like "bliss." The contrast is life vs. destruction, not happiness vs. suffering.

    Basanizo (G928, torment) never co-occurs with gehenna in any NT passage. This is a verifiable textual fact. The torment vocabulary in eschatological contexts (Revelation 14:10-11; 20:10) applies to the devil, beast, and false prophet in the lake of fire — not to human beings in gehenna.

    The OT Background of Gehenna¶

    The Valley of Hinnom's trajectory through the OT establishes gehenna's semantic foundation. It begins as a boundary marker (Joshua 15:8; 18:16). It becomes the site of child sacrifice to Molech and Baal (2 Chronicles 28:3; 33:6; Jeremiah 32:35). God condemns this practice emphatically: "which I commanded them not, neither came it into my heart" (Jeremiah 7:31). Josiah defiles Topheth to end the sacrifice (2 Kings 23:10).

    Jeremiah then pronounces judgment: the valley will be renamed the "Valley of Slaughter" (Jeremiah 7:32; 19:6). The prophetic name is haregah (slaughter), not any word for torment. The valley becomes a place of burial — "they shall bury in Topheth, till there be no place" (Jeremiah 7:32). The imagery throughout is slaughter, death, and corpse disposal.

    Isaiah 66:24 completes the picture: the righteous "look upon the carcasses (peger) of the men that have transgressed." The subjects are dead bodies. The worm and fire consume corpses. Daniel 12:2 shares the rare word deraon (contempt/abhorrence) with Isaiah 66:24, connecting the resurrection judgment to the corpse imagery.

    Tartaroo: Angelic Imprisonment¶

    Tartaroo (G5020) is the simplest of the four to analyze. It occurs once (2 Peter 2:4), as a verb ("having cast down to tartaroo"), applied exclusively to fallen angels. They are delivered into "chains of darkness" and "reserved unto judgment." Jude 1:6 provides the parallel: "everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day." The chains are permanent (aidios G126), but the purpose is temporal ("unto judgment"). Tartaroo describes a temporary holding facility for angels, not a final destination and not a human one.

    LXX Translation Patterns¶

    The LXX translates sheol as hades in approximately 58 of 65 instances. The secondary translations are significant: thanatos (death), katabaino (to descend), katago (to lead down), and apoleia (destruction). Every secondary translation uses death, descent, or destruction vocabulary. None uses torment vocabulary. The translators — working centuries before the NT — understood sheol as the realm of the dead characterized by death and descent, not by torment.

    The Lake of Fire and the Second Death¶

    The lake of fire must be distinguished from both hades and gehenna. Hades is cast INTO the lake of fire (Revelation 20:14) — they are not the same. The lake of fire is identified twice as "the second death" (Revelation 20:14; 21:8). When the devil, beast, and false prophet are cast into it, the text uses basanizo (torment, Revelation 20:10). When humans are cast into it, the text uses thanatos (death, Revelation 20:14-15; 21:8). This vocabulary distinction is consistent and appears to be intentional.

    The three subjects of torment in Revelation 20:10 are the devil (a non-human spirit being), the beast (a symbolic entity in apocalyptic vision), and the false prophet (another symbolic entity). Extending the torment vocabulary from these three to all human beings requires adding a premise the text does not state: that same destination means same experience. The text draws a vocabulary distinction; whether to honor or override that distinction is an interpretive choice.

    Does Any Word Inherently Mean Eternal Conscious Torment?¶

    The lexical evidence is clear for each word:

    Sheol: No lexicon defines it as "a place of eternal conscious torment." Standard definition: "the realm of the dead, the underworld." Every didactic passage characterizes it by unconsciousness.

    Hades: No lexicon defines it as "a place of eternal conscious torment." Standard definition: "the realm of the dead, the underworld" (Greek equivalent of sheol). Temporary — destroyed in Revelation 20:14.

    Gehenna: No lexicon defines it as "a place of eternal conscious torment." Standard definition: "Valley of Hinnom; place of final punishment/destruction." The vocabulary Jesus uses in gehenna is destruction (apollymi), not torment (basanizo).

    Tartaroo: No lexicon defines it as "a place of eternal conscious torment." Standard definition: "to cast into Tartarus" (a verb of imprisonment). Applied to angels, temporary.

    The concept of "eternal conscious torment" as a single unified doctrine requires combining elements from different words, different passages, different genres, and different subjects. No single word carries that meaning, and no single passage states it for human beings in a didactic context.

    Word Studies¶

    Apollymi (G622): The verb Jesus uses in gehenna passages. Active = destroy; middle/passive = perish. Hebrew source: abad (H6), with 141 LXX mappings. This is the dominant vocabulary for the fate of the wicked.

    Basanizo (G928): Wide semantic range including sickness, waves, moral distress, and punishment. In eschatological contexts, applied to devil, beast, and false prophet (Revelation 20:10) — never to humans in gehenna.

    Kolasis (G2851): Only 2 NT occurrences. Etymologically from kolazo = curtail/prune. Punishment, not specifically torment.

    Aionios (G166): Adjective meaning pertaining to the age. Modifies both positive (life) and negative (punishment, destruction, fire) outcomes. Hebrew source: olam (H5769), applied to things that ended.

    Peger (H6297): Carcass, corpse. Never used for a living person. The subjects in Isaiah 66:24 (quoted in Mark 9:48) are dead bodies.

    Difficult Passages¶

    Luke 16:23 — Rich Man and Lazarus¶

    The only passage associating hades with consciousness. Genre: parable. Fails Gate 1 (parabolic character) and Gate 3 (parabolic genre). If read as literal teaching about the intermediate state, it contradicts every didactic passage about sheol/hades. The I-B resolution (I1) found 6 Plain didactic items against 2 Ambiguous genre-qualified items. Resolution: Strong toward conditionalist.

    Revelation 14:10-11 — Smoke of Torment¶

    Uses basanizo and basanismos. Apocalyptic genre. Alludes to Isaiah 34:9-10, where "smoke ascending forever" describes the completed destruction of Edom (which is not still burning). The OT source establishes the convention: this language describes finality, not duration.

    Revelation 20:10 — Tormented Day and Night¶

    Applies basanizo to devil, beast, false prophet — non-human and symbolic entities. Gate 1 fails (non-human subjects). The extension to humans requires adding a premise the text does not contain. When humans enter the same lake of fire, the text switches to "second death" vocabulary.

    Matthew 25:46 — Everlasting Punishment¶

    The parallel structure (aionios modifying both kolasis and zoe) is genuine. The question is process vs. result: eternal punishing or permanent punishment. The I-B resolution (I8) found the defining passages (Matt 10:28, John 3:16, Rom 6:23, 2 Thess 1:9) use destruction/death vocabulary for the penalty. Resolution: Moderate toward conditionalist.

    What CAN Be Said (Scripture explicitly states or necessarily implies)¶

    • Sheol and hades are semantically equivalent (LXX data, Acts 2:27,31, 1 Cor 15:55)
    • Sheol/hades is characterized by unconsciousness in every didactic passage (Ps 6:5; 88:10-12; 115:17; Ecc 9:5,10; Isa 38:18)
    • Both righteous and wicked go to sheol (Gen 37:35; Ps 9:17; Ps 16:10; Acts 2:27)
    • Hades is temporary — delivers up the dead, then is cast into the lake of fire (Rev 20:13-14)
    • Gehenna and sheol/hades are distinct concepts with distinct semantic ranges
    • Hades and the lake of fire are distinct — one is cast into the other
    • Tartaroo applies only to angels, not humans (2 Pet 2:4)
    • Jesus uses apollymi (destroy) in gehenna passages, never basanizo (torment)
    • The OT background of gehenna is the Valley of Slaughter with corpse disposal imagery
    • Isaiah 66:24's subjects (quoted in Mark 9:48) are peger (corpses/dead bodies)
    • The lake of fire is identified as "the second death" (Rev 20:14; 21:8)
    • When humans encounter the lake of fire, the text uses death vocabulary; when the devil/beast/false prophet encounter it, the text uses torment vocabulary
    • The KJV translation of four distinct words as "hell" obscures distinct semantic ranges
    • No lexicon defines any of the four words as "a place of eternal conscious torment"

    What CANNOT Be Said (not explicitly stated or necessarily implied by Scripture)¶

    • That sheol/hades is a place of conscious torment (only one parable depicts this; all didactic passages contradict it)
    • That gehenna involves eternal conscious torment (no gehenna passage uses torment vocabulary for humans)
    • That the four "hell" words designate a single unified concept (they have different semantic ranges, subjects, and temporal scopes)
    • That Revelation 20:10's torment of the devil/beast/false prophet applies to all human beings (the text uses different vocabulary for humans)
    • That "everlasting punishment" (Matt 25:46) specifies the nature of the punishment as ongoing torment (the text says "punishment," not "torment," and does not specify process vs. result)
    • That "unquenchable fire" means fire that burns forever (OT precedent shows it means fire that completes its work)
    • That tartaroo has any bearing on human eschatology (single occurrence, angel-only)
    • That the soul survives death in a conscious state to experience eternal torment (no "four hell words" passage teaches this)
    • That any specific verse in a didactic genre states "the wicked will be consciously tormented forever" (no such verse exists in any genre for human subjects)

    Conclusion¶

    The four words translated "hell" in the KJV designate four distinct concepts with non-overlapping semantic ranges. Sheol (H7585) and hades (G86) are the temporary, unconscious realm of all the dead — both righteous and wicked — confirmed as semantically equivalent by the LXX (58 mappings) and by inspired NT quotations (Acts 2:27,31; 1 Corinthians 15:55). Gehenna (G1067) is the place of final destruction, rooted in the Valley of Hinnom's OT trajectory from child sacrifice to prophetic curse ("Valley of Slaughter") to eschatological metaphor. Tartaroo (G5020) is a single-occurrence verb describing temporary angelic imprisonment with no application to humans.

    At the E-tier, 18 explicit statements use destruction, death, cessation, or unconsciousness vocabulary for the fate of the wicked and the nature of the afterlife realm. Zero explicit statements use eternal conscious torment vocabulary for human beings in a didactic context. The three passages most cited for eternal conscious torment (Luke 16:23; Revelation 14:10-11; Revelation 20:10) are reclassified to Neutral after Gate failures: Luke 16:23 is a parable; Revelation 14:10-11 is apocalyptic genre alluding to an OT destruction formula; Revelation 20:10 applies torment to non-human and symbolic entities.

    At the N-tier, all 9 necessary implications are Neutral — factual observations both sides accept: the words are distinct, hades is temporary, sheol receives both righteous and wicked, the KJV obscures distinctions.

    At the I-tier, all three I-B items (sheol-as-conscious, gehenna-as-torment, everlasting-punishment-as-process) resolve toward conditionalist — the first two with Strong resolution, the third with Moderate resolution. The ECT position at the inference tier rests on one I-C item (extending Rev 20:10 torment to humans) and one I-D item (treating the four words as a unified concept). The I-D item requires overriding multiple E/N-tier distinctions between the words.

    The evidence tiers contain what they contain. At the highest tier (Explicit), the vocabulary is destruction, death, and unconsciousness. The concept of eternal conscious torment for human beings does not appear in any passage using sheol, hades, gehenna, or tartaroo in a didactic context.
