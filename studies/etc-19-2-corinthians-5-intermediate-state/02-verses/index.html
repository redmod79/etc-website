    Verses¶

    Question¶

    What does 2 Corinthians 5:1-8 teach about the intermediate state? Does "unclothed" (ekdysasthai) mean disembodied? What is Paul's actual preference and what does the clothing metaphor mean? Does this passage support conscious existence between death and resurrection, or is Paul expressing preference for resurrection without dying?

    Core passage: earthly tent vs. heavenly building, groaning, clothing metaphor, and arrabon of the Spirit¶

    5:1 For we know that if our earthly house of [this] tabernacle were dissolved, we have a building of God, an house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens. 5:2 For in this we groan, earnestly desiring to be clothed upon with our house which is from heaven: 5:3 If so be that being clothed we shall not be found naked. 5:4 For we that are in [this] tabernacle do groan, being burdened: not for that we would be unclothed, but clothed upon, that mortality might be swallowed up of life. 5:5 Now he that hath wrought us for the selfsame thing [is] God, who also hath given unto us the earnest of the Spirit.

    Context: Paul's second letter to the Corinthians, following 2 Cor 4:7-18 where he discusses suffering, the outward man perishing while the inward man is renewed, and the contrast between temporal affliction and eternal glory. The immediate preceding verse (4:18) sets up the temporal/eternal contrast that continues into 5:1. Paul speaks as an apostle to a church he planted, expressing the shared Christian hope.

    Notes: Five key observations: (1) The conditional in v.1 uses ean + aorist passive subjunctive katalythe (third-class condition), not first-class; the proleptic present echomen ('we have') expresses certainty about a future reality. (2) The textual variant in v.3 — endysamenoi (G1746, 'having put on') vs. ekdysamenoi (G1562, 'having put off') — both readings are well-attested; the N1904 text has endysamenoi. (3) The three-part preference in v.4: ou thelomen ekdysasthai (NOT want to be unclothed) / all' ependysasthai (BUT to be clothed upon) / hina katapothE to thnEton hypo tEs zOEs (PURPOSE: that mortality be swallowed by life). (4) katapothE in v.4 is aorist passive subjunctive of katapino G2666, the same verb appearing as KatepothE (aorist passive indicative) in 1 Cor 15:54. (5) arrabon (v.5) is a commercial deposit/guarantee — the Spirit guarantees the future resurrection body.

    Core passage: absent from body / present with the Lord — the key verses for the intermediate state debate¶

    5:6 Therefore [we are] always confident, knowing that, whilst we are at home in the body, we are absent from the Lord: 5:7 (For we walk by faith, not by sight:) 5:8 We are confident, [I say], and willing rather to be absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord.

    Context: Continuation of the preceding clothing/body passage. Paul draws the practical inference: confidence despite present mortality. V.7 is a parenthetical explaining why we are 'absent' from the Lord — we walk by faith, not direct sight. V.8 uses the endemeo/ekdemeo pair (both only in 2 Cor 5) with aorist infinitives.

    Notes: Critical grammar: v.8 has two aorist active infinitives connected by kai — ekdEmEsai (V-AAN from ekdemeo G1553) and endEmEsai (V-AAN from endemeo G1736). The grammar reference on aorist infinitive temporal relationships confirms that kai between two aorist infinitives does NOT encode temporal sequence — it is compatible with sequential, simultaneous, or comprehensive readings. The eudokoumen (V-PAI-1P from eudokeo G2106) is present tense expressing ongoing willingness, not a punctiliar decision. The mallon ('rather') indicates preference, not demand.

    Core passage: whether present or absent, aim to please — judgment seat of Christ¶

    5:9 Wherefore we labour, that, whether present or absent, we may be accepted of him. 5:10 For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ; that every one may receive the things [done] in [his] body, according to that he hath done, whether [it be] good or bad.

    Context: The conclusion of the argument. V.9 uses the eite...eite ('whether...or') construction with present participles endemountes/ekdemountes, treating the two states as mutually exclusive alternatives. V.10 introduces the judgment seat (bema) of Christ as future accountability — receiving recompense for deeds done 'through the body' (dia tou somatos).

    Notes: V.10 places accountability at the future judgment seat, not at death. The phrase 'dia tou somatos' (through the body) indicates that bodily existence is the arena of moral action. The future judgment is the culmination of the argument, not intermediate-state consciousness.

    Immediate preceding context: explicit resurrection hope¶

    Knowing that he which raised up the Lord Jesus shall raise up us also by Jesus, and shall present [us] with you.

    Context: Within the 2 Cor 4:7-18 suffering/hope passage. Paul explicitly names resurrection as his hope — the same God who raised Jesus will raise us. This verse frames the entire clothing metaphor that follows in ch.5.

    Notes: The verb egeiro (G1453) appears twice — 'he which raised up the Lord Jesus shall raise up us also.' Paul's explicit hope before the clothing metaphor begins is bodily resurrection, not disembodied existence.

    Immediate preceding context: outward man perishing, inward man renewed, temporal vs. eternal¶

    4:16 For which cause we faint not; but though our outward man perish, yet the inward [man] is renewed day by day. 4:17 For our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding [and] eternal weight of glory; 4:18 While we look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen: for the things which are seen [are] temporal; but the things which are not seen [are] eternal.

    Context: The temporal/eternal contrast that flows directly into 5:1. The 'outward man perishing' is the tent being dissolved; the 'eternal weight of glory' is the building from God. The argument is continuous across the chapter boundary.

    Notes: The perishing outward man (4:16) = the tent (skenos) of 5:1. The inward man renewed = the believer's hope. The 'things not seen' that are 'eternal' (4:18) = the building from God, eternal in the heavens (5:1). The logical flow is seamless.

    Same-author resurrection vocabulary: natural body vs. spiritual body¶

    15:35 But some [man] will say, How are the dead raised up? and with what body do they come? 15:36 [Thou] fool, that which thou sowest is not quickened, except it die: 15:37 And that which thou sowest, thou sowest not that body that shall be, but bare grain, it may chance of wheat, or of some other [grain]: 15:38 But God giveth it a body as it hath pleased him, and to every seed his own body. ... 15:42 So also [is] the resurrection of the dead. It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption: 15:43 It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory: it is sown in weakness; it is raised in power: 15:44 It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body. There is a natural body, and there is a spiritual body.

    Context: Paul's extended resurrection argument in 1 Corinthians 15. The seed/body analogy provides the conceptual framework for the clothing metaphor in 2 Cor 5. The 'bare grain' (gymnon, G1131) sown before receiving its body parallels the 'naked' state Paul fears in 2 Cor 5:3.

    Notes: The gymnon ('bare') grain in v.37 uses the same adjective as gymnoi ('naked') in 2 Cor 5:3. The sowing/raising paradigm describes death/resurrection, not a conscious intermediate state. The proleptic present tense egreiretai ('it is raised') in vv.42-44 treats the future resurrection with present-tense certainty.

    Same-author parallel: identical vocabulary — endyo, katapino, thnetos, athanasia¶

    15:51 Behold, I shew you a mystery; We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed, 15:52 In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed. 15:53 For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal [must] put on immortality. 15:54 So when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality, then shall be brought to pass the saying that is written, Death is swallowed up in victory.

    Context: The climax of the resurrection chapter. The vocabulary here is identical to 2 Cor 5:4: endysasthai (put on, G1746), katepothE/katapothE (swallowed up, G2666), thneton (mortal, G2349), athanasian (immortality, G110). This links both passages to the same resurrection event.

    Notes: CRITICAL: In v.53, endysasthai (V-AMN, aorist middle infinitive of endyo G1746) = same form as in 2 Cor 5:3. In v.54, KatepothE (V-API-3S, aorist passive indicative of katapino G2666) differs from 2 Cor 5:4's katapothE (V-APS-3S, aorist passive subjunctive) only in mood — indicative (realized quotation from Isa 25:8) vs. subjunctive (purpose clause). Same verb, same concept. The 'we shall not all sleep' (v.51) is the exact parallel to ependysasthai in 2 Cor 5:4 — transformation without death.

    Same-author: Spirit as guarantee of bodily resurrection — quicken your mortal bodies¶

    8:10 And if Christ [be] in you, the body [is] dead because of sin; but the Spirit [is] life because of righteousness. 8:11 But if the Spirit of him that raised up Jesus from the dead dwell in you, he that raised up Christ from the dead shall also quicken your mortal bodies by his Spirit that dwelleth in you.

    Context: Paul's discussion of the Spirit's role in resurrection. The Spirit indwelling believers guarantees future bodily resurrection — the same concept as the arrabon (earnest) of the Spirit in 2 Cor 5:5.

    Notes: thneta (G2349, mortal) soma (bodies) — same adjective as 2 Cor 5:4 thneton. The Spirit quickens (zoopoiesei, G2227) the mortal body — this is the 'swallowing up of mortality by life' in 2 Cor 5:4.

    Same-author parallel: groaning (stenazomen), waiting for redemption of our body¶

    And not only [they], but ourselves also, which have the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, [to wit], the redemption of our body.

    Context: Part of Rom 8:18-30, Paul's discussion of creation's groaning and the believer's hope. The Spirit is called 'firstfruits' (aparche, G536) — a parallel metaphor to arrabon (G728) in 2 Cor 5:5. Both describe the Spirit as present guarantee of future completion.

    Notes: stenazomen (V-PAI-1P, present active indicative of stenazo G4727) — identical form to 2 Cor 5:2 and 5:4. The groaning is for 'the redemption of our body' (ten apolutrosin tou somatos) — explicitly bodily, not for disembodied existence. The object of groaning in Romans 8 defines the object of groaning in 2 Cor 5.

    Parallel passage: depart and be with Christ¶

    1:21 For to me to live [is] Christ, and to die [is] gain. 1:22 But if I live in the flesh, this [is] the fruit of my labour: yet what I shall choose I wot not. 1:23 For I am in a strait betwixt two, having a desire to depart, and to be with Christ; which is far better: 1:24 Nevertheless to abide in the flesh [is] more needful for you.

    Context: Paul in prison (probably Rome), facing possible execution. He weighs continued ministry vs. death. The construction in v.23: eis to analysai kai syn Christo einai — two infinitives (aorist active analysai, V-AAN from analyo G360; and present active einai, V-PAN from eimi G1510) connected by kai.

    Notes: analysai (V-AAN, aorist active infinitive from analyo G360) = 'to depart.' The cognate noun analusis (G359) appears in 2 Tim 4:6. einai is present infinitive (not aorist), suggesting the state of being with Christ, not a punctiliar arrival. Same grammar question as 2 Cor 5:8: does kai require immediate temporal sequence? Same-letter context is decisive: Phil 3:11 'the resurrection of the dead' and Phil 3:20-21 'who shall change our vile body.'

    Same-letter context for Phil 1:23: Paul's hope is resurrection¶

    If by any means I might attain unto the resurrection of the dead.

    Context: Same letter as Phil 1:23. Paul identifies his ultimate aspiration as resurrection (exanastasin ten ek nekron). This frames what 'being with Christ' means in 1:23 — it is the resurrection hope, not immediate post-death consciousness.

    Notes: exanastasin (G1815) is a rare compound form of anastasis, emphasizing the 'out from among' character of resurrection. If Paul already believed he would be consciously with Christ immediately at death, why would resurrection be something he hopes to 'attain'?

    Same-letter context: Saviour who shall change our vile body¶

    3:20 For our conversation is in heaven; from whence also we look for the Saviour, the Lord Jesus Christ: 3:21 Who shall change our vile body, that it may be fashioned like unto his glorious body, according to the working whereby he is able even to subdue all things unto himself.

    Context: Same letter as Phil 1:23. Paul looks for the Saviour FROM heaven (not to go to heaven at death). The transformation verb metaschEmatisei (G3345) = 'shall change the form of' — bodily transformation, same concept as ependysasthai in 2 Cor 5:4.

    Notes: The 'vile body' (to soma tes tapeinoseos) corresponds to the 'tent' of 2 Cor 5:1. The 'glorious body' (to soma tes doxes) corresponds to the 'building from God.' The mechanism is transformation (metaschEmatisei), not soul-release.

    Same-author: Paul's departure — crown at that day, not at death¶

    4:6 For I am now ready to be offered, and the time of my departure is at hand. 4:7 I have fought a good fight, I have finished [my] course, I have kept the faith: 4:8 Henceforth there is laid up for me a crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, shall give me at that day: and not to me only, but unto all them also that love his appearing.

    Context: Paul's final letter, facing imminent death. The noun analusis (G359) in v.6 is cognate with analysai (G360) in Phil 1:23. Paul's hope is a crown 'at that day' (en ekeinE tE hEmera) — future, at the parousia, not at death.

    Notes: analusis (G359, N-GSF) appears only here in the NT. apodOsei (V-FAI-3S, future active indicative of apodidomi G591) = 'shall give' — future tense. en ekeinE tE hEmera = 'in/at that day' — the day of Christ's appearing (epiphaneian, G2015). The crown is received at the parousia, not at death. Even facing death, Paul's hope remains eschatological.

    Same-author: the dead in Christ rise first — being with the Lord placed at resurrection¶

    4:13 But I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope. 4:14 For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so them also which sleep in Jesus will God bring with him. 4:15 For this we say unto you by the word of the Lord, that we which are alive [and] remain unto the coming of the Lord shall not prevent them which are asleep. 4:16 For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first: 4:17 Then we which are alive [and] remain shall be caught up together with them in the clouds, to meet the Lord in the air: and so shall we ever be with the Lord.

    Context: Paul's earliest letter, addressing concern about believers who have died before the parousia. The key phrase is v.17: 'and so (houtos) shall we ever be with the Lord' — being with the Lord is placed at the resurrection/catching-up, not at death.

    Notes: koimao (G2837) = 'those who are asleep' (v.13,14,15) — death-as-sleep vocabulary. The sequence in vv.16-17 is explicit: (1) Lord descends, (2) dead in Christ rise first, (3) living caught up together, (4) 'so shall we ever be with the Lord.' Being with the Lord follows resurrection. The 'we which are alive and remain' in v.15 corresponds to the ependysasthai hope of 2 Cor 5:4.

    Same-author: whether we wake or sleep, we live with him¶

    Who died for us, that, whether we wake or sleep, we should live together with him.

    Context: Same letter as 1 Thess 4:13-17. Paul envisions both possibilities: being alive (gregOromen) or being dead (katheudomen) at the parousia. Either way, the outcome is 'live together with him.'

    Notes: The eite...eite ('whether...or') construction here parallels 2 Cor 5:9. Paul considers both scenarios — alive or dead at Christ's return — with the same expected outcome.

    Same-author: if no resurrection, those asleep in Christ are perished¶

    15:18 Then they also which are fallen asleep in Christ are perished. 15:19 If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men most miserable. 15:20 But now is Christ risen from the dead, [and] become the firstfruits of them that slept.

    Context: Paul's argument about the necessity of resurrection. If the dead are already consciously with Christ, v.18 makes no sense — how would they have 'perished' if they are already enjoying conscious fellowship?

    Notes: This is one of the strongest arguments against conscious intermediate existence. If deceased believers are already consciously with Christ in heaven, Paul could not say they have 'perished' (apolonto, from apollymi G622) if there were no resurrection. The logic requires that without resurrection, death is the end — the dead are not conscious anywhere.

    OT death-state: the dead know not anything¶

    Context: David's prayer in distress. He appeals to God for healing on the ground that dead people cannot praise or remember God.

    Notes: zeker (H2143) = 'remembrance/memory.' sheol (H7585) = 'the grave/underworld.' David's argument presupposes that death means cessation of conscious activity.

    The dead praise not the LORD, neither any that go down into silence.

    Context: A psalm of trust in God. The dead are described as going 'into silence' (dumah H1745) — the opposite of praise and worship.

    Notes: dumah (H1745) = 'silence.' This is inconsistent with conscious existence in God's presence, where one would expect praise, not silence.

    OT death-state: Hezekiah — the grave cannot praise thee¶

    38:18 For the grave cannot praise thee, death can [not] celebrate thee: they that go down into the pit cannot hope for thy truth. 38:19 The living, the living, he shall praise thee, as I [do] this day: the father to the children shall make known thy truth.

    Context: Hezekiah's prayer after being healed from a terminal illness. He expresses gratitude for life by contrasting it with the inability of the dead to praise God.

    Notes: Hezekiah explicitly contrasts the dead (who cannot praise, celebrate, or hope) with the living (who can praise). This is the OT framework within which Paul was educated.

    OT death-state: man lies down and rises not till the heavens be no more¶

    14:12 So man lieth down, and riseth not: till the heavens [be] no more, they shall not awake, nor be raised out of their sleep. 14:13 O that thou wouldest hide me in the grave, that thou wouldest keep me secret, until thy wrath be past, that thou wouldest appoint me a set time, and remember me! 14:14 If a man die, shall he live [again]? all the days of my appointed time will I wait, till my change come.

    Context: Job's reflection on mortality. He describes death as sleep from which one does not awake until the heavens are no more. He hopes for a future 'change' (chaliphah H2487) — resurrection.

    Notes: chaliphah (H2487) = 'change/renewal.' Job's hope is for a future change (resurrection), and he says he will 'wait' for it. The dead lie down and do not rise 'till the heavens be no more' — implying resurrection at the end of the age, not immediate conscious existence.

    OT death-state: spirit returns to God who gave it — reversal of Gen 2:7¶

    Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it.

    Context: Solomon's description of death as the reversal of creation. Dust returns to earth (Gen 3:19), spirit/breath returns to God (who gave it in Gen 2:7).

    Notes: This verse describes the dissolution of the living being created in Gen 2:7 (dust + breath = living soul). The spirit/ruach returning to God does not imply conscious existence — animals have the same ruach (Ecc 3:19-21) and it returns to God as well. Stephen's prayer (Acts 7:59) and Jesus' commendation (Luke 23:46) follow this pattern.

    Counter-evidence: souls under the altar crying out — requires genre analysis¶

    6:9 And when he had opened the fifth seal, I saw under the altar the souls of them that were slain for the word of God, and for the testimony which they held: 6:10 And they cried with a loud voice, saying, How long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge and avenge our blood on them that dwell on the earth? 6:11 And white robes were given unto every one of them; and it was said unto them, that they should rest yet for a little season, until their fellowservants also and their brethren, that should be killed as they [were], should be fulfilled.

    Context: Part of the seven seals in Revelation — apocalyptic literature using symbolic imagery throughout. Under the altar is the location where sacrificial blood was poured (Lev 4:7); the 'souls' are under the altar because they were 'slain.'

    Notes: Genre: apocalyptic symbolism. Compare Gen 4:10 where Abel's blood 'cries out' from the ground — without implying Abel is conscious. Compare Rev 16:7 where the altar itself speaks. The 'souls' receive white robes and are told to 'rest' — which is consistent with the sleep metaphor. ECT interpreters who take this literally must also take the altar speaking literally (Rev 16:7).

    Counter-evidence: today shalt thou be with me in paradise — punctuation debate¶

    And Jesus said unto him, Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise.

    Context: Jesus on the cross, speaking to the repentant thief. The Greek has no punctuation — 'amen soi lego semeron met emou ese en to paradeiso.' The question is whether 'today' modifies 'I say' or 'you will be.'

    Notes: Critical context: Jesus himself had NOT ascended to the Father by Sunday morning (John 20:17). If 'today' means the thief would be in paradise that same day, then either Jesus went to paradise on Friday (contradicted by John 20:17) or the thief went without Jesus. The Semitic pattern 'I say unto you today' is well-attested (Deut 4:26, 6:6, 8:19, 30:18). Acts 2:34: 'David is not ascended into the heavens.'

    NT explicit: David is not ascended into the heavens¶

    For David is not ascended into the heavens: but he saith himself, The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou on my right hand,

    Context: Peter's Pentecost sermon. David died, was buried, and his tomb is known. Peter explicitly states David has NOT ascended to heaven — even though David was a man after God's own heart.

    Notes: This is a plain NT statement. If OT saints like David are not in heaven, the intermediate state is not one of conscious heavenly existence.

    NT explicit: OT faithful received not the promise — await resurrection¶

    11:39 And these all, having obtained a good report through faith, received not the promise: 11:40 God having provided some better thing for us, that they without us should not be made perfect.

    Context: The conclusion of the 'faith hall of fame' chapter. All the OT faithful — Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Moses, etc. — 'received not the promise.' They wait for the resurrection, when they will be 'made perfect' together with NT believers.

    Notes: If OT saints are already consciously in heaven with God, in what sense did they 'not receive the promise'? The text requires that their reward is future, awaiting the resurrection.

    Same-letter: arrabon of the Spirit — sealing and pledge¶

    Who hath also sealed us, and given the earnest of the Spirit in our hearts.

    Context: Earlier in the same letter. The arrabon metaphor first appears here, paired with 'sealing' (sphragizo G4972).

    Notes: arrabon (G728) + sphragizo (G4972) — commercial guarantee paired with ownership seal. Both metaphors are eschatological — they guarantee a future completion.

    Same-author: Spirit as arrabon until the redemption of the purchased possession¶

    1:13 In whom ye also [trusted], after that ye heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation: in whom also after that ye believed, ye were sealed with that holy Spirit of promise, 1:14 Which is the earnest of our inheritance until the redemption of the purchased possession, unto the praise of his glory.

    Context: Paul's Ephesian letter. The Spirit is arrabon (G728) of our inheritance 'until' (eis) the redemption (apolytrosis G629) of the purchased possession.

    Notes: The 'until' (eis) marks a future terminus. The arrabon operates UNTIL the full redemption. This temporal structure confirms that the full inheritance (including the resurrection body) is future, not present.

    OT creation formula: foundational anthropology¶

    And the LORD God formed man [of] the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.

    Context: The creation of the first human. Dust + breath of life = living soul. The formula defines the human constitution.

    Notes: Man BECAME a nephesh chayyah — he did not RECEIVE a soul. Death reverses this formula: dust returns to earth (Gen 3:19, Ecc 12:7), breath/spirit returns to God (Ecc 12:7), and the nephesh ceases. This anthropology governs how Paul's body language in 2 Cor 5 should be understood.

    Stephen: receive my spirit, then he fell asleep — spirit-commitment + sleep pattern¶

    7:59 And they stoned Stephen, calling upon [God], and saying, Lord Jesus, receive my spirit. 7:60 And he kneeled down, and cried with a loud voice, Lord, lay not this sin to their charge. And when he had said this, he fell asleep.

    Context: The martyrdom of Stephen, narrated by Luke. Stephen commits his spirit to Jesus (parallel to Jesus committing his spirit to the Father, Luke 23:46), then 'falls asleep' (ekoimEthE, from koimao G2837).

    Notes: The pattern: spirit-commitment (Ecc 12:7 fulfilled) + sleep (death described as unconsciousness). Jesus committed his spirit to the Father (Luke 23:46) but had 'not yet ascended' by Sunday (John 20:17). Spirit-commitment does NOT mean immediate conscious presence.

    Peter: body as tabernacle (skenoma) — parallel tent metaphor¶

    1:13 Yea, I think it meet, as long as I am in this tabernacle, to stir you up by putting [you] in remembrance; 1:14 Knowing that shortly I must put off [this] my tabernacle, even as our Lord Jesus Christ hath shewed me.

    Context: Peter uses skenoma (G4638) for his body, the same tent metaphor as Paul's skenos (G4636) in 2 Cor 5:1,4. Peter speaks of 'putting off' (apothesis G595) the tent at death.

    Notes: Peter uses different but cognate vocabulary for the same concept. He does not describe what happens after putting off the tent — he does not mention conscious existence between death and resurrection.

    Jesus: temple made with hands vs. another made without hands — acheiropoietos¶

    We heard him say, I will destroy this temple that is made with hands, and within three days I will build another made without hands.

    Context: False witnesses at Jesus' trial report his temple-body saying (cf. John 2:19-21). The cheiropoietos/acheiropoietos contrast parallels Paul's use of acheiropoieton in 2 Cor 5:1.

    Notes: acheiropoieton (G886) = 'not made with hands.' Same word used in 2 Cor 5:1 for the resurrection body/building from God. Jesus refers to the temple of his body (John 2:21) — the acheiropoietos temple is the resurrection body.

    OT source for katapino — swallow up death in victory¶

    He will swallow up death in victory; and the Lord GOD will wipe away tears from off all faces; and the rebuke of his people shall he take away from off all the earth: for the LORD hath spoken [it].

    Context: Isaiah's prophecy of the eschatological feast on Mount Zion. The Hebrew uses bala (H1104), rendered katapino in the LXX. Paul quotes this in 1 Cor 15:54 and alludes to it in 2 Cor 5:4.

    Notes: bala (H1104) = 'swallow.' LXX: katepien (aorist of katapino). Paul's KatepothE in 1 Cor 15:54 quotes this verse. His katapothE in 2 Cor 5:4 alludes to it. Both passages point to the same eschatological event — the future swallowing up of death/mortality.

    And the angels which kept not their first estate, but left their own habitation, he hath reserved in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day.

    Context: Jude's warning about fallen angels. oiketerion (G3613) appears only here and in 2 Cor 5:2. In Jude, it describes the proper dwelling/domain that fallen angels abandoned.

    Notes: oiketerion (G3613) only 2x in NT. In Jude, it refers to the proper assigned domain/habitation of angels. In 2 Cor 5:2, it refers to the heavenly dwelling (resurrection body) believers desire. Both uses describe a divinely assigned proper dwelling — one abandoned by angels, the other longed for by believers.

    OT resurrection hope: sleep in the dust, awake¶

    And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame [and] everlasting contempt.

    Context: Daniel's vision of the end time. Death described as 'sleep in the dust' — the standard OT vocabulary. Awakening comes at the eschatological hour.

    Notes: yasheney (from yashen H3462, 'to sleep') admat-aphar ('dust of the earth') — death as sleep in the dust, awaiting resurrection. This is the OT framework that Paul inherits.

    Context: Same letter as 2 Cor 5. Paul describes a visionary experience. He twice says he does NOT know whether he was in or out of the body — he does not equate being out of body with consciousness.

    Notes: Paul's agnosticism about his own body-state during this vision is significant. If he routinely expected conscious out-of-body existence, he would not express such uncertainty.

    Context: Jesus answering the Sadducees about resurrection. The key phrase in v.38: 'for all live unto him' (pantes gar auto zosin). Does this mean they are currently alive or that they will live (because God will resurrect them)?

    Context: God confronting Cain after Abel's murder. Abel's blood 'cries out' from the ground — figurative language for injustice demanding divine response.

    Notes: No one argues Abel's blood is literally conscious and speaking. This is the OT precedent for Rev 6:9-11's souls crying out — the blood/lives of martyrs cry out for justice, not because they are conscious.

    OT resurrection vision: valley of dry bones — bodily resurrection prophesied¶

    37:1 The hand of the LORD was upon me, and carried me out in the spirit of the LORD, and set me down in the midst of the valley which [was] full of bones, ... 37:10 So I prophesied as he commanded me, and the breath came into them, and they lived, and stood up upon their feet, an exceeding great army. ... 37:12 Therefore prophesy and say unto them, Thus saith the Lord GOD; Behold, O my people, I will open your graves, and cause you to come up out of your graves, and bring you into the land of Israel.

    Context: Ezekiel's vision of the dry bones. The bones are reassembled, covered with flesh, and receive breath/spirit (ruach) — they come to life. The pattern reverses the death of Gen 2:7 / Ecc 12:7.

    Notes: The resurrection pattern: bones + flesh + breath/spirit = living people. No intermediate conscious state is described — the dead are in graves (v.12-13), and God opens the graves. This is bodily resurrection, not soul-release.

    Core passage v.2: groaning, desiring to be clothed upon with heavenly dwelling¶

    For in this we groan, earnestly desiring to be clothed upon with our house which is from heaven:

    Context: Second verse of the core passage. Paul describes believers groaning (stenazomen, G4727) and earnestly desiring (epipothountes, G1971) to be clothed upon (ependysasthai, G1902) with the oiketerion (G3613) from heaven.

    Notes: stenazomen (V-PAI-1P) = same form as Rom 8:23. ependysasthai (V-AMN) = aorist middle infinitive of ependyomai G1902. oiketerion (G3613) = habitation/dwelling, only 2x NT (here and Jude 1:6). The groaning is for the heavenly body, not for death.

    Core passage v.4: three-part preference — NOT unclothed, BUT clothed upon, THAT mortality be swallowed by life¶

    For we that are in [this] tabernacle do groan, being burdened: not for that we would be unclothed, but clothed upon, that mortality might be swallowed up of life.

    Context: THE decisive verse. Paul explicitly states his preference using three elements: (1) ou thelomen ekdysasthai — we do NOT want to be unclothed; (2) all' ependysasthai — but [we want] to be clothed upon; (3) hina katapothE to thnEton hypo tEs zOEs — that mortality might be swallowed up by life.

    Notes: ou thelomen (V-PAI-1P, negated present of thelo G2309) = explicit non-preference for death. ekdysasthai (V-AMN, aorist middle infinitive of ekdyo G1562) = to be unclothed/stripped. ependysasthai (V-AMN, aorist middle infinitive of ependyomai G1902) = to be clothed upon/over. katapothE (V-APS-3S, aorist passive subjunctive of katapino G2666) = might be swallowed up. thneton (G2349) = mortality. zoes (G2222) = of life. The vocabulary (katapothE, thneton) directly links to 1 Cor 15:54.

    Core passage v.7: parenthetical — we walk by faith not by sight¶

    (For we walk by faith, not by sight:)

    Context: A parenthetical clause explaining why being 'at home in the body' means being 'absent from the Lord' (v.6). The present life is lived by faith (pistis, G4102), not by direct sight (eidos, G1491).

    Notes: This parenthetical explains the present tension: believers walk by faith, not by sight. Being 'absent from the Lord' in v.6 does not mean separated from God's care but refers to the indirect, faith-mediated relationship of the present age vs. the direct, face-to-face presence of the future.

    Immediate context: light affliction vs. eternal weight of glory, temporal vs. eternal¶

    Context: The temporal/eternal contrast that flows directly into 5:1. The 'light affliction' corresponds to the groaning in the tent (5:2,4). The 'eternal weight of glory' corresponds to the building from God (5:1).

    Notes: The seen/unseen and temporal/eternal contrasts in 4:18 set up the tent/building contrast in 5:1. The logical flow is continuous across the chapter boundary.

    Same-author: if only in this life we have hope — resurrection as THE hope¶

    15:19 If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men most miserable. 15:20 But now is Christ risen from the dead, [and] become the firstfruits of them that slept.

    Context: Part of Paul's resurrection argument. If hope in Christ is only for this life (i.e., there is no resurrection), believers are most pitiable. The resurrection of Christ as 'firstfruits' guarantees the future resurrection of believers.

    Notes: If believers who die are already consciously with Christ in bliss, Paul could not say hope limited to this life makes them 'most miserable.' The logic requires resurrection as the essential basis of hope, not an intermediate conscious state.

    Supporting: Jesus commends his spirit to the Father — spirit-commitment pattern¶

    And when Jesus had cried with a loud voice, he said, Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit: and having said thus, he gave up the ghost.

    Context: Jesus on the cross, at the moment of death. He commits his spirit (pneuma) to the Father, echoing Psa 31:5 and the pattern of Ecc 12:7.

    Notes: Jesus commended his spirit to the Father on Friday. But by Sunday morning he told Mary he had 'not yet ascended to my Father' (John 20:17). Therefore, committing one's spirit to God does NOT mean immediate conscious presence with God. This is decisive for interpreting Acts 7:59 and the spirit-commitment pattern.

    Supporting: Jesus had not yet ascended — spirit-commitment did NOT mean immediate presence¶

    Jesus saith unto her, Touch me not; for I am not yet ascended to my Father: but go to my brethren, and say unto them, I ascend unto my Father, and your Father; and [to] my God, and your God.

    Context: Easter Sunday morning. Jesus tells Mary Magdalene he has 'not yet ascended' to the Father — even though he commended his spirit to the Father on Friday (Luke 23:46).

    Notes: oupo anabebeka (V-RAI-1S, perfect active indicative of anabaino G305) = 'I have not yet ascended.' The perfect tense confirms a present state — as of Sunday morning, Jesus has not been in the Father's presence. This directly refutes the claim that committing one's spirit means immediate conscious heavenly presence.

    OT: same ruach for man and beast, both return to dust¶

    3:19 For that which befalleth the sons of men befalleth beasts; even one thing befalleth them: as the one dieth, so dieth the other; yea, they have all one breath; so that a man hath no preeminence above a beast: for all [is] vanity. 3:20 All go unto one place; all are of the dust, and all turn to dust again. 3:21 Who knoweth the spirit of man that goeth upward, and the spirit of the beast that goeth downward to the earth?

    Context: Solomon's observation on the shared mortality of humans and animals. Both have the same ruach (breath/spirit). Both return to dust. The question in v.21 is rhetorical — who can demonstrate a difference?

    Notes: ruach echad (one breath/spirit) — man and beast share the same animating principle. If ruach returning to God at death (Ecc 12:7) proved conscious survival, it would prove the same for animals. The shared ruach demonstrates that the spirit/breath is the life-force, not a conscious immortal entity.

    OT death-state: no work, device, knowledge, or wisdom in sheol¶

    Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do [it] with thy might; for [there is] no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest.

    Context: Solomon's exhortation to live fully, grounded in the reality that death ends all activity. Sheol (the grave) is characterized by the complete absence of work, thought, knowledge, and wisdom.

    Notes: Four categories of conscious activity are negated: work (maaseh), device/planning (cheshbon), knowledge (daat), wisdom (chokmah). This is the most comprehensive OT description of death as the cessation of all mental and practical activity.

    OT resurrection hope: thy dead shall live, awake and sing ye that dwell in dust¶

    Context: Isaiah's prophecy of future resurrection. The dead 'dwell in dust' (not in heaven) and will 'awake and sing' — resurrection from the dust, not release from a conscious intermediate state.

    Notes: The dead are described as 'dwelling in dust' — their location is the grave, not heaven. The resurrection is an awakening (haqitsu) from death's sleep. Parallels Dan 12:2.

    OT resurrection hope: in my flesh shall I see God¶

    Context: Job's declaration of faith in bodily resurrection despite present suffering. His hope is to see God 'in my flesh' — bodily resurrection, not disembodied vision.

    Notes: Job expects to see God 'in my flesh' (mibbsari) — bodily, physical resurrection. The 'latter day' (acharown) places this at the eschatological horizon, not at death. This is the OT resurrection hope that Paul inherits.

    Same-author: some are fallen asleep — death-as-sleep vocabulary¶

    After that, he was seen of above five hundred brethren at once; of whom the greater part remain unto this present, but some are fallen asleep.

    Context: Paul's list of resurrection witnesses. He notes that some witnesses have since died, using the sleep euphemism (kekoimentai, from koimao G2837).

    Notes: kekoimentai (V-RPI-3P, perfect passive indicative of koimao G2837) = 'have fallen asleep' (have died). Paul's consistent vocabulary for death is sleep — implying unconsciousness and expected awakening.

    Same-author: resurrection order — Christ, then his at his coming, then the end¶

    15:23 But every man in his own order: Christ the firstfruits; afterward they that are Christ's at his coming. 15:24 Then [cometh] the end, when he shall have delivered up the kingdom to God, even the Father; when he shall have put down all rule and all authority and power. 15:25 For he must reign, till he hath put all enemies under his feet. 15:26 The last enemy [that] shall be destroyed [is] death.

    Context: Paul's timeline for resurrection events. The order is: (1) Christ as firstfruits, (2) believers at his coming (parousia), (3) the end. Death is the LAST enemy destroyed — not at each individual's death, but at the final resurrection.

    Notes: en te parousia autou (v.23) = 'at his coming' — believers are resurrected at the parousia, not at death. Death as the 'last enemy' (v.26) is destroyed at the final resurrection, not piecemeal as each believer dies and enters conscious bliss.

    Jesus: fear him who can destroy both soul and body in gehenna — soul is not immortal¶

    And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell.

    Context: Jesus' instruction to the twelve before sending them out. He distinguishes between those who can kill the body (human persecutors) and God who can destroy (apolesai, from apollymi G622) both soul and body in gehenna.

    Notes: apolesai (V-AAN, aorist active infinitive of apollymi G622) = 'to destroy.' God can DESTROY the psyche (soul, G5590). If the soul were inherently immortal and indestructible, Jesus' warning would be meaningless. The soul/body distinction here is functional (what humans can vs. what God can affect), not ontological (an immortal substance vs. a mortal container).

    OT clothing metaphor: garments of salvation, robe of righteousness¶

    I will greatly rejoice in the LORD, my soul shall be joyful in my God; for he hath clothed me with the garments of salvation, he hath covered me with the robe of righteousness, as a bridegroom decketh [himself] with ornaments, and as a bride adorneth [herself] with her jewels.

    Context: Isaiah's clothing metaphor for salvation. Being 'clothed with garments of salvation' uses the same conceptual framework Paul employs in 2 Cor 5 — clothing as acquisition of a divine gift.

    Notes: The OT background for Paul's clothing metaphor. Salvation is depicted as a garment placed upon the person — something received, not something inherent. This supports the endyo/ependyomai = acquisition reading.

    Same-author: put on the Lord Jesus Christ — endyo metaphor for acquisition¶

    But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to [fulfil] the lusts [thereof].

    Context: Paul's ethical exhortation. Believers are to 'put on' (endysasthe, from endyo G1746) Christ — the clothing metaphor applied to identity and character.

    Notes: endysasthe (V-AMM-2P, aorist middle imperative of endyo G1746) = 'put on.' Every Pauline use of endyo describes acquisition of something not previously worn. This confirms the 2 Cor 5 / 1 Cor 15 clothing metaphor means receiving, not revealing.

    Same-author: put on Christ in baptism — clothing metaphor as new identity¶

    For as many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ.

    Context: Paul's baptism teaching. Baptism is described as 'putting on' (enedysasthe, from endyo G1746) Christ — acquisition of new identity.

    Notes: enedysasthe (V-AMI-2P, aorist middle indicative of endyo G1746) = 'you put on.' Another Pauline use of endyo confirming the acquisition sense of the clothing metaphor.

    Same-author: bare grain — gymnos applied to the seed sown before receiving its body¶

    And that which thou sowest, thou sowest not that body that shall be, but bare grain, it may chance of wheat, or of some other [grain]:

    Context: Paul's seed analogy for resurrection. The 'bare' (gymnon, G1131) grain is sown without the body it will receive — paralleling the 'naked' state Paul fears in 2 Cor 5:3.

    Notes: gymnon (G1131) = 'bare/naked.' The same word as gymnoi in 2 Cor 5:3. The bare grain = the dead person without a resurrection body. Being 'found naked' (2 Cor 5:3) = being in the death-state without the resurrection body — the undesirable intermediate condition.

    OT: ransom from the grave, victory over death — quoted by Paul in 1 Cor 15:55¶

    I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death: O death, I will be thy plagues; O grave, I will be thy destruction: repentance shall be hid from mine eyes.

    Context: Hosea's prophecy of God's victory over death and the grave. Paul quotes this in 1 Cor 15:55 alongside Isa 25:8. Both OT sources place the victory over death at a future eschatological event, not at each individual's death.

    Notes: sheol (H7585) = 'grave/death-realm.' maveth (H4194) = 'death.' Paul combines this verse with Isa 25:8 in 1 Cor 15:54-55 to describe the resurrection event. The victory over death is future and corporate, not individual and immediate.

    OT: Hezekiah's lament about death — cutting off of days, tent removed¶

    38:10 I said in the cutting off of my days, I shall go to the gates of the grave: I am deprived of the residue of my years. 38:11 I said, I shall not see the LORD, [even] the LORD, in the land of the living: I shall behold man no more with the inhabitants of the world. 38:12 Mine age is departed, and is removed from me as a shepherd's tent: I have cut off like a weaver my life: he will cut me off with pining sickness: from day [even] to night wilt thou make an end of me. 38:13 I reckoned till morning, [that], as a lion, so will he break all my bones: from day [even] to night wilt thou make an end of me.

    Context: Hezekiah's lament when facing death. He describes death as: going to the gates of sheol, not seeing the LORD in the land of the living, and his life being removed 'as a shepherd's tent.'

    Notes: V.12: 'removed from me as a shepherd's tent' (ke-ohel roi) — the tent metaphor for the mortal body, the same image Paul uses in 2 Cor 5:1. V.11: 'I shall not see the LORD in the land of the living' — death means NOT seeing God, the opposite of conscious heavenly presence. Continues into vv.18-19 where the dead cannot praise God.
