    Verse-by-Verse Analysis¶

    Question¶

    Matthew 10:28 -- "And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell." What does this verse teach about the fate of the wicked? Same-author usage analysis of psyche (G5590), apollymi (G622), gehenna (G1067), and phobeo (G5399) in Matthew's Gospel.

    The Crux Text¶

    Matthew 10:28¶

    Context: Jesus' commissioning discourse to the twelve apostles (Matt 10:1-42). After warning of persecution (v.16-23), Jesus delivers three "fear not" commands (v.26, v.28, v.31). The verse builds an a fortiori argument: humans can only kill the body; God can destroy both soul and body in gehenna.

    Direct statement: God is able to destroy (apollymi, G622) both soul (psyche, G5590) and body (soma, G4983) in gehenna (G1067). Human persecutors can kill (apokteino, G615) the body but lack the ability to kill the soul.

    Original language: The parser confirms six key grammatical features: 1. phobeisthe (V-PNM-2P) -- present middle/passive imperative. The me + present imperative construction means "stop fearing" or "do not continue fearing" -- the disciples are in a situation where fear is natural, and Jesus commands them to cease that ongoing fear. 2. apokteinai (V-AAN) -- aorist active infinitive of apokteino (G615). Complementary infinitive completing dynamai -- what persecutors are "able" to do: kill the body. 3. apolesai (V-AAN) -- aorist active infinitive of apollymi (G622). Complementary infinitive completing dynamai -- what God is "able" to do: destroy soul and body. Both infinitives present their actions as complete events (aorist aspect). 4. The verb shift from apokteino ("kill") to apollymi ("destroy") signals a qualitative difference. If Jesus meant God merely kills the soul the way humans kill the body, he could have repeated apokteino. He escalated to apollymi -- a word meaning "to destroy fully" (apo + ollymi), the canonical NT heir to Hebrew abad (H6, 141 LXX mappings, PMI 6.14). 5. kai...kai (G2532...G2532) -- correlative conjunction binding psychen and soma into a single object of apolesai. This "both...and" construction emphasizes totality: God destroys the complete person, not merely one component. 6. en geenne (N-DSF) -- dative of place. The destruction occurs in gehenna, not hades. Gehenna collocates with fire (6x) and apollymi (3x) across the NT; it never collocates with basanizo (G928) for human subjects.

    Cross-references: Luke 12:4-5 (synoptic parallel), Matt 5:29-30 (same author, apollymi + gehenna + soma), Matt 10:39 (same discourse, psyche + apollymi = "lose one's life"), James 4:12 (sozo/apollymi antonym pair applied to God), Ezek 18:4 (nephesh + muwth = "the soul that sins shall die").

    Relationship to other evidence: This verse is the intersection of all four key word studies. Every Matthean usage pattern for each word converges here. Apollymi in judgment contexts means actual destruction (21:41, 22:7). Psyche in same-discourse usage means "life" (10:39). Gehenna is consistently paired with destruction/judgment vocabulary, never torment vocabulary. Phobeo in the present imperative establishes an ongoing posture of reverential fear.

    Matthean Psyche (G5590) Occurrences¶

    Matthew 2:20¶

    Context: Angel tells Joseph: "they are dead which sought the young child's life (psyche)." First occurrence of psyche in Matthew.

    Direct statement: Psyche = physical life that was sought/threatened. Those who sought Jesus' psyche are themselves dead.

    Original language: Psyche here denotes physical life under threat of violence. Matthew's first use of the word establishes its basic meaning: the life of a person that can be taken by killing.

    Cross-references: Matt 2:13 uses apollymi for Herod's intent to "destroy" the child -- the same verb that appears in 10:28. The psyche sought in 2:20 is the same entity Herod would apollymi in 2:13.

    Relationship to other evidence: Establishes that Matthew's baseline use of psyche is "physical life," not "immortal soul."

    Matthew 6:25¶

    Context: Sermon on the Mount. Jesus says "Take no thought for your life (psyche), what ye shall eat, or what ye shall drink; nor yet for your body (soma), what ye shall put on."

    Direct statement: Psyche here is paired with soma as two aspects of physical existence: psyche = what you eat/drink (sustenance), soma = what you wear (covering). Both are earthly, physical concerns.

    Original language: The psyche/soma pairing in 6:25 describes parallel physical concerns. In 10:28, the same psyche/soma pairing appears as the dual object of divine destruction in gehenna. The 6:25 usage shows Matthew does not treat psyche as an immaterial entity separate from physical existence -- he pairs it with soma as two aspects of the same embodied life.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (same word pair, different context -- destruction in gehenna).

    Relationship to other evidence: The psyche/soma pairing reinforces that Matthew treats psyche and soma as components of the complete person, not as separable entities with different ontological statuses.

    Matthew 10:39¶

    Context: Same commissioning discourse as 10:28, just 11 verses later. "He that findeth his life (psyche) shall lose (apollymi) it: and he that loseth (apollymi) his life (psyche) for my sake shall find it."

    Direct statement: The identical word pair psyche + apollymi from 10:28 appears here meaning "lose one's life," not "destroy an immaterial soul."

    Original language: Parser confirms: apollymi appears as V-FAI-3S (apolesai, future active indicative) and V-AAP-NSM (apolesas, aorist active participle). Psyche is N-ASF (accusative singular feminine) -- morphologically identical to its form in 10:28. The semantic shift between 10:28 (divine judgment) and 10:39 (losing life for Christ's sake) operates within the same discourse, demonstrating Matthew's flexibility with the word pair while maintaining the core sense of loss/cessation.

    Cross-references: Matt 16:25 (same paradox in different discourse), Luke 9:24 (synoptic parallel).

    Relationship to other evidence: This is the single most important same-author datum for interpreting 10:28. The same writer, in the same speech, uses psyche + apollymi to mean "lose one's life." The "life" meaning is not imported from a distant context -- it is the immediate literary context.

    Matthew 11:29¶

    Context: Jesus' invitation: "Take my yoke upon you...and ye shall find rest unto your souls (psyche)."

    Direct statement: Psyche = the inner self that experiences weariness and rest.

    Original language: Psyche here denotes the inner person, the seat of existential burden and relief. This is the "inner self" sense, not a statement about immortality or immateriality.

    Cross-references: Jer 6:16 (OT rest for the soul); Ps 23:3 (restoring the nephesh).

    Relationship to other evidence: Demonstrates the breadth of psyche's semantic range in Matthew -- from physical life (2:20, 6:25, 10:39, 20:28) to inner person (11:29, 22:37, 26:38).

    Matthew 12:18¶

    Context: Matthew quoting Isa 42:1: "my beloved, in whom my soul (psyche) is well pleased."

    Direct statement: Psyche applied to God -- reflecting OT nephesh language where God speaks of "my nephesh."

    Original language: This OT quotation preserves the anthropomorphic nephesh-language of Isaiah. God's psyche is "well pleased" -- psyche here denotes the seat of divine delight, following the OT pattern where nephesh is applied to God (Gen 6:6; Judg 10:16; Isa 1:14).

    Cross-references: Isa 42:1 (source quotation using nephesh).

    Relationship to other evidence: If psyche can apply to God without implying that God is a "soul" in the Greek philosophical sense, then the word does not carry inherent ontological implications when applied to humans either.

    Matthew 16:25-26¶

    Context: Following Peter's confession and the first passion prediction. "For whosoever will save his life (psyche) shall lose (apollymi) it: and whosoever will lose (apollymi) his life (psyche) for my sake shall find it. For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul (psyche)?"

    Direct statement: The same word psyche is translated "life" in v.25 and "soul" in v.26 within the same two-verse passage. The KJV translation shift from "life" to "soul" is an English artifact -- the Greek word is identical in both verses.

    Original language: Apollymi appears in v.25 (lose one's psyche). The psyche + apollymi word pair recurs for the third time in Matthew (after 10:28 and 10:39). In v.26, the KJV switches to "soul" for the same word, but the meaning remains the whole self/life that is at stake. The question "what shall a man give in exchange for his psyche?" treats psyche as something that can be forfeited -- lost permanently.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28, 10:39 (earlier uses of same word pair).

    Relationship to other evidence: The translation variation within two verses demonstrates that the English "life/soul" distinction does not map to a conceptual difference in Greek. Matthew uses psyche for the whole self that can be lost (apollymi) or found.

    Matthew 20:28¶

    Context: "Even as the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life (psyche) a ransom for many."

    Direct statement: Psyche = physical life given up in death. Jesus' psyche is the ransom price -- he surrenders it in death.

    Original language: Psyche here clearly denotes physical life laid down in death. Jesus' psyche is mortal -- he gives it up on the cross. This usage is incompatible with a naturally immortal psyche that survives bodily death.

    Cross-references: Mark 10:45 (parallel), Isa 53:10-12 (nephesh = guilt offering).

    Relationship to other evidence: Jesus' own psyche is something he gives up in death. If even Jesus' psyche can be surrendered in death, psyche is not inherently deathless.

    Matthew 22:37¶

    Context: Jesus quoting the Shema (Deut 6:5): "Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul (psyche), and with all thy mind."

    Direct statement: Psyche = the whole self/devotion -- standard OT nephesh usage in the covenant formula.

    Original language: The Shema quotation uses psyche for wholehearted devotion, reflecting nephesh in Deut 6:5. In the OT, "all your nephesh" means "your entire self" -- it is an intensity marker, not a metaphysical claim about an immortal component.

    Cross-references: Deut 6:5 (source quotation), Mark 12:30, Luke 10:27 (parallel).

    Relationship to other evidence: Another instance of psyche denoting the whole person, not a separable immortal component.

    Matthew 26:38¶

    Context: Gethsemane. "My soul (psyche) is exceeding sorrowful, even unto death (heos thanatou)."

    Direct statement: Jesus says his psyche is sorrowful "unto death." His psyche can reach the point of death -- it is mortal.

    Original language: The phrase heos thanatou ("even unto death") directly connects psyche to thanatos (death). If psyche were inherently immortal, the phrase "my psyche is sorrowful unto death" would be incoherent -- an immortal entity cannot approach death. Matthew records Jesus describing his own psyche as mortal.

    Cross-references: Ps 42:5-6 (OT nephesh sorrowful), Jonah 4:9 (nephesh wishes for death).

    Relationship to other evidence: Matthew's most personal use of psyche: Jesus' own inner self approaches death. This is the last Matthean occurrence before the crucifixion and confirms that psyche, even in Jesus' self-description, is subject to death.

    Matthean Apollymi (G622) Occurrences¶

    Matthew 2:13¶

    Context: "Herod will seek the young child to destroy (apollymi) him."

    Direct statement: Apollymi = literal physical destruction (killing the child). First occurrence of apollymi in Matthew.

    Original language: The articular purpose construction tou apolesai (T-GSN + V-AAN) marks the explicit purpose: "to destroy" the child. Active voice, deliberate intent. Matthew's first use of apollymi is for murderous intent producing physical death.

    Cross-references: Matt 2:20 (those who sought the child's psyche are dead -- connecting apollymi with psyche from the start of the Gospel).

    Relationship to other evidence: Establishes the baseline: apollymi in Matthew's opening narrative means killing/physical destruction.

    Matthew 5:29-30¶

    Context: Sermon on the Mount. "It is profitable for thee that one of thy members should perish (apollymi), and not that thy whole body should be cast into hell (gehenna)."

    Direct statement: A member perishing (apollymi) is set in parallel with the whole body being cast into gehenna. The apollymi + gehenna collocation that appears in 10:28 first occurs here.

    Original language: Parser confirms apoletai = V-2AMS-3S (aorist middle subjunctive) of apollymi. The verb describes a member "perishing" (ceasing to function/exist). The contrast is: lose a part (apollymi) or lose the whole body to gehenna. This pairs apollymi with gehenna, as does 10:28.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (same apollymi + gehenna + soma vocabulary), Matt 18:9 (same body-in-gehenna language).

    Relationship to other evidence: First occurrence of the apollymi-gehenna collocation in Matthew. The pattern established here (apollymi = loss/destruction, gehenna = the place where it happens) carries through to 10:28.

    Matthew 8:25¶

    Context: Storm on the sea. "Lord, save (sozo) us: we perish (apollymi)."

    Direct statement: Apollymi = facing physical death by drowning. Sozo (save) and apollymi (perish) appear as antonyms.

    Original language: Apollymi in the middle voice = perishing (physical death). The sozo-apollymi antithesis appears here for the first time in Matthew: "save us from perishing."

    Cross-references: Matt 18:11 (sozo + apollymi antonym pair), James 4:12 (same antonym pair applied to God).

    Relationship to other evidence: Establishes the sozo-apollymi antithesis that constrains apollymi's meaning in judgment contexts: what sozo rescues from is what apollymi inflicts.

    Matthew 9:17¶

    Context: "The bottles break, and the wine runneth out, and the bottles perish (apollymi)."

    Direct statement: Apollymi used for the destruction of inanimate objects (wineskins).

    Original language: Apollymi describes wineskins that cease to function -- they are ruined. This demonstrates the "ruin/destruction" end of the semantic range. The wineskins do not continue in a ruined state forever; they are destroyed.

    Cross-references: No direct cross-reference to judgment passages.

    Relationship to other evidence: Shows the breadth of apollymi's range but does not determine its meaning in judgment contexts. Context, not the bare lexical entry, determines which sense applies in each occurrence.

    Matthew 10:6¶

    Context: Same commissioning discourse as 10:28. "Go rather to the lost (apollymi) sheep of the house of Israel."

    Direct statement: Apollymi in the perfect middle participle = "lost/straying" (recoverable condition).

    Original language: The participle apolololota (perfect middle) describes an ongoing state of being lost. This is the "lost/straying" sense of apollymi -- middle voice, describing the condition of Israel's sheep. Distinct from the active voice "destroy" sense used in 10:28 (just 22 verses later in the same discourse).

    Cross-references: Matt 15:24 (same formula), Matt 18:11 (same concept).

    Relationship to other evidence: The "lost sheep" usage is middle voice and describes a recoverable condition. The judgment usage in 10:28 is active voice and describes God's ability to destroy. Same word, different voice, different sense.

    Matthew 12:14¶

    Context: "Then the Pharisees went out, and held a council against him, how they might destroy (apollymi) him."

    Direct statement: Apollymi = plot to kill Jesus. Active voice, subjunctive mood -- deliberate planning of lethal action.

    Original language: Human agents using apollymi for murderous intent. The same verb used of God's action in 10:28, but there God's scope extends beyond mere killing to destroying soul AND body.

    Cross-references: Matt 27:20 (same verb, same intent), Matt 2:13 (Herod's intent to apollymi Jesus).

    Relationship to other evidence: Human apollymi is limited to killing the body (as 10:28 states). Divine apollymi encompasses both soul and body.

    Matthew 15:24¶

    Context: "I am not sent but unto the lost (apollymi) sheep of the house of Israel."

    Direct statement: Same "lost sheep" formula as 10:6. Apollymi = straying/lost (recoverable condition).

    Original language: Second use of the perfect middle participle for "lost." The straying sense persists when the voice is middle.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:6 (identical formula).

    Relationship to other evidence: Confirms the pattern: middle voice apollymi = lost/straying; active voice apollymi = destroy.

    Matthew 18:11¶

    Context: "For the Son of man is come to save (sozo) that which was lost (apollymi)."

    Direct statement: Sozo and apollymi as antonyms. What sozo rescues from is what apollymi describes. The Son of Man saves (preserves life for) what would otherwise perish (lose life).

    Original language: The sozo-apollymi antithesis at its most direct in Matthew. If sozo = "give/preserve life," then apollymi = "remove/end life."

    Cross-references: James 4:12 (God able to sozo and apollymi), John 3:16 (not perish but have everlasting life).

    Relationship to other evidence: The sozo-apollymi antithesis constrains the meaning of apollymi in 10:28. God can destroy (end) what He also has the power to save (preserve).

    Matthew 18:14¶

    Context: "It is not the will of your Father which is in heaven, that one of these little ones should perish (apollymi)."

    Direct statement: God does not will that any should apollymi (perish). The Father's will is set against apollymi.

    Original language: Apollymi in the aorist middle subjunctive (apolhetai) = perish. The Father's will opposes apollymi -- this is incompatible with the idea that God actively preserves the wicked alive in eternal torment (which would require willing their ongoing conscious existence rather than their perishing).

    Cross-references: 2 Pet 3:9 ("not willing that any should perish"), John 3:16.

    Relationship to other evidence: The Father wills that none should perish (apollymi). The wicked who reject God face apollymi -- the very thing the Father sought to prevent.

    Matthew 21:41¶

    Context: Parable of the wicked husbandmen. "He will miserably destroy (apollymi) those wicked men."

    Direct statement: The vineyard owner (representing God/authority) apollymi the wicked tenants. Actual destruction by authority.

    Original language: Parser confirms apolesei = V-FAI-3S (future active indicative) of apollymi. Active voice, future tense -- a prediction of what the authority will do to the wicked. Judgment apollymi = actual destruction.

    Cross-references: Matt 22:7 (same pattern -- authority destroys the wicked), Matt 10:28 (God destroys soul and body).

    Relationship to other evidence: When Matthew records God or authority figures using apollymi on the wicked, the result is actual destruction. Three instances: 10:28 (God destroys soul and body), 21:41 (vineyard lord destroys wicked tenants), 22:7 (king destroys murderers). All are active voice, all describe actual destruction.

    Matthew 22:7¶

    Context: Parable of the marriage feast. "He sent forth his armies, and destroyed (apollymi) those murderers, and burned up their city."

    Direct statement: The king (representing God) apollymi the murderers AND burned their city. Destruction + fire vocabulary together.

    Original language: Parser confirms apolesen = V-AAI-3S (aorist active indicative). Destruction + burning (empiprhmi). Apollymi is paired with fire vocabulary, echoing 10:28 where apollymi is paired with gehenna (a fire-associated location). When God/king destroys the wicked, the result is actual destruction accompanied by fire, not ongoing preservation in fire.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (apollymi + gehenna), Matt 3:12 (burn up chaff with unquenchable fire).

    Relationship to other evidence: The third and final judgment-apollymi passage in Matthew. All three (10:28, 21:41, 22:7) use active voice and describe actual destruction by an authority figure acting on the wicked.

    Matthew 27:20¶

    Context: "The chief priests and elders persuaded the multitude...to destroy (apollymi) Jesus."

    Direct statement: Apollymi = kill/execute. Human agents seeking to execute Jesus.

    Original language: Active voice. Another instance of human destructive intent. Same verb as 10:28 but limited to physical killing -- humans cannot apollymi the psyche (as 10:28 states).

    Cross-references: Matt 2:13 (Herod's intent), Matt 12:14 (Pharisees' intent).

    Relationship to other evidence: Human apollymi = killing the body only. Divine apollymi = destroying both soul and body. The scope difference is precisely what 10:28 asserts.

    Matthean Gehenna (G1067) Occurrences¶

    Matthew 5:22¶

    Context: Sermon on the Mount. "Whosoever shall say, Thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire (geenne tou pyros)."

    Direct statement: First occurrence of gehenna in Matthew. Gehenna is paired with fire (pyr) in an escalating series of judgments: judgment → council → gehenna fire.

    Original language: Gehenna + fire is the first and dominant collocation pattern in Matthew. No torment vocabulary appears in this passage.

    Cross-references: Matt 5:29-30 (same context, body in gehenna), Matt 18:9 (gehenna fire).

    Relationship to other evidence: Establishes the gehenna + fire collocation from the first mention. The pattern continues through all Matthean gehenna passages.

    Matthew 18:9¶

    Context: "It is better for thee to enter into life (zoe) with one eye, rather than having two eyes to be cast into hell fire (geenne tou pyros)."

    Direct statement: The life-vs-gehenna contrast. The alternative to gehenna is zoe (life). Not happiness vs. suffering, but life vs. fiery destruction.

    Original language: Zoe (G2222) and gehenna (G1067) are mutually exclusive destinations. The same antithesis appears in Mark 9:43, 45, 47. Gehenna is the alternative to life, framing gehenna as the place where life ends.

    Cross-references: Mark 9:43-48 (parallel with Isa 66:24 allusion), Matt 5:29-30 (earlier body-in-gehenna passage).

    Relationship to other evidence: The life/gehenna antithesis reinforces the destruction reading of 10:28. If the alternative to gehenna is life, then gehenna is the place where life does not continue.

    Matthew 23:15¶

    Context: "Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees...ye make him twofold more the child of hell (huios geennes) than yourselves."

    Direct statement: "Son of gehenna" -- a Semitic idiom of destiny (cf. "son of perdition," John 17:12). Gehenna is the destiny, described as a destination, not as a place of ongoing conscious experience.

    Original language: The Semitic idiom "son of X" identifies the person's ultimate destiny. "Son of gehenna" = one destined for gehenna. This parallels "son of perdition" (huios tes apoleias, John 17:12; 2 Thess 2:3) -- both using cognates of apollymi.

    Cross-references: John 17:12 (son of perdition/apoleia), Matt 23:33 (damnation of gehenna).

    Relationship to other evidence: The destiny-idiom connects gehenna (place) with apoleia (destruction-noun) -- both cognates of apollymi point to the same outcome.

    Matthew 23:33¶

    Context: "Ye serpents, ye generation of vipers, how can ye escape the damnation of hell (krisis tes geennes)?"

    Direct statement: Gehenna paired with krisis (judgment/condemnation). Not gehenna + basanizo (torment), but gehenna + krisis (judgment). Judgment vocabulary, not torment vocabulary.

    Original language: Krisis (G2920) = judgment/condemnation. The collocation pattern remains consistent: gehenna pairs with judgment/destruction vocabulary throughout Matthew, never with torment vocabulary.

    Cross-references: Matt 5:22 (gehenna + fire), Matt 10:28 (gehenna + apollymi), Matt 18:9 (gehenna + fire).

    Relationship to other evidence: Completes the Matthean gehenna vocabulary pattern: fire (5:22, 18:9), apollymi/destroy (10:28, 5:29-30), krisis/judgment (23:33), destiny-idiom (23:15). No occurrence of basanizo (torment) with gehenna in any NT passage.

    Matthean Phobeo (G5399) Occurrences¶

    Matthew 10:26 (Pericope context)¶

    Context: "Fear (phobeo) them not therefore: for there is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed."

    Direct statement: First "fear not" in the pericope. Introduces the fear hierarchy that peaks in 10:28.

    Original language: Me phobeisthe (V-PNM-2P) -- present middle/passive imperative with me. Same form as 10:28. "Stop fearing" the persecutors.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (second "fear not"), Matt 10:31 (third "fear not").

    Relationship to other evidence: Part of the tripartite "fear not" structure (v.26, v.28, v.31) that frames the a fortiori argument.

    Matthew 10:31¶

    Context: "Fear ye not therefore, ye are of more value than many sparrows."

    Direct statement: Third "fear not" in the pericope. After the warning (v.28), reassurance (v.31): God who can destroy also values and protects.

    Original language: Me phobeisthe (V-PNM-2P) -- same form as v.26 and v.28. The shift from warning (v.28: fear God who destroys) to reassurance (v.31: you are valued by God) completes the a fortiori argument.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (warning), Matt 10:29-30 (sparrow analogy).

    Relationship to other evidence: The reassurance in v.31 presupposes the warning in v.28. The disciples should not fear persecutors (v.26) because God, who has the greater power to destroy (v.28), actually values them and protects them (v.29-31).

    Matthew 14:27¶

    Context: Jesus walking on the water. "Be of good cheer; it is I; be not afraid (me phobeisthe)."

    Direct statement: "Fear not" -- same present imperative construction as 10:28. Phobeo for physical fear being addressed.

    Original language: Me phobeisthe (V-PNM-2P). The disciples are currently afraid (the action is ongoing), and Jesus commands them to stop. Same construction, different context -- showing that Matthew's "fear not" pattern is a signature of Jesus' speech.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (same form), Matt 17:7 (same form), Matt 28:5, 10 (same form).

    Relationship to other evidence: Part of Matthew's pattern of 8 "fear not" commands: 1:20, 10:26, 10:28, 10:31, 14:27, 17:7, 28:5, 28:10.

    Matthew 14:30¶

    Context: "But when he saw the wind boisterous, he was afraid (phobeo); and beginning to sink, he cried, saying, Lord, save (sozo) me."

    Direct statement: Phobeo for physical terror, followed by a cry for sozo (save). The phobeo-sozo pairing: Peter fears and cries "save me."

    Original language: Phobeo used for natural physical fear. The pairing with sozo reinforces the sozo-apollymi antithesis: Peter fears (phobeo) physical death and calls for rescue (sozo).

    Cross-references: Matt 8:25 (sozo-apollymi antithesis in the same storm context).

    Relationship to other evidence: Phobeo in physical contexts denotes terror before a lethal threat.

    Matthew 17:6-7¶

    Context: Transfiguration. "They fell on their face, and were sore afraid (phobeo)...Arise, and be not afraid (me phobeisthe)."

    Direct statement: Phobeo for numinous dread -- terror before the divine theophany. Jesus' response: "stop fearing" (me phobeisthe, same construction as 10:28).

    Original language: Phobeo here denotes overwhelming awe/terror before the supernatural. The disciples' response to God's manifest presence is fear. Jesus' "fear not" command (present imperative) addresses an ongoing state.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (same form), Exod 20:18-20 (Israel fears at Sinai; Moses says "fear not").

    Relationship to other evidence: The transfiguration shows that phobeo before the divine is a natural human response. Matt 10:28 redirects this natural fear: do not fear humans who can only kill the body; rather, maintain this reverent fear toward God who can destroy both soul and body.

    Matthew 28:4-5¶

    Context: Resurrection morning. Guards shake with phobos (fear noun, G5401). Angel says "Fear not (me phobeisthe) ye" to the women.

    Direct statement: Phobeo/phobos at the resurrection. The guards experience paralyzing terror. The women receive the "fear not" command -- same construction as 10:28.

    Original language: Phobos (noun, G5401) in v.4; phobeisthe (verb, V-PNM-2P) in v.5. The present imperative prohibition me phobeisthe is identical to 10:28a. Part of Matthew's bookend pattern: the Gospel begins with a "fear not" (1:20, Joseph) and ends with "fear not" commands (28:5, 10, at the resurrection).

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (same verb form), Matt 28:10 (another "fear not" from Jesus).

    Relationship to other evidence: The phobeo pattern bookends Matthew's Gospel, establishing "fear not" as Jesus' characteristic response to human fear throughout the narrative.

    Matthew 28:10¶

    Context: Post-resurrection appearance. "Be not afraid (me phobeisthe): go tell my brethren."

    Direct statement: Final phobeo occurrence in Matthew. Jesus' characteristic "fear not" command. The Gospel that opens with "fear not" (1:20) closes with "fear not" (28:10).

    Original language: Me phobeisthe (V-PNM-2P). Same form as 10:28. Matthew's final use of phobeo completes the arc: from Joseph's fear (1:20) to the women's fear (28:10), with the pivotal 10:28 at the center redirecting fear from human persecutors to God.

    Cross-references: Matt 1:20 (first "fear not"), Matt 10:28 (central "fear not").

    Relationship to other evidence: The phobeo pattern in Matthew serves a literary-theological function. Jesus consistently tells people not to fear -- except in 10:28, where he tells them TO fear God. The unique positive command to fear (10:28b: phobeisthe without me) marks the singular exception, making God's destructive power the one thing worthy of ongoing reverential fear.

    The Synoptic Parallel¶

    Luke 12:4-5¶

    Context: Luke's version of the same logion. Three key differences from Matt 10:28:

    Direct statement: (1) Luke uses aorist passive imperative phobethete (V-AOM-2P, decisive act of fearing) vs. Matthew's present imperative phobeisthe (V-PNM-2P, ongoing posture). (2) Luke uses apokteino (kill) for BOTH human and divine action, while Matthew escalates to apollymi (destroy). (3) Luke omits psyche entirely and adds a temporal sequence: "after he hath killed, hath power to cast into gehenna."

    Original language: The parser confirms: Luke 12:4 phobethete = V-AOM-2P (aorist passive imperative); Luke 12:5 apokteino = V-AAN (aorist active infinitive, same form as in Matt 10:28a); embalein = V-2AAN (aorist active infinitive of emballo, "to cast into"). Luke describes a two-step process: kill, then cast into gehenna. Matthew collapses this into one action: destroy both soul and body in gehenna.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (parallel).

    Relationship to other evidence: Matthew's distinctive contribution is the verb shift from apokteino to apollymi. Luke maintains apokteino throughout, emphasizing the sequence of events (kill, then cast). Matthew changes the verb to emphasize the nature of God's action (destroy, not merely kill). Both agree on the location (gehenna) and the reason for fear (God's ultimate authority over human destiny). Neither uses basanizo or any torment vocabulary.

    OT Gehenna Background¶

    Jeremiah 7:31-32¶

    Context: Jeremiah prophesying against the Valley of Hinnom. The site of child sacrifice will be renamed "Valley of Slaughter" -- death and burial, not ongoing torment.

    Direct statement: The Valley of Hinnom (ge-Hinnom = gehenna) will be a place of slaughter and burial. "They shall bury in Tophet, till there be no place." The OT background establishes gehenna as a place of death and corpse disposal.

    Original language: The vocabulary is haregah (slaughter) and qabar (bury). Death and burial -- not torment and ongoing consciousness. The valley will overflow with corpses.

    Cross-references: Jer 19:6 (same renaming), Josh 15:8 (original geographic reference).

    Relationship to other evidence: When Jesus uses "gehenna" in Matt 10:28, his audience would understand it against this OT background: a place of death, fire, and corpse disposal -- not a place of eternal conscious torment.

    Isaiah 66:24¶

    Context: Final verse of Isaiah. The righteous "look upon the carcases (peger) of the men that have transgressed."

    Direct statement: The subjects of the undying worm and unquenchable fire are peger -- corpses/carcasses. The word peger (H6297) is never used for a living person in the OT; it exclusively means "corpse/carcass."

    Original language: Parser confirms: pigrey = Noun.mp.Cst of peger (H6297). The subjects are dead bodies. The worm (tolahath) is a decomposition agent acting on corpses. The fire (esh) is "not quenched" -- meaning it cannot be extinguished before completing its work (same phrase used of Jerusalem's fire in Jer 17:27, which is not still burning).

    Cross-references: Mark 9:43-48 (quotes this passage), Matt 10:28 (gehenna context).

    Relationship to other evidence: Mark 9:43-48's gehenna passage directly alludes to Isa 66:24. The subjects of the worm and fire are corpses, not living persons in torment. This OT source text determines the meaning of the NT allusion.

    Joshua 15:8, 2 Chronicles 28:3, 2 Chronicles 33:6, 2 Kings 23:10, Jeremiah 19:6, Isaiah 30:33¶

    Context (collective): These passages trace the Valley of Hinnom from a geographic boundary marker (Josh 15:8) through its associations with child sacrifice (2 Chr 28:3, 33:6; 2 Ki 23:10), prophetic cursing (Jer 7:31-32, 19:6), and eschatological judgment (Isa 30:33).

    Direct statement: The OT trajectory of ge-Hinnom: geographic term → child sacrifice site → prophetically cursed → eschatological metaphor. At every stage, the associations are fire, death, and destruction -- not ongoing torment. Josiah defiled the valley (2 Ki 23:10). Jeremiah renamed it "Valley of Slaughter" (Jer 19:6). Isaiah prepared it with "fire and much wood" kindled by "the breath of the Lord" (Isa 30:33).

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (Jesus' use of gehenna draws on this background).

    Relationship to other evidence: The OT background of gehenna is fire + death + destruction. No OT passage uses the Valley of Hinnom as a place of ongoing torment. Jesus' audience would understand gehenna against this background.

    OT Nephesh Mortality¶

    Genesis 2:7¶

    Context: The creation of man. "The LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became (wayehi) a living soul (nephesh chayyah)."

    Direct statement: The verb wayehi (Qal wayyiqtol of hayah, H1961) is a transformation verb: man BECAME a living nephesh. Man did not receive a nephesh as a separate component -- the composite entity of dust + divine breath IS the living nephesh.

    Original language: Parser confirms: wayehi = Verb.Qal.Wayq.3ms of hayah ("be/become"). nephesh = Noun.fs.Abs (H5315). chayyah = Adj.fs.Abs (H2416, "alive"). The same phrase nephesh chayyah is applied to animals in Gen 1:20, 24 -- the term does not distinguish humans from animals.

    Cross-references: Gen 1:20, 24 (animals as nephesh chayyah), Gen 3:22-24 (tree of life barred -- immortality not inherent).

    Relationship to other evidence: If man IS a nephesh rather than having a nephesh, and if the same designation applies to animals, then psyche in Matt 10:28 denotes the whole living person, not an immortal soul-component. God destroys the whole person (both psyche and soma) in gehenna.

    Ezekiel 18:4¶

    Context: "The soul (nephesh) that sinneth, it shall die (muwth)."

    Direct statement: Nephesh + muwth = the soul can die. The sinning nephesh dies -- not "is separated" or "is tormented forever."

    Original language: Parser confirms: nephesh = Noun.fs.Abs (H5315). tamuwth = Verb.Qal.Impf.3fs of muwth (H4191, "die"). The nephesh co-occurs with muwth 47 times in the OT. The sinning nephesh dies.

    Cross-references: Ezek 18:20 (repeated formula), Matt 10:28 (God destroys the psyche -- NT parallel to nephesh dying).

    Relationship to other evidence: The OT establishes that the nephesh/soul can die. The LXX translates nephesh as psyche 599 times (87%). Matt 10:28 continues this OT teaching: God can destroy the psyche. The nephesh dies (OT); the psyche is destroyed (NT). Both use verbs of cessation, not torment.

    Ezekiel 18:20¶

    Context: "The soul (nephesh) that sinneth, it shall die."

    Direct statement: Repeated formula from v.4. The emphatic repetition within a single chapter establishes nephesh mortality as a settled principle.

    Original language: Identical to 18:4: nephesh + muwth. The repetition rules out misunderstanding: the sinning nephesh does not merely suffer -- it dies.

    Cross-references: Ezek 18:4 (first statement), Rom 6:23 (wages of sin is death).

    Relationship to other evidence: The double statement in Ezekiel brackets the principle of individual accountability: each nephesh dies for its own sin. This is the OT background for the NT's "wages of sin is death" (Rom 6:23) and "God can destroy the psyche" (Matt 10:28).

    Counter-Evidence Texts¶

    Matthew 25:46¶

    Context: Sheep and goats judgment. "These shall go away into everlasting punishment (kolasis aionios): but the righteous into life eternal (zoe aionios)."

    Direct statement: The word for "punishment" is kolasis (G2851), NOT basanismos (G929, torment). Kolasis = penal infliction (from kolazo, "to curtail/prune"). The punishment is aionios -- the same adjective applied to "life."

    Original language: Parser confirms: kolasin = N-ASF of kolasis (G2851). aionion = A-ASF of aionios (G166). Kolasis has only 2 NT occurrences (Matt 25:46 and 1 John 4:18). Matthew could have used basanismos if he meant ongoing torment; he chose kolasis (punishment).

    Cross-references: 1 John 4:18 (second kolasis occurrence), Matt 10:28 (apollymi, not basanizo, for God's judgment).

    Relationship to other evidence: The parallel structure ("everlasting punishment" // "life eternal") uses the same adjective aionios. Both sides of the debate note this. The question is whether aionios describes the duration of the punishing process or the permanence of the punishment's result. The Hebrews pattern (eternal redemption, Heb 9:12; eternal judgment, Heb 6:2; eternal salvation, Heb 5:9) shows aionios can describe the permanent results of completed acts.

    Malachi 4:1-3¶

    Context: Last OT prophet's eschatological vision. The wicked become stubble, are burned up leaving "neither root nor branch," and become "ashes under the soles of your feet."

    Direct statement: The wicked are completely consumed by fire. The result is ashes. They are trodden underfoot. The fire produces total destruction, not ongoing burning.

    Original language: The wicked become epher (ashes). They are trodden underfoot. "Neither root nor branch" = nothing remains. This is the OT eschatological fire paradigm: complete consumption, not eternal preservation in flame.

    Cross-references: Matt 3:12 (burn up chaff -- same paradigm), Matt 13:40 (tares burned -- same paradigm), 2 Pet 2:6 (Sodom turned to ashes).

    Relationship to other evidence: Malachi's eschatological fire produces the same result as every other OT fire judgment: ashes, cessation, nothing remaining. The NT judgment parables in Matthew draw on this same paradigm.

    Supporting Evidence¶

    Revelation 20:9-10¶

    Context: Post-millennium judgment. In consecutive verses, John uses different verbs for different subjects: katephagen ("devoured," G2719) for human armies (v.9); basanisthesontai ("shall be tormented," G928) for the devil, beast, and false prophet (v.10).

    Direct statement: Humans are devoured (destruction verb); non-human symbolic entities are tormented. The vocabulary differentiation by subject is consistent across consecutive verses.

    Original language: Parser confirms: Rev 20:9 katephagen = V-2AAI-3S of katesthio (G2719), aorist active indicative -- "devoured." Rev 20:10 basanisthesontai = V-FPI-3P of basanizo (G928), future passive indicative -- "shall be tormented." Different verbs for different subjects.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (apollymi for humans in gehenna), Rev 20:14 (lake of fire = second death, thanatos).

    Relationship to other evidence: The vocabulary differentiation in Rev 20:9-10 reinforces the pattern seen throughout the NT: destruction/death vocabulary for human subjects, torment vocabulary limited to non-human/symbolic entities. Matt 10:28 uses apollymi (destroy) for humans, consistent with this pattern.

    James 4:12¶

    Context: "There is one lawgiver, who is able to save (sozo) and to destroy (apollymi)."

    Direct statement: The same verb pair as Matt 10:28's implicit structure, applied to God. God's two abilities: save (sozo) and destroy (apollymi). These are antonyms describing opposite outcomes.

    Original language: Parser confirms: sosai = V-AAN of sozo (G4982, aorist active infinitive). apolesai = V-AAN of apollymi (G622, aorist active infinitive). Both are complementary infinitives completing dynamenos ("being able"). Identical grammatical structure to Matt 10:28b: dynamenos + aorist active infinitive of apollymi.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (same structure), Matt 8:25 (sozo/apollymi antonym pair).

    Relationship to other evidence: James 4:12 provides an independent witness to the same verb pair used with identical grammar. God's two capacities are save and destroy -- not save and torment.

    1 Timothy 6:16, 1 Corinthians 15:53-54, Romans 2:7, Genesis 3:22-24¶

    Context (collective): The anthropological prerequisite for interpreting Matt 10:28.

    Direct statement: (1) God alone (monos) has immortality/athanasia (1 Tim 6:16). (2) Mortals "put on" immortality at the resurrection -- it is not presently possessed (1 Cor 15:53-54). (3) Believers "seek for" immortality -- you do not seek what you already have (Rom 2:7). (4) God barred the tree of life after the fall -- if humans were already immortal, barring the tree would serve no purpose (Gen 3:22-24).

    Original language: Athanasia (G110, alpha-privative: a- + thanatos = "deathlessness") appears only 3 times in the NT. Monos echon athanasian ("alone having immortality," 1 Tim 6:16) restricts immortality to God. Endysasthai athanasian ("to put on immortality," 1 Cor 15:53) uses a clothing metaphor -- the mortal does not currently possess it.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (God destroys the psyche -- possible only if the psyche is not inherently immortal).

    Relationship to other evidence: If humans do not inherently possess immortality, the wicked cannot be tormented forever. God's ability to "destroy both soul and body in gehenna" (Matt 10:28) is coherent only if the soul is destructible -- which it is, if immortality belongs to God alone and must be acquired by the redeemed at the resurrection. The wicked, who do not receive immortality, simply cease to exist when destroyed.

    Romans 6:23¶

    Context: "The wages of sin is death (thanatos); but the gift of God is eternal life (zoe aionios)."

    Direct statement: The wages of sin is death, not eternal torment. Death and eternal life are mutually exclusive outcomes.

    Original language: Thanatos (G2288, death) vs. zoe aionios (eternal life). If the punishment is death and the reward is life, then the punishment is the absence of life.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (apollymi = destroy, not torment), John 3:16 (perish vs. everlasting life).

    Relationship to other evidence: Paul's most definitive statement about the penalty for sin uses death vocabulary, not torment vocabulary. Consistent with Matt 10:28's use of apollymi (destroy) rather than basanizo (torment).

    John 3:16¶

    Context: "Whosoever believeth in him should not perish (apollymi), but have everlasting life (zoe aionios)."

    Direct statement: Apollymi and zoe aionios are mutually exclusive outcomes. Perishing is the alternative to life -- not an alternative form of life.

    Original language: Apolletai (V-2AMS-3S, aorist middle subjunctive of apollymi) vs. zoen aionion. The apollymi/life antithesis is the NT's most famous statement of the two possible destinies.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (apollymi in gehenna), Rom 6:23 (death vs. life).

    Relationship to other evidence: John's apollymi/life antithesis parallels Matthew's gehenna/life antithesis (Matt 18:9). Both frame the alternative to life as destruction/perishing, not as torment.

    2 Thessalonians 1:9¶

    Context: "Who shall be punished with everlasting destruction (olethros aionios) from the presence of the Lord."

    Direct statement: The fate of the wicked is "everlasting destruction" (olethros aionios). The word is olethros (G3639, destruction/ruin), not basanismos (torment). Modified by aionios -- the destruction is permanent/irrevocable.

    Original language: Olethros aionion -- everlasting destruction. The phrase "everlasting torment" (basanismos aionios) never appears in the NT. The destruction is "from" (apo) the presence of the Lord -- separation from the source of all life.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (apollymi = destroy), Matt 7:13 (apoleia = destruction).

    Relationship to other evidence: Paul uses destruction vocabulary (olethros) for the final fate of the wicked, consistent with Matthew's apollymi. No NT author uses "everlasting torment" for the fate of human beings.

    Matthew 3:12, 13:40-42, 13:49-50¶

    Context (collective): Matthean judgment parables using katakaio (G2618, "burn up/consume").

    Direct statement: The verb katakaio means "burn down/consume" -- the prefix kata intensifies: burn COMPLETELY. Matt 3:12: chaff burned up with unquenchable fire. Matt 13:40: tares gathered and burned (katakaio). Matt 13:42, 50: furnace of fire with wailing and gnashing of teeth.

    Original language: Katakaio (G2618) has 12 NT occurrences, all describing total consumption. The simile in 13:40 requires correspondence: as tares are consumed by fire, so the wicked are consumed. The "wailing and gnashing of teeth" describes the response to the judgment, not an ongoing state.

    Cross-references: Mal 4:1-3 (wicked become ashes), Matt 10:28 (apollymi in gehenna), Matt 22:7 (destroyed + burned up).

    Relationship to other evidence: Matthew's judgment parables consistently use destruction/consumption vocabulary. No torment vocabulary (basanizo, basanismos) appears in any of Matthew's judgment passages or parables.

    Luke 16:23-28 (Rich Man and Lazarus)¶

    Context: The Rich Man and Lazarus parable. Uses basanizo (torment) in hades (G86, NOT gehenna G1067).

    Direct statement: The parable uses torment vocabulary (basanizo) -- but in hades, not gehenna. The parabolic context (narrative with fictional characters illustrating a point), the pre-judgment timing (the rich man's brothers are still alive), and the use of hades (temporary intermediate state) rather than gehenna (final judgment) all distinguish this passage from Matt 10:28.

    Original language: Basanizo (G928) appears here in hades (G86). Gehenna and hades are different words with different referents. The parable is set in hades (the intermediate state), not gehenna (the final judgment). Even if taken as descriptive of an actual state, it describes a pre-judgment condition, not the final fate of the wicked.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (gehenna, not hades; apollymi, not basanizo), Rev 20:13-14 (hades gives up its dead and is cast into the lake of fire -- hades is temporary).

    Relationship to other evidence: The Rich Man and Lazarus is set in hades (temporary), uses basanizo (torment), and is parabolic. Matt 10:28 is set in gehenna (final), uses apollymi (destroy), and is direct teaching. The two passages address different topics in different contexts using different vocabulary.

    Revelation 14:10-11¶

    Context: Angel's warning about beast-worshippers. Uses basanizo (torment). Smoke "ascending forever" echoes Isa 34:10.

    Direct statement: Basanizo appears for beast-worshippers specifically. The subjects are "whosoever receiveth the mark of his name" -- tied to the apocalyptic beast-worship narrative, not a universal statement about all the wicked. The "smoke ascending forever" alludes to Isa 34:10, where Edom's smoke "shall go up for ever" -- yet Edom is not still burning. The OT formula expresses the irrevocable nature of judgment, not necessarily its infinite duration.

    Original language: Basanizo (G928) appears here in an apocalyptic context with symbolic imagery. The "no rest day nor night" may refer to the period of judgment, not to an eternal state.

    Cross-references: Isa 34:10 (Edom's smoke ascending "for ever" -- Edom is not still burning), Matt 10:28 (apollymi, not basanizo, for destruction in gehenna).

    Relationship to other evidence: Rev 14:10-11 uses torment vocabulary in an apocalyptic context. Matt 10:28 uses destruction vocabulary in didactic teaching. The genre difference (apocalyptic vs. didactic) is relevant to classification: didactic passages are clearer than apocalyptic ones per the clarity criteria.

    Mark 9:43-48¶

    Context: Mark's gehenna passage with the "worm dieth not and fire is not quenched" allusion to Isa 66:24.

    Direct statement: The allusion to Isa 66:24 establishes the referent: the worm and fire act on peger (corpses/carcasses), not living persons. The worm is a decomposition agent feeding on dead bodies. "Unquenchable" fire = fire that cannot be put out before completing its work.

    Original language: The Isa 66:24 source text uses peger (H6297, corpses) -- a word never used for a living person. The "unquenchable" fire in the OT (Jer 17:27; Amos 5:6; Ezek 20:47-48) burned to completion -- Jerusalem's fire was unquenchable but is not still burning.

    Cross-references: Isa 66:24 (source text), Matt 10:28 (gehenna context), Matt 18:9 (parallel).

    Relationship to other evidence: The OT source for the "unquenchable fire" and "undying worm" describes agents acting on corpses. The allusion in Mark 9 carries this meaning into the NT. Fire and worms acting on dead bodies = decomposition/consumption of what is already dead, not torment of the living.

    Jude 1:7 and 2 Peter 2:6¶

    Context: Sodom suffered "eternal fire" (Jude 1:7) -- yet was "turned to ashes" (2 Pet 2:6).

    Direct statement: The "eternal fire" produced complete destruction (ashes). The fire is "eternal" in its effect (the destruction is permanent and irreversible), not in its duration. Sodom is not still burning.

    Original language: Tephrosas (2 Pet 2:6) = "turning to ashes." The verb explicitly describes the result: ashes. This is the interpretive key for "eternal fire" -- it produces permanent destruction, not eternal burning.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:28 (destruction in gehenna), Matt 25:41 (everlasting fire), Mal 4:1-3 (wicked become ashes).

    Relationship to other evidence: Sodom's "eternal fire" provides a biblical definition of the phrase: fire that produces permanent, irrevocable destruction. The result is ashes, not ongoing burning. This constrains the meaning of "everlasting fire" in Matt 25:41.

    Luke 12:47-48¶

    Context: Proportional judgment: many stripes vs. few stripes.

    Direct statement: Judgment is proportional to knowledge and responsibility. "Few stripes" is incompatible with identical infinite punishment for all. Proportional judgment implies finite, measured punishment.

    Cross-references: Matt 10:15 (more tolerable for Sodom), Matt 11:23-24 (more tolerable for Sodom than Capernaum).

    Relationship to other evidence: Proportionality of judgment is incompatible with uniform eternal conscious torment. If judgment is measured ("few stripes" vs. "many stripes"), then punishment is finite.

    Patterns Identified¶

    1. Same-Author Verb Pattern¶

    When Matthew records God or authority figures using apollymi on the wicked, the meaning is actual destruction in all three instances: 10:28 (God destroys soul and body), 21:41 (vineyard lord destroys wicked tenants), 22:7 (king destroys murderers and burns city). All are active voice. None uses torment vocabulary.

    2. Same-Author Psyche Pattern¶

    Matthew uses psyche 16 times. In 8 instances, it denotes physical life (2:20, 6:25 2x, 10:39 2x, 16:25 2x, 20:28). In 8, it denotes the whole person/inner self (10:28 2x, 11:29, 12:18, 16:26 2x, 22:37, 26:38). The "life" uses include the most immediate contextual control: 10:39 uses the identical word pair psyche + apollymi = "lose one's life" just 11 verses after the crux text.

    3. Gehenna Vocabulary Pattern¶

    Gehenna collocates in the NT with: fire (6x), apollymi/destroy (3x), ballo/cast (5x), zoe/life as antithesis (3x), krisis/judgment (1x). Gehenna never collocates with basanizo (torment) for human subjects. This is a verifiable textual fact confirmed by cross-referencing all 12 gehenna occurrences with all 12 basanizo occurrences.

    4. Sozo-Apollymi Antithesis¶

    14 NT verses set sozo (save) and apollymi (destroy/perish) in direct opposition. If sozo = preserve/give life, then apollymi = remove/end life. James 4:12 applies both verbs to God with identical grammar to Matt 10:28: God is "able to save (sozo, V-AAN) and to destroy (apollymi, V-AAN)."

    5. Destruction/Death vs. Torment Vocabulary¶

    When the NT describes the fate of human wicked, the vocabulary is consistently destruction (apollymi, apoleia, olethros, katakaio, katesthio) and death (thanatos, "second death"). Torment vocabulary (basanizo, basanismos) is reserved for non-human/symbolic entities or parabolic/apocalyptic contexts. No didactic passage uses basanizo for the final fate of human beings.

    Word Study Integration¶

    The Apokteino-Apollymi Verb Shift¶

    The verb shift from apokteino (G615, "kill") to apollymi (G622, "destroy") in Matt 10:28 is the crux grammatical datum. Both are aorist active infinitives (V-AAN), both complement participial forms of dynamai. The shift signals that God's action is qualitatively different from human killing. Apokteino = straightforward physical killing (limited to the body). Apollymi = utter destruction (encompassing both soul and body). No lexicon places apollymi in a torment domain. Louw-Nida classifies it in Violence/Destroy (20.003) and Exist/Non-exist (13.003).

    The Abad-Apollymi LXX Bridge¶

    Apollymi is the canonical NT heir to Hebrew abad (H6). The LXX maps abad to apollymi 141 times (PMI 6.14) -- one of the strongest Hebrew-Greek verb pairings. When the OT says the wicked "perish" (abad, Ps 37:20; Ps 146:4), the LXX uses apollymi. Jesus' choice of apollymi in Matt 10:28 carries the full semantic weight of the OT destruction vocabulary.

    The Nephesh-Psyche LXX Bridge¶

    The LXX maps nephesh (H5315) to psyche in approximately six hundred instances (87%). This means NT psyche inherits the OT nephesh concept. Nephesh can die (Ezek 18:4, 20), can refer to corpses (Lev 21:11; Num 6:6), is shared with animals (Gen 1:20, 24), and is the result of dust + breath (Gen 2:7). Matt 10:28's psyche is destructible because the OT nephesh it translates is mortal.

    The Kai...Kai Totality Construction¶

    The kai...kai correlative in Matt 10:28 binds psyche and soma into a single object of apolesai, asserting that God can destroy the COMPLETE person. If only the body were destroyed while the soul survived for torment, the "both...and" construction would be misleading -- God would not actually be destroying "both soul and body" but only the body.

    Cross-Testament Connections¶

    Ezekiel 18:4 → Matthew 10:28¶

    The OT declares: "the soul (nephesh) that sinneth, it shall die (muwth)." The NT declares: God "is able to destroy (apollymi) both soul (psyche) and body in gehenna." Both use verbs of cessation (die, destroy) applied to the soul (nephesh/psyche). The connection is reinforced by the LXX bridge: nephesh → psyche (599x), abad/muwth → apollymi (141x for abad).

    Isaiah 66:24 → Mark 9:43-48 → Matthew 10:28¶

    Isaiah's carcasses (peger) subject to worm and fire → Mark's gehenna with "worm dieth not and fire is not quenched" → Matthew's gehenna where God destroys soul and body. The chain establishes that gehenna imagery derives from a passage about consuming corpses, not tormenting the living.

    Malachi 4:1-3 → Matthew 3:12, 13:40¶

    Malachi's eschatological fire that leaves "neither root nor branch" and reduces the wicked to ashes → Matthew's judgment parables using katakaio (burn up/consume). The OT paradigm of total consumption carries into Matthew's judgment vocabulary.

    Genesis 2:7 + 3:22-24 → 1 Tim 6:16 + 1 Cor 15:53-54 → Matt 10:28¶

    Man became a nephesh (not immortal by nature) + tree of life barred (immortality requires access) → God alone has immortality + mortals put it on at resurrection → therefore the soul is destructible (Matt 10:28). The anthropological chain from creation to eschatology establishes that the soul's destructibility in Matt 10:28 is consistent with the Bible's entire framework.

    Difficult or Complicating Passages¶

    Matthew 25:46 — "Everlasting Punishment"¶

    The parallel structure ("everlasting punishment" // "life eternal") uses the same adjective aionios for both outcomes. This parallel is the primary text cited against a conditionalist reading of Matt 10:28. The complication: if "everlasting life" means life that never ends, does "everlasting punishment" mean punishment that never ends?

    Two observations moderate this complication: (1) The word for "punishment" is kolasis (G2851, penal infliction from kolazo "to curtail"), not basanismos (ongoing torment). The word choice emphasizes the penalty, not the experience of being tortured. (2) The Hebrews aionios pattern (eternal redemption, Heb 9:12; eternal judgment, Heb 6:2; eternal salvation, Heb 5:9) demonstrates that aionios can describe the permanent results of completed acts, not necessarily the infinite duration of a process.

    Luke 16:23-28 — Rich Man in Torment¶

    The Rich Man and Lazarus parable uses basanizo (torment) vocabulary. Three factors limit its applicability to the question of Matt 10:28: (1) It is parabolic (genre), (2) it is set in hades (not gehenna), and (3) it describes a pre-judgment state (the rich man's brothers are still alive). Even if the parable describes a real intermediate state, it does not address the final fate in gehenna.

    Revelation 14:10-11 — Smoke Ascending Forever¶

    The strongest ECT-direction text using torment vocabulary for human subjects. However, the subjects are specifically beast-worshippers in an apocalyptic context. The "smoke ascending forever" formula echoes Isa 34:10 (Edom's smoke ascending "for ever"), where the result was complete destruction, not ongoing burning. The apocalyptic genre and specific prophetic context require careful handling.

    The "Fear" Argument¶

    One might argue that the fear hierarchy in Matt 10:28 requires the threat to be worse than physical death. If God only destroys the wicked (ending their existence), is that more fearsome than what human persecutors can do? The text does not require that the duration of the consequence be the distinguishing factor. The scope is the distinguishing factor: humans can only kill the body; God can destroy the complete person (both soul and body). The finality and totality of divine destruction -- not its duration -- is what makes it more fearsome.

    Preliminary Synthesis¶

    The same-author analysis of psyche, apollymi, gehenna, and phobeo in Matthew's Gospel produces a consistent pattern:

    1. Psyche in Matthew denotes physical life or the whole person. The nearest contextual control (10:39, same discourse) uses psyche + apollymi to mean "lose one's life." No Matthean use of psyche requires or implies an immortal, indestructible soul.

    2. Apollymi in Matthew's judgment contexts (10:28, 21:41, 22:7) consistently means actual destruction by an authority figure. The active voice, the pairing with fire vocabulary, and the OT heritage (abad → apollymi, 141 LXX mappings) all point to cessation, not torment.

    3. Gehenna in Matthew collocates with fire and apollymi/destruction, never with basanizo/torment. The OT background (Valley of Slaughter, corpse disposal) and the life-vs-gehenna antithesis (18:9) frame gehenna as the place where life ends, not where conscious torment continues.

    4. Phobeo in Matt 10:28 establishes a fear hierarchy: the present imperative commands an ongoing posture of reverential fear toward God, whose destructive power encompasses the complete person. The phobeo pattern across Matthew (8 "fear not" commands) makes 10:28b the singular exception where fear is positively commanded.

    The counter-evidence texts (Matt 25:46, Luke 16:23-28, Rev 14:10-11, Mark 9:43-48) use different vocabulary (kolasis, basanizo), operate in different genres (parabolic, apocalyptic), or address different subjects (hades vs. gehenna, non-human entities vs. humans). None of them overrides the direct, didactic statement in Matt 10:28 that God destroys both soul and body in gehenna.
