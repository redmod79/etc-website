    CONCLUSION — Matthew 10:28: Same-Author Usage and the Fate of the Wicked¶

    Question¶

    Matthew 10:28 -- "And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell." What does this verse teach about the fate of the wicked? Same-author usage analysis of psyche (G5590), apollymi (G622), gehenna (G1067), and phobeo (G5399) in Matthew's Gospel.

    Summary Answer¶

    Matthew 10:28 teaches that God possesses the ability to destroy the complete person -- both soul and body -- in gehenna. The verb Jesus uses for God's action is apollymi (G622, "destroy"), not basanizo (G928, "torment"). Same-author analysis of Matthew's Gospel shows that apollymi in judgment contexts describes actual destruction, that psyche denotes physical life or the whole person (not an immortal soul-component), that gehenna consistently collocates with destruction vocabulary and never with torment vocabulary, and that the fear hierarchy commands ongoing reverential fear toward a God whose power encompasses total destruction.

    Key Verses¶

    God Destroys Soul and Body in Gehenna (Matthew 10:28) And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell.

    Same-Discourse Word Pair (Matthew 10:39) He that findeth his life shall lose it: and he that loseth his life for my sake shall find it.

    The Soul That Sins Dies (Ezekiel 18:4) Behold, all souls are mine; as the soul of the father, so also the soul of the son is mine: the soul that sinneth, it shall die.

    Wages of Sin Is Death (Romans 6:23) For the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.

    God Alone Has Immortality (1 Timothy 6:16) Who only hath immortality, dwelling in the light which no man can approach unto; whom no man hath seen, nor can see: to whom be honour and power everlasting. Amen.

    Perish or Everlasting Life (John 3:16) For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life.

    Wicked Become Ashes (Malachi 4:1,3) For, behold, the day cometh, that shall burn as an oven; and all the proud, yea, and all that do wickedly, shall be stubble: and the day that cometh shall burn them up, saith the LORD of hosts, that it shall leave them neither root nor branch. ... And ye shall tread down the wicked; for they shall be ashes under the soles of your feet in the day that I shall do this, saith the LORD of hosts.

    Everlasting Destruction (2 Thessalonians 1:9) Who shall be punished with everlasting destruction from the presence of the Lord, and from the glory of his power.

    Analysis¶

    The Verb Shift: Apokteino to Apollymi¶

    The grammatical center of Matt 10:28 is the verb shift from apokteino (G615, "kill") to apollymi (G622, "destroy"). Both verbs appear as aorist active infinitives (V-AAN) complementing participial forms of dynamai ("to be able"). The first half of the verse describes what human persecutors can do: they are able to kill (apokteino) the body, but they are not able to kill the soul. The second half describes what God can do: He is able to destroy (apollymi) both soul and body in gehenna.

    The shift from apokteino to apollymi is not stylistic variation. If Jesus intended to say that God merely "kills" the soul in the same way humans kill the body, he could have repeated apokteino. Instead he escalated to apollymi -- a stronger verb meaning "to destroy fully," from the compound apo ("away from, completely") + ollymi ("destroy"). The lexical data confirms this: Louw-Nida classifies apollymi in the Violence/Destroy domain (20.003) and the Exist/Non-exist domain (13.003). No lexicon places apollymi in a torment domain. The LXX maps Hebrew abad (H6, "perish/destroy") to apollymi 141 times (PMI 6.14), making apollymi the canonical NT heir to the OT's primary destruction verb. When the OT says the wicked "perish" (abad, Ps 37:20; Ps 146:4), the Greek translation uses apollymi -- the same verb Jesus uses in Matt 10:28 for what God does to soul and body in gehenna.

    The aorist aspect of both infinitives (apokteinai and apolesai) presents these actions as complete events -- bounded acts of killing and destroying, not ongoing processes. The grammar describes what agents are able to accomplish as completed actions, not states they maintain indefinitely.

    Same-Author Usage: Psyche in Matthew's Gospel¶

    Psyche (G5590, 105 NT occurrences) appears sixteen times across Matthew's Gospel. The distribution falls into two semantic categories. Eight occurrences denote physical life: Matt 2:20 (those who sought the child's "life" are dead), 6:25 (take no thought for your "life" regarding food and drink, twice), 10:39 (lose one's "life" for Christ's sake, twice), 16:25 (save/lose one's "life," twice), and 20:28 (give his "life" a ransom). Eight denote the whole person or inner self: Matt 10:28 (soul and body destroyed in gehenna, twice), 11:29 (rest for your "souls"), 12:18 (God's "soul" well pleased), 16:26 (what profit to lose one's own "soul," twice), 22:37 (love God with all your "soul"), and 26:38 ("my soul" is sorrowful unto death).

    The most significant datum for interpreting 10:28 is Matt 10:39. In the same commissioning discourse, just 11 verses later, Matthew uses the identical word pair psyche + apollymi to mean "lose one's life" -- not "destroy an immaterial soul." The parser confirms morphological identity: psyche appears as N-ASF (accusative singular feminine) in both verses. The word pair that in 10:28 describes divine judgment (God destroys both soul and body) in 10:39 describes the discipleship paradox (whoever loses his life finds it). The same-discourse proximity makes 10:39 the closest contextual control for the meaning of psyche in 10:28.

    Matt 26:38 adds another datum: Jesus says "my psyche is exceeding sorrowful, even unto death (heos thanatou)." Jesus describes his own psyche as approaching death. If psyche were inherently immortal, the phrase would be incoherent -- an immortal entity cannot approach death. Matthew records Jesus treating his own psyche as mortal.

    The OT background reinforces this reading. The LXX maps nephesh (H5315, 788 OT occurrences) to psyche in approximately six hundred instances (87% mapping rate). Man became (wayehi, transformation verb) a living nephesh (Gen 2:7) -- man IS a nephesh, not a body that has a nephesh. The same phrase "nephesh chayyah" (living soul/creature) applies to animals (Gen 1:20, 24). Nephesh can die: "the soul (nephesh) that sinneth, it shall die (muwth)" appears in both Ezek 18:4 and 18:20. Nephesh can refer to corpses (nephesh meth, Lev 21:11; Num 6:6). The LXX nephesh-psyche bridge means NT psyche inherits the full semantic range of OT nephesh -- including mortality.

    Same-Author Usage: Apollymi in Matthew's Gospel¶

    Apollymi (G622, 102 NT occurrences) appears eighteen times in Matthew. The occurrences fall into five semantic categories:

    (a) Judgment/authority destruction (active voice, actual destruction): Matt 10:28 -- God destroys soul and body in gehenna. Matt 21:41 -- the vineyard lord "will miserably destroy those wicked men" (apolesei, V-FAI-3S, future active indicative). Matt 22:7 -- the king "destroyed those murderers and burned up their city" (apolesen, V-AAI-3S, aorist active indicative, paired with fire vocabulary).

    (b) Lost/straying (middle voice, recoverable condition): Matt 10:6, 15:24 -- "lost sheep of the house of Israel" (perfect middle participle). Matt 18:11 -- "to save that which was lost."

    (c) Physical peril (middle voice, facing death): Matt 8:25 -- "Lord, save us: we perish." Sozo and apollymi as antonyms.

    (d) Human destructive intent (active voice, plotting to kill): Matt 2:13 -- Herod seeks to destroy the child. Matt 12:14 -- Pharisees conspire to destroy Jesus. Matt 27:20 -- crowd persuaded to destroy Jesus.

    (e) Ruin of inanimate objects: Matt 9:17 -- wineskins perish.

    (f) Loss of life paradox: Matt 10:39, 16:25 -- "he that loseth (apollymi) his life (psyche) shall find it."

    The pattern reveals a voice-dependent semantic distinction. Active voice apollymi with a divine or authority-figure subject consistently describes actual destruction: the child is killed (2:13), wicked tenants are destroyed (21:41), murderers are destroyed and their city burned (22:7), and God destroys soul and body in gehenna (10:28). Middle voice apollymi describes being lost (10:6, 15:24, 18:11) or perishing/facing death (8:25, 18:14). The "lost sheep" usage (middle voice, recoverable loss) does not govern the meaning of the judgment usage (active voice, actual destruction by authority).

    Matt 18:14 provides a distinctive angle: "It is not the will of your Father which is in heaven, that one of these little ones should perish (apollymi)." God does not will that any should apollymi. The Father's will opposes apollymi -- perishing. If apollymi in judgment contexts described eternal conscious torment rather than cessation, the Father's will against apollymi would mean God opposes eternal torment for the wicked rather than opposes their destruction.

    Same-Author Usage: Gehenna in Matthew's Gospel¶

    Matthew contains seven of the twelve total NT gehenna (G1067) occurrences. Every Matthean gehenna passage follows a consistent vocabulary pattern:

    • Matt 5:22: gehenna + fire (pur)
    • Matt 5:29-30: gehenna + apollymi (member perishing) + ballo (body cast into)
    • Matt 10:28: gehenna + apollymi (destroy soul and body)
    • Matt 18:9: gehenna + fire (pur) + zoe (life as antithesis)
    • Matt 23:15: gehenna as destiny-idiom ("son of gehenna")
    • Matt 23:33: gehenna + krisis (judgment/condemnation)

    The collocates are: fire (5:22, 18:9), apollymi/destroy (5:29-30, 10:28), ballo/cast (5:29-30), zoe/life as antithesis (18:9), krisis/judgment (23:33), destiny-idiom (23:15). At no point does Matthew -- or any other NT author -- pair gehenna with basanizo (torment) for human subjects. This zero-overlap between gehenna vocabulary and basanizo vocabulary is a verifiable textual fact confirmed by cross-referencing all 12 gehenna occurrences with all 12 basanizo occurrences.

    The OT background of gehenna (ge-Hinnom, Valley of the son of Hinnom) reinforces the destruction reading. The Valley of Hinnom progressed from a geographic boundary marker (Josh 15:8) through its association with child sacrifice (2 Chr 28:3; 33:6; 2 Ki 23:10), to prophetic renaming as the "Valley of Slaughter" (Jer 7:31-32; 19:6), to an eschatological judgment metaphor (Isa 30:33). At every stage, the associations are fire, death, slaughter, and corpse disposal -- not ongoing torment. When Jesus uses "gehenna" in Matt 10:28, his audience would understand it against this OT background.

    The life-vs-gehenna contrast in Matt 18:9 (and parallels in Mark 9:43, 45, 47) is instructive: "enter into life" vs. "be cast into gehenna fire." The alternatives are life and gehenna. If gehenna were a place of ongoing conscious existence (even tormented existence), the contrast would be life vs. alternative-life, not life vs. destruction. The antithesis between zoe (life) and gehenna frames gehenna as the place where life does not continue.

    Same-Author Usage: Phobeo in Matthew's Gospel¶

    Phobeo (G5399, 110 NT occurrences) appears sixteen times in Matthew. The pattern is distinctive: 8 of these are "fear not" commands (me + phobeo in various forms): Matt 1:20, 10:26, 10:28, 10:31, 14:27, 17:7, 28:5, 28:10. Jesus' characteristic response to human fear throughout the Gospel is "fear not."

    The singular exception is Matt 10:28b, where phobeo is positively commanded: "phobeisthe de mallon ton dynamenon..." -- "rather fear the one who is able..." This is the only place in Matthew where Jesus tells his audience TO fear. The positive command to fear marks God's destructive power as the one thing worthy of ongoing reverential fear.

    The tense distinction is significant. Matt 10:28 uses the present imperative phobeisthe (V-PNM-2P), which in prohibition (with me) means "stop fearing" and in positive command means "continue to fear / maintain a posture of fear." Luke's parallel (12:5) uses the aorist imperative phobethete (V-AOM-2P), which commands a decisive act of fearing. Matthew's present imperative indicates an ongoing disposition of reverential fear toward God, while Luke's aorist imperative commands a specific, decisive act.

    The phobeo pattern bookends Matthew's Gospel: the first occurrence is the angel's "fear not" to Joseph (1:20); the last is Jesus' "fear not" to the women at the empty tomb (28:10). At the center of the narrative, the pivotal 10:28 redirects fear from human persecutors to God. The literary structure emphasizes that the proper response to God's power is not terror (which Jesus repeatedly tells people to stop) but reverential fear grounded in the recognition that God can destroy the complete person.

    The Kai...Kai Construction and Totality¶

    The correlative kai...kai in Matt 10:28 binds psyche and soma into a single object of apolesai: "to destroy both (kai) soul and (kai) body in gehenna." This construction emphasizes totality -- God destroys the complete person, not merely one component. The correlative kai...kai functions differently from the simple copulative kai ("and"): it pairs two elements into a conceptual unit, asserting that both are equally included in the predicate.

    The theological significance is direct. If God destroys BOTH soul and body, then neither component survives the destruction. If the soul survived destruction in gehenna (continuing in conscious torment while the body was destroyed), Jesus' assertion that God destroys "both soul and body" would be misleading -- He would actually destroy only the body while preserving the soul. The kai...kai construction asserts the totality of destruction: the complete person ceases.

    The Sozo-Apollymi Antithesis¶

    Fourteen NT verses set sozo (G4982, "save") and apollymi (G622, "destroy/perish") in direct opposition. The antonym relationship constrains apollymi's meaning in judgment contexts: what sozo rescues from is what apollymi inflicts. If sozo = "preserve life / give eternal life," then apollymi = "end life / remove from life."

    James 4:12 provides the clearest parallel to Matt 10:28's implicit structure: "There is one lawgiver, who is able to save (sosai, V-AAN, aorist active infinitive of sozo) and to destroy (apolesai, V-AAN, aorist active infinitive of apollymi)." The grammatical structure is identical: dynamenos + aorist active infinitive of apollymi (and sozo). God's two abilities are save and destroy -- not save and torment. The antonym pair defines apollymi by its contrast with sozo: destruction as the opposite of salvation.

    Matt 8:25 applies the same antithesis in a physical context: "Lord, save (sozo) us: we perish (apollymi)." The disciples face physical death by drowning. Sozo rescues from perishing. The antonym relationship is consistent across physical and eschatological contexts.

    The Anthropological Foundation¶

    The destructibility of the psyche in Matt 10:28 is grounded in the broader biblical anthropology. Four data points establish that humans do not inherently possess immortality:

    1. God alone has athanasia (1 Tim 6:16 -- monos echon athanasian). The alpha-privative athanasia (a- + thanatos) means "deathlessness." Only God has it.

    2. Mortals "put on" (enduo) immortality at the resurrection (1 Cor 15:53-54). The clothing metaphor indicates the mortal does not currently possess immortality -- it must be acquired.

    3. Believers "seek for" (zeteo, present active participle) immortality (Rom 2:7). One does not seek what one already has.

    4. God barred the tree of life after the fall (Gen 3:22-24). If humans already had immortal souls, barring the tree would serve no purpose -- they would live forever regardless.

    If the wicked never receive immortality (they do not "put it on" at the resurrection, they do not "seek" it), they cannot be tormented forever. Without immortality, the wicked simply cease to exist when destroyed. God's ability to "destroy both soul and body in gehenna" (Matt 10:28) is coherent only if the soul is destructible.

    The Synoptic Parallel: Luke 12:4-5¶

    Luke's version of the same logion differs from Matthew's in three ways:

    1. Tense of phobeo: Luke uses the aorist imperative phobethete (decisive act); Matthew uses the present imperative phobeisthe (ongoing posture).

    2. Verb for God's action: Luke uses apokteino (kill) for BOTH human and divine action; Matthew escalates from apokteino (human action) to apollymi (divine action).

    3. Psyche: Luke omits psyche entirely, adding a temporal sequence: "after he hath killed, hath power to cast into gehenna."

    Matthew's distinctive contribution is the verb shift from apokteino to apollymi. Luke maintains apokteino throughout, emphasizing the sequence (kill, then cast). Matthew changes the verb to emphasize the nature of God's action (destroy, not merely kill). Both versions agree on the location (gehenna), the reason for fear (God's ultimate authority), and the absence of torment vocabulary. Neither version uses basanizo.

    Counter-Evidence: Passages Used for ECT¶

    Matt 25:46 ("everlasting punishment"): The word is kolasis (G2851, punishment from kolazo "to curtail/prune"), not basanismos (ongoing torment). The parallel structure with "everlasting life" uses the same adjective aionios, but the Hebrews aionios pattern (eternal redemption Heb 9:12, eternal judgment Heb 6:2, eternal salvation Heb 5:9) shows aionios can describe the permanent results of completed acts. "Everlasting punishment" is consistent with a punishment whose result is permanent (irreversible destruction), not necessarily with a punishing process that continues forever.

    Luke 16:23-28 (Rich Man and Lazarus): This parable uses basanizo (torment) in hades (G86), not gehenna (G1067). The passage is parabolic (genre), set in hades (not the final judgment location), and describes a pre-judgment state (the rich man's brothers are still alive). Even if the parable describes a real intermediate state, it does not address the final fate of the wicked in gehenna, which is the subject of Matt 10:28.

    Rev 14:10-11 ("tormented with fire and brimstone"): This passage uses basanizo for beast-worshippers in an apocalyptic context. The "smoke ascending forever" formula echoes Isa 34:10, where Edom's smoke "shall go up for ever" -- yet Edom is not still burning. The OT formula expresses irrevocable judgment, not necessarily infinite duration. The apocalyptic genre requires genre-appropriate interpretation, and this passage carries less evidentiary weight than didactic passages per the clarity criteria.

    Mark 9:43-48 ("worm dieth not, fire is not quenched"): The allusion is to Isa 66:24, where the subjects are peger (H6297, corpses/carcasses) -- a word never used for a living person in the OT. The worm is a decomposition agent acting on dead bodies. "Unquenchable" fire in the OT (Jer 17:27; Amos 5:6; Ezek 20:47-48) burned to completion -- Jerusalem's fire was unquenchable but is not still burning.

    Word Studies¶

    Psyche (G5590): 105 NT occurrences. Feminine noun from psycho ("to breathe"). KJV: "soul/souls" (58%), "life/lives" (33%). LXX maps nephesh (H5315) to psyche 599 times (87%). Matthew has 16 occurrences split evenly: 8 as "life," 8 as "soul." The translation split is an English artifact -- the Greek word is the same. Same-discourse usage in Matt 10:39 (psyche + apollymi = "lose one's life") provides the nearest contextual control for 10:28. Applied to animals (nephesh chayyah, Gen 1:20; psyche in Rev 16:3). Can die (Ezek 18:4,20). Can be destroyed (Matt 10:28). Can refer to corpses (nephesh meth, Lev 21:11). Not a technical term for an immortal soul.

    Apollymi (G622): 102 NT occurrences. Verb from apo + ollymi ("destroy fully"). Active = destroy; Middle/Passive = perish, be lost. KJV: "perish" (36%), "destroy" (30%), "lose/lost" (27%). LXX maps abad (H6) to apollymi 141 times (PMI 6.14). Louw-Nida: Violence/Destroy (20.003) + Exist/Non-exist (13.003). No lexicon places apollymi in a torment domain. Matthew's judgment-context apollymi (10:28, 21:41, 22:7) consistently means actual destruction.

    Gehenna (G1067): 12 NT occurrences. Transliteration of ge-Hinnom (Valley of Hinnom). Matthew has 7 (58%). Collocates: fire (6x), apollymi (3x), ballo (5x), zoe as antithesis (3x). Never collocates with basanizo for human subjects. OT background: geographic boundary → child sacrifice site → "Valley of Slaughter" → eschatological judgment metaphor. Associations at every stage: fire, death, destruction.

    Phobeo (G5399): 110 NT occurrences. Deponent verb from phobos (G5401). Matthew has 16 occurrences, 8 in "fear not" commands. Matt 10:28 contains the only positive fear-command in Matthew (phobeisthe de mallon -- "rather fear"). Present imperative = ongoing posture of reverential fear. LXX maps yare (H3372) to phobeo 241 times (PMI 5.75).

    Difficult Passages¶

    Matt 25:46 -- The aionios parallel: The most frequently cited passage against a conditionalist reading of Matt 10:28. The same adjective aionios modifies both "punishment" (kolasis) and "life" (zoe). Both sides agree that the adjective is the same. The question is whether aionios describes the duration of a process (everlasting punishing) or the permanence of a result (everlasting punishment-as-completed-act). The word for "punishment" is kolasis (G2851, penal infliction from kolazo "to curtail"), not basanismos (ongoing torment). The Hebrews aionios pattern shows the adjective can describe permanent results. The aionios parallel does not resolve the question on its own -- it is compatible with both readings.

    Rev 14:10-11 -- Torment with fire and brimstone: Uses basanizo (torment) for beast-worshippers. This is the strongest ECT-direction text using torment vocabulary applied to human subjects. However, it is apocalyptic (not didactic), uses the Isa 34:10 "smoke ascending forever" formula (which described Edom's completed destruction), and specifically addresses beast-worshippers within the Revelation judgment sequence. The apocalyptic genre means this text does not carry the same evidentiary weight as didactic passages.

    The fear hierarchy argument: Does the fear hierarchy require the threat to be "worse than death" in a way that only eternal conscious torment satisfies? The text does not state that duration is the distinguishing factor. The distinguishing factor is scope: humans can only kill the body; God can destroy BOTH soul and body. The totality of divine destruction (the complete person, not just the physical body) is what makes God more fearsome, regardless of whether the destruction is permanent cessation or ongoing torment. The kai...kai construction emphasizes the scope of God's power, not the duration of its effect.

    Conclusion¶

    Matt 10:28 states that God is able to destroy (apollymi) both soul (psyche) and body (soma) in gehenna. Same-author analysis of Matthew's four key words produces a consistent pattern: psyche denotes the whole person or physical life, apollymi in judgment contexts means actual destruction, gehenna pairs with destruction/judgment vocabulary but never with torment vocabulary, and phobeo establishes an ongoing posture of reverential fear toward a God whose power encompasses total destruction. The OT background (nephesh mortality, ge-Hinnom as "Valley of Slaughter," the abad-apollymi LXX bridge) and the broader NT framework (God alone has immortality, wages of sin is death, perish-vs-life antithesis) reinforce the same reading. The counter-evidence texts use different vocabulary (basanizo, kolasis), operate in different genres (parabolic, apocalyptic), or address different subjects (hades vs. gehenna, non-human entities vs. humans).

    What CAN Be Said (Scripture explicitly states or necessarily implies)¶

    • God is able to destroy (apollymi) both soul and body in gehenna (Matt 10:28).
    • The soul (nephesh) that sins shall die (Ezek 18:4, 20).
    • God alone possesses immortality (1 Tim 6:16).
    • The wages of sin is death, not eternal life in torment (Rom 6:23).
    • The wicked become ashes, leaving neither root nor branch (Mal 4:1, 3).
    • Perishing and everlasting life are mutually exclusive outcomes (John 3:16).
    • The punishment of the wicked is described as "everlasting destruction" (2 Thess 1:9).
    • Gehenna vocabulary and basanizo (torment) vocabulary never overlap for human subjects in any NT passage.
    • Matt 25:46 uses kolasis (punishment), not basanismos (torment).
    • The subjects of worm and fire in Isa 66:24 are corpses (peger), not living persons.
    • Humans are "devoured" (katesthio) in Rev 20:9; the devil/beast/false prophet are "tormented" (basanizo) in Rev 20:10 -- different verbs for different subjects.

    What CANNOT Be Said (not explicitly stated or necessarily implied by Scripture)¶

    • That the soul is inherently immortal -- no verse states this; Scripture says God alone has immortality.
    • That gehenna is a place of eternal conscious torment -- no gehenna passage uses torment vocabulary for humans.
    • That basanizo (torment) applies to human beings in gehenna -- basanizo and gehenna never co-occur.
    • That apollymi in Matt 10:28 means "ruin" rather than "destroy" -- no judgment-context apollymi in Matthew uses the "ruin" sense; the "ruin" sense (9:17, wineskins) applies to inanimate objects.
    • That the fear hierarchy requires ongoing torment -- the text distinguishes scope (body vs. soul+body), not duration.
    • That "everlasting punishment" in Matt 25:46 necessarily means "everlasting punishing" -- the word is kolasis (judicial penalty), and aionios can describe permanent results.
    • That Luke 16:23-28 describes the final fate of the wicked in gehenna -- the passage is parabolic, set in hades, and pre-judgment.
    • That Rev 14:10-11 establishes the duration of punishment for all the wicked -- the passage is apocalyptic, addresses beast-worshippers specifically, and uses the Isa 34:10 completed-destruction formula.
