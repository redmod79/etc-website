    Verses¶

    Question¶

    Matthew 10:28 -- "And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell." What does this verse teach about the fate of the wicked? Same-author usage analysis of psyche (G5590), apollymi (G622), gehenna (G1067), and phobeo (G5399) in Matthew's Gospel.

    Full pericope: fear hierarchy, sparrow analogy, 'fear not' repetitions¶

    Matthew 10:26 Fear them not therefore: for there is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed; and hid, that shall not be known. Matthew 10:27 What I tell you in darkness, [that] speak ye in light: and what ye hear in the ear, [that] preach ye upon the housetops. Matthew 10:28 And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell. Matthew 10:29 Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father. Matthew 10:30 But the very hairs of your head are all numbered. Matthew 10:31 Fear ye not therefore, ye are of more value than many sparrows.

    Context: Part of Jesus' commissioning discourse to the twelve apostles (Matt 10:1-42). Jesus warns of persecution (v.16-23), then transitions to three 'fear not' commands (v.26, v.28, v.31), building an a fortiori argument: men can only kill the body; God can destroy both soul and body in gehenna. The sparrow analogy (v.29-31) reassures the disciples of God's providential care.

    Notes: Matt 10:28 is the crux text. The verb shift from apokteino (kill, v.28a) to apollymi (destroy, v.28b) signals a qualitative difference: human killing is limited to the body; divine destroying encompasses both soul and body. The kai...kai correlative construction emphasizes totality. The location is gehenna (G1067), not hades (G86).

    Same discourse, same word-pair psyche+apollymi = 'lose one's life'¶

    Matthew 10:39 He that findeth his life shall lose it: and he that loseth his life for my sake shall find it.

    Context: Just 11 verses after 10:28, in the same commissioning discourse. Jesus uses the identical word-pair psyche + apollymi, but here it clearly means 'lose one's life' — not 'destroy an immaterial soul.' This same-author, same-discourse usage provides the most immediate contextual control for interpreting psyche and apollymi in 10:28.

    • ὁ (G3588) — T-NSM — Nom Sg M
    • εὑρὼν (G2147) — V-2AAP-NSM — Aor Act Ptcp Nom Sg M
    • ψυχὴν (G5590) — N-ASF — Acc Sg F
    • αὐτοῦ (G846) — P-GSM — Gen Sg M
    • ἀπολέσει (G622) — V-FAI-3S — Fut Act Ind Sg thirdPers
    • καὶ (G2532) — CONJ — ὁ ὁ G3588 [T-NSM] Nom Sg M
    • ἕνεκεν (G1752) — PREP — ἐμοῦ ἐγώ G1473 [P-1GS] Gen Sg

    Notes: Parser confirms: apollymi here is V-FAI-3S (future active indicative) and V-AAP-NSM (aorist active participle). Psyche is N-ASF (accusative singular feminine) — identical form to 10:28. The semantic shift between 10:28 (divine judgment destruction) and 10:39 (losing one's life for Christ's sake) shows Matthew's flexibility with the word pair while maintaining the core sense of loss/cessation.

    Matthean apollymi — Herod seeks to destroy the child¶

    Matthew 2:13 And when they were departed, behold, the angel of the Lord appeareth to Joseph in a dream, saying, Arise, and take the young child and his mother, and flee into Egypt, and be thou there until I bring thee word: for Herod will seek the young child to destroy him.

    Context: Herod's intent to kill Jesus. Apollymi used for literal physical destruction — killing the child. First occurrence of apollymi in Matthew.

    Notes: Apollymi here = literal physical destruction (killing). Active voice, articular infinitive (tou apolesai). Matthew's first use of apollymi is in the context of murderous intent.

    Matthean psyche — 'they are dead which sought the young child's life'¶

    Matthew 2:20 Saying, Arise, and take the young child and his mother, and go into the land of Israel: for they are dead which sought the young child's life.

    Context: Angel's message to Joseph after Herod's death. Psyche = physical life that was sought/threatened. First occurrence of psyche in Matthew.

    Notes: Psyche used for physical life. Matthew's first use of psyche — establishing the word's basic meaning in his vocabulary.

    Matthean psyche — 'Take no thought for your life (psyche)'¶

    Matthew 6:25 Therefore I say unto you, Take no thought for your life, what ye shall eat, or what ye shall drink; nor yet for your body, what ye shall put on. Is not the life more than meat, and the body than raiment?

    Context: Sermon on the Mount. Jesus distinguishes psyche (life — what you eat/drink) from soma (body — what you wear). Both are physical concerns.

    Notes: Psyche here = physical life requiring sustenance. The psyche/soma pairing in 6:25 (life vs. body as parallel physical concerns) contrasts with the psyche/soma pairing in 10:28 (soul vs. body as objects of destruction in gehenna).

    Matthean gehenna — 'danger of hell fire' — first gehenna reference in Matthew¶

    Matthew 5:22 But I say unto you, That whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment: and whosoever shall say to his brother, Raca, shall be in danger of the council: but whosoever shall say, Thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire.

    Context: Sermon on the Mount. Jesus introduces gehenna in an escalating series of judgments: judgment → council → gehenna fire. First use of gehenna in Matthew.

    Notes: First of Matthew's 7 gehenna occurrences. Gehenna is paired with fire (pyr) — the dominant collocation pattern.

    Matthean gehenna — 'whole body cast into hell' — body-in-gehenna parallel to 10:28¶

    Matthew 5:29 And if thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out, and cast [it] from thee: for it is profitable for thee that one of thy members should perish, and not [that] thy whole body should be cast into hell. Matthew 5:30 And if thy right hand offend thee, cut it off, and cast [it] from thee: for it is profitable for thee that one of thy members should perish, and not [that] thy whole body should be cast into hell.

    Context: Sermon on the Mount, immediately following 5:22. The body-in-gehenna language directly parallels 10:28. Uses apollymi (V-2AMS-3S, apoletai — aorist middle subjunctive) for the member 'perishing' and ballo (V-APS-3S, blethe — aorist passive subjunctive) for the body being 'cast into' gehenna.

    Notes: Parser confirms apollymi appears in these verses (apoletai). The pattern is: it is better to lose (apollymi) a part than for the whole body to be cast into gehenna. This pairs apollymi with gehenna, as does 10:28.

    Matthean apollymi — 'Lord, save us: we perish' — physical death fear¶

    Matthew 8:25 And his disciples came to [him], and awoke him, saying, Lord, save us: we perish.

    Context: Storm on the sea. Disciples fear drowning. Apollymi = facing physical death. Sozo (save) and apollymi (perish) used as antonyms.

    Notes: Apollymi in the middle voice = perishing (physical death by drowning). The sozo-apollymi antithesis appears here: save us from perishing.

    Matthean apollymi — wineskins perish — apollymi for inanimate ruin/destruction¶

    Matthew 9:17 Neither do men put new wine into old bottles: else the bottles break, and the wine runneth out, and the bottles perish: but they put new wine into new bottles, and both are preserved.

    Context: New wine and old wineskins parable. Apollymi used for the ruin of inanimate objects (wineskins) — demonstrating the word's broad semantic range.

    Notes: Apollymi can describe inanimate objects being ruined/destroyed. This shows the 'ruin' end of the semantic range — the wineskins cease to function as vessels.

    Matthean apollymi — 'lost sheep of the house of Israel' — apollymi as 'lost/straying'¶

    Matthew 10:6 But go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.

    Context: Same commissioning discourse as 10:28. 'Lost' (apollymi, perfect middle participle) sheep — recoverable loss, not destruction. Shows the 'lost/straying' sense.

    Notes: Apollymi here = 'lost/straying' (recoverable condition). This is the middle-voice 'lost' sense, distinct from the active-voice 'destroy' sense used in 10:28. Same discourse but different semantic category.

    Matthean psyche — 'find rest unto your souls' — psyche as inner person¶

    Matthew 11:29 Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls.

    Context: Jesus' invitation to the weary. Psyche = the inner self that experiences rest or turmoil.

    Notes: Psyche as the inner person/self — the seat of weariness and rest. Not a statement about immortality.

    Matthean apollymi — Pharisees conspire to destroy Jesus — human destructive intent¶

    Matthew 12:14 Then the Pharisees went out, and held a council against him, how they might destroy him.

    Context: After Jesus heals on the Sabbath. The Pharisees plot to apollymi (destroy/kill) Jesus. Active voice, subjunctive mood — deliberate planning of lethal action.

    Notes: Apollymi = plot to kill. Human agents using apollymi for murderous intent. Same verb used of God's action in 10:28 — but there God's scope extends beyond mere killing to destroying soul AND body.

    Matthean psyche — 'in whom my soul is well pleased' — God's psyche (OT quotation)¶

    Matthew 12:18 Behold my servant, whom I have chosen; my beloved, in whom my soul is well pleased: I will put my spirit upon him, and he shall shew judgment to the Gentiles.

    Context: Matthew quoting Isa 42:1. God's psyche is 'well pleased' with the Servant. OT quotation preserving the anthropomorphic nephesh-language of Isaiah.

    Notes: Psyche applied to God — reflecting the OT nephesh usage where God speaks of 'my nephesh.' Not a statement about the immortality or immateriality of psyche.

    Matthean apollymi — 'lost sheep of the house of Israel' — second 'lost' usage¶

    Matthew 15:24 But he answered and said, I am not sent but unto the lost sheep of the house of Israel.

    Context: Jesus' response to the Canaanite woman. Same 'lost sheep' formula as 10:6.

    Notes: Second use of apollymi as 'lost' (perfect middle participle). The straying/lost sense, not destruction.

    Matthean psyche+apollymi — save/lose life; what profit to gain world and lose soul¶

    Context: Rich man and Lazarus parable. Uses basanizo (torment) in hades (G86, NOT gehenna G1067). The parabolic context, the pre-judgment timing (the rich man's brothers are still alive), and the use of hades rather than gehenna all raise questions about whether this passage describes the final state of the wicked or is a parabolic illustration using Second Temple imagery.

    Notes: Basanizo (G928) appears here in hades (G86). NOT gehenna. The parable is set before the final judgment (the rich man's brothers are alive). Even if taken as descriptive, it describes an intermediate state in hades, not the final fate in gehenna. Gehenna and basanizo never co-occur in any NT passage.

    Context: Angel's warning about beast-worshippers. Uses basanizo (torment). The subjects are specifically those who worship the beast. The smoke 'ascending forever' echoes Isa 34:10 (Edom's smoke ascends forever — yet Edom is not still burning). The 'no rest day nor night' may refer to the period of torment, not to eternal duration.

    Notes: Basanizo appears here for beast-worshippers specifically. The smoke-ascending-forever language echoes Isa 34:10 where Edom's smoke 'shall not be quenched night nor day; the smoke thereof shall go up for ever' — yet Edom is a physical location that is not still burning. The formula expresses the irrevocable, irreversible nature of the judgment, not necessarily its infinite duration.

    Sodom as example of 'eternal fire' yet 'turned to ashes' (2 Pet 2:6)¶

    Notes: Aionios fire produced ashes. The fire is 'eternal' in its effect (the destruction is permanent and irreversible), not in its duration. Sodom is not still burning.

    Context: Peter's example: Sodom's 'eternal fire' (Jude 1:7) turned the cities to ashes. The result of the eschatological fire is ashes — complete destruction — not ongoing burning.

    Notes: Tephrosas — 'turning to ashes.' The verb explicitly describes the result: ashes. This is the interpretive key for 'eternal fire' — it produces permanent destruction, not eternal burning.

    Proportional judgment — many stripes vs. few stripes — finite, measured punishment¶

    Luke 12:47 And that servant, which knew his lord's will, and prepared not [himself], neither did according to his will, shall be beaten with many [stripes]. Luke 12:48 But he that knew not, and did commit things worthy of stripes, shall be beaten with few [stripes]. For unto whomsoever much is given, of him shall be much required: and to whom men have committed much, of him they will ask the more.

    Context: Jesus' teaching on proportional judgment. The servant who knew and disobeyed receives many stripes; the ignorant servant receives few stripes. 'Few stripes' is incompatible with identical infinite punishment for all — it implies finite, measured, proportional judgment.

    Notes: The proportionality principle: 'many stripes' vs. 'few stripes.' If judgment is proportional to knowledge and responsibility, then identical infinite torment for all is excluded. Proportional judgment is consistent with finite destruction of varying severity, not with uniform eternal conscious torment.
